This chapter (Chapter 29 in the text) covers the final steps of the research phase: preserving digital evidence, analyzing collected material, closing out an investigation properly, and adapting the workflow for time-compressed situations. It also presents six target-type flowcharts, one for each category of starting identifier. The chapter ends with a preview of note-taking tools covered more fully in Chapter 30.

## Capture and Collection

Three approaches to preserving digital evidence are described, distinguished by how much automation is involved.

**Manual capture** is user-initiated and produces files from a single page or set of pages, usually through browser extensions or JavaScript tools. The recommended folder structure nests logically: a case folder in the VM shared directory, then a subfolder matching the tool category (for example, "Email Addresses"), then a subfolder named for the specific identifier being queried (for example, "kirby@rockerzandrollerz.com"). Any images or videos found during research are saved into the appropriate subfolder. For each saved image or video, a full-page screen capture of the source page is also saved in the same folder, to show visually how that item was presented in its original context.

**Passive capture** centers on Hunchly, a Chrome extension that records every page loaded during a browsing session at the source-code level and captures all images on those pages. The steps are: create a new Hunchly case named the same as the investigative notebook, confirm the extension is active and pointed at the correct case name, and proceed with research normally in Chrome. When a key image is found, right-click it, select the Hunchly context-menu entry, choose "Tag Image", enter a caption, and save. Hunchly can later generate a forensically sound report containing all tagged images.

**Scripted capture** uses programs such as Instaloader to automatically collect content from a specified account or source. For scripts that prompt for a save location, the same nested folder structure used in manual capture applies. For scripts that default to a system directory such as Downloads, the files should be moved (not copied) to the case folder after collection, to limit clutter and abandoned case data. A note recording which script was used, what it targeted, and the date and time should be added to the investigative notes, because most scripts produce no log of their own. For evidence intended for court use, a parallel manual capture of the most critical items is advised, because an investigator may be asked to explain a scripted tool to a jury and may not be able to do so confidently. A manual save is easy to explain.

## Analysis

Two types of analysis are described as part of the research phase.

**Multimedia (visual) analysis** involves examining recovered images and video at the highest available resolution and looking for intelligence unintentionally captured in the frame, such as business signage visible behind a profile photo, distinctive surroundings, or other details that narrow down who the subject is or where they are located. This is time-consuming but remains one of the most effective methods for locating a subject who has otherwise covered their online tracks.

**Link analysis** maps how pieces of information connect to one another: people, locations, websites, phone numbers, and other identifiers associated with online accounts. An example in the text shows an email address linked to a YouTube channel and a Reddit username through a common Google account. The recommended tool for drawing link charts is Draw.io. Not every case requires a link chart, but one should be considered when the case involves complex organizations or when a visual representation of connections would add value.

## Submission and Cleanup

Before moving to the next investigation, several closing steps are recommended. Handwritten notes should be transferred to digital notes or the final report, or scanned as a PDF using a printer or directly into OneNote. Paper notes are then filed securely per agency policy or shredded. Digital case directories are archived according to agency evidence retention rules. Some teams retain working files on network-attached storage or optical discs for three to six months, because having historical notes from a previous incident can save significant time if a subject resurfaces. The VM used for the case should be exported if appropriate, then reverted to a clean snapshot or clone. Fresh accounts and replacement tools should be prepared for the next investigation.

## Rapid Threat Assessment (20-Minute Workflow)

The same workflow applies whether an investigation spans months or must be completed in 20 to 30 minutes, but the time allocated to each step shrinks accordingly. The worked example is a threat assessment, such as a user in an online chatroom threatening suicide.

The condensed steps are:

- **Triage.** Verbally confirm the known identifiers and the expected intelligence output (for example: "User DlckTraC on 4chan is threatening to kill himself in a post. We want to know who he is, where he lives, and whether he is likely to carry out the threat."). Identify whether a non-OSINT solution such as contacting a human resource exists first.
- **Roll your tools.** Use a legal pad and pen. If a fresh OSINT VM instance is available, use it. If not, use "utility" social media accounts, defined as accounts kept on hand for non-criminal assessments where speed is essential and some cross-contamination is an acceptable trade-off. Fresh accounts would be preferable but are not always possible.
- **Collaboration.** If working with a team on a shared platform such as OneNote, create a page for each participant to paste key findings, to prevent confusion. Use OneNote in a browser rather than the desktop app in multi-user sessions, because the browser version syncs almost instantly whereas the desktop version has syncing issues with multiple simultaneous users. Assign one person to track overall progress and build the big picture.
- **Premium and government resources.** Run the target through commercial aggregators and government databases first, as these checks are fast and often return low-hanging fruit quickly.
- **OSINT searches.** Start with targeted Google searches such as `site:4chan.org "username"`, then pass known identifiers through custom OSINT tools. Open only highly promising links in new tabs and scan pages quickly, giving priority to images, which the brain can process much faster than text.
- **Deadline briefing.** Be prepared to deliver a situational report at the deadline even if the picture is incomplete, for example: "We have located and preserved the original posting, there is a history of similar suicidal threats from that user, but we do not yet know who or where they are."
- **Follow-up.** After the crisis passes, complete any remaining collection, analysis, and reporting steps within the normal workflow.

## Target Flowcharts

The chapter presents six flowcharts that describe the standard sequence of steps for each category of starting identifier. The author notes that while no single template covers every investigation, these charts represent useful starting priorities. They are not exhaustive and will evolve as tools and services change. The six categories are: Email Address, Username, Real Name, Telephone Number, Domain Name, and Location.

The logic common to all flowcharts is to move from the starting identifier toward new identifier types, then repeat the cycle with each new piece of information discovered.

### Email Address Flowchart

Goal: find usernames and real names from an email address.

The path runs through TruMail (Trumail.io) for address validation, quoted searches on Google, Bing, and Yandex, compromised database services (HIBP, Dehashed, Spycloud, CitODay, LeakedSource, Pastebin, and miscellaneous breaches), the IntelTechniques Email Addresses Tool, paste archives (PSBDMP, IntelX), and additional services including EmailRep, That's Them, Spytox, Newsgroups, DomainData, OCCRP, Analyze ID, Whoxy, and Gravatar. Email assumptions for common personal providers (Gmail, Yahoo, Hotmail, Outlook, Live, ProtonMail, Me.com, Mail.com, Facebook) and work formats are generated and each verified with Trumail.io. The username portion of the address is also searched in isolation after removing the domain.

### Username Flowchart

Goal: find social networks and verify an email address from a username.

The path includes the IntelTechniques Username Search Tool, search engines (Google, Bing, Yandex), username aggregators (Knowem, UserSearch, Namevine, CheckUserName, SocialSearcher, WhatsMyName, Skype, Gravatar, InstagramBio), major social platforms (Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, Reddit, TikTok, YouTube, Snapchat), compromised databases (HIBP, Dehashed, LeakedSource, LeakPeek, PSBDMP), cached versions of pages (Wayback Machine, Google Cache, Yandex Cache, Bing Cache, Screenshot History), the IntelTechniques Domain Tool, and the IntelTechniques Real Name Tool. Email assumptions are generated by appending the username to common provider domains.

### Real Name Flowchart

Goal: find email addresses, usernames, and a telephone number from a real name.

The path runs through the IntelTechniques People Search Tool, a broad set of people-search aggregators (TruePeopleSearch, FastPeople, Nuwber, XLEK, FamilyTreeNow, Intelius, Radaris, CyberBackgrnd, Spytox, SearchPeople, JohnDoe, Spokeo, AdvBackground, Zabasearch, WhitePages), search engines, Facebook (searched by user number and user name via the IntelTechniques Facebook Tool, examining comments, photos, videos, and posts), Twitter (via the IntelTechniques Twitter Tool), resumes, address records, voter records, and the IntelTechniques Data Breaches Tool.

### Telephone Number Flowchart

Goal: verify a name, identify a physical address, and find relatives from a phone number.

The path starts with reverse caller-ID APIs (EveryoneAPI, BulkVS, Twilio, CallerIDService, Telnyx, PeopleDataLabs, Android Emulator), then contacts-lookup services (Calls.ai, TrueCaller), the IntelTechniques Number Search Tool, a broad set of reverse-lookup aggregators (411, 800Notes, AdvBackground, AmericaPhone, Callersmart, FastPeople, InfoTracer, John Doe, Nuwber, PeopleSearch, Phone Owner, ReverseLookup, SearchPeople, Spytox, Sync.me, ThatsThem, TruePeople, WhitePages), Old Phonebook, compromised databases (Dehashed, LeakPeek), and search engines. Expected outputs include real name, relatives, addresses, usernames, social networks, businesses, and websites.

### Domain Name Flowchart

Goal: locate a real name and address from a domain name.

The path includes search engines with site-colon operators, Wayback Machine, Archive.is, the IntelTechniques Domain Search Tool, analytics services (SimilarWeb, Alexa, AnalyzeID), WHOIS history services (Who.is, ViewDNS Whois, Whoisology, DomainData, Whoxy, WhoisArchives, Who.is History), data breach sources (SpyOnWeb, Dehashed, Phonebook.cz, IntelX, LeakPeek), subdomain enumeration tools (SubdomainFinder, DNSDumpster, Pentest Tools), backlink analysis (SharedCount), IP address lookup, metadata extraction (Metagoofil), hidden page discovery via Robots.txt, new domain lookups (NerdyData, DomainIQ), and domain age estimation (CarbonDating). Expected outputs include real name, email address, usernames, social networks, screen captures, and documents.

### Location Flowchart

Goal: identify who is associated with a location and discover new locations from social content.

The path includes the IntelTechniques Location Tool, GPS coordinate input to satellite imagery services (LandViewer, Google Earth App), historic satellite views (LandViewer, Zoom Earth, Yandex Maps, Descartes, MapBox), street-level views (Google Maps, Bing Maps, Mapillary, Open Street Cams), physical address lookups via people-search sites (TruePeople, Nuwber, That's Them, FastPeople, AdvBackground), property databases for interior images (Zillow, Redfin), Snapchat Map for geolocated social posts, and an Android emulator with GPS spoofing for mobile app location checks. Expected outputs include usernames, telephone numbers, real names, GPS coordinates, new locations, and social network profiles.

## Practice Scenarios

The author recommends practicing against real, non-criminal targets using these flowcharts as a guide. Examples include researching previous owners of a Zillow home listing, reverse-searching an unknown incoming phone number, locating social profiles of a wanted criminal's significant other, identifying a restaurant server's vehicle from their online presence, finding direct contact details for an AirBnB host, obtaining a full birth date and relatives' comments from a radio birthday mention, identifying home addresses of business reviewers, and locating social profiles of a person quoted in a newspaper. The author notes that disclosing findings to unwitting subjects is inadvisable.

## Note-Taking Tools (start of Chapter 30)

The text transitions into Chapter 30 on documentation and reporting. Three note-taking applications are described.

**Standard Notes** (standardnotes.org) is presented as the best privacy-focused option. It uses AES-256 encryption, is open source, and has clients for Mac, Windows, iOS, Android, Linux, and web browsers. It can operate entirely offline. Free accounts support sync and end-to-end encryption. The premium tier adds multi-factor authentication and automated backups to a cloud service of the user's choice, along with aesthetic options such as themes.

**OneNote** (onenote.com/download) is recommended for investigators whose organizations use Microsoft Office. The standalone desktop version is preferred over the Office 365 cloud edition. The 64-bit build requires selecting "Other download options" at the bottom of the download page. A fresh Microsoft account should be created using a ProtonMail address rather than an Outlook.com address, to prevent cross-contamination with other projects. OneNote organizes content hierarchically: notebooks contain tabbed sections, and sections contain individual pages, mirroring a physical case binder. PDFs and other files can be embedded as a rendered printout image, as a native file attachment, or both. Template sections can be copied into each new case notebook by right-clicking the section tab and choosing "Move or Copy". Entire notebooks export in a proprietary format. Pages and sections can also be exported as PDFs, Word documents, and other formats.

**CherryTree** (giuspen.com/cherrytree) is the recommended option for Linux. It is open source, runs offline, supports hierarchical nodes (equivalent to notebook sections) and subnodes (equivalent to pages), and has limited support for images, tables, and other file types. Installation on the OSINT Linux VM uses the terminal command `sudo apt-get install cherrytree`.
