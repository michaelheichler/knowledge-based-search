This chapter presents a complete, step-by-step workflow for conducting a professional OSINT investigation, from receiving the initial assignment through collecting and organizing evidence. It is aimed at practitioners who already know the individual tools described in the rest of the book and need a repeatable process for applying them effectively. The core principle throughout is that staying organized and following a repeatable process is the primary factor in producing a professional work product.

## Receiving the Mission

OSINT assignments arrive through what law enforcement calls "intake." Common request types include threat assessments for individuals (identifying who someone is and what their true intent is), threat assessments for events (monitoring intelligence before and during significant incidents), target profiles for individuals (full online presence, email addresses, home addresses, associates, and hobbies), target profiles for organizations (online footprint and technological infrastructure of a business, criminal enterprise, or organized group), and subscriber identification or account attribution (linking a domain, IP address, or online account to a real person).

## Triage

Triage means assessing the mission and forming a plan before doing any searching. Rushing to action with no clear direction is the most common mistake new practitioners make. Depending on urgency, triage might take 30 seconds or 30 minutes, but having a deliberate plan before starting is essential.

**Clarify mission objectives.** The first step is to articulate the investigative questions, ideally in writing. Confirm them with whoever made the request, repeating back the specific identifiers they provided (email addresses, phone numbers, IP addresses) so errors can be caught early. A sample confirmation: "To be clear, you want to know the real name and physical addresses associated with the person in control of the email account kirby@rockerzandrollerz.com, and we have two hours to accomplish this. Is this correct?" This sets expectations, defines scope, creates a written record in case details are later misremembered, and gives the requestor a chance to catch typos or mix-ups between victim and suspect identifiers. Those mix-ups occur frequently and can waste significant investigative time if not caught at the start. The confirmed questions become explicit goals, such as: "Find the legal name of the real person associated with kirby@rockerzandrollerz.com" and "Find any home and/or work addresses for kirby@rockerzandrollerz.com."

**Gather context.** Ask how each initial lead was obtained and how reliable it is. Ask what is already known about the target's location, culture, or habits. Background context makes it easier to recognize relevant accounts and identifiers during research. Never assume the requestor has shared all available information.

**Legal preservation letters.** For law enforcement work, if a known platform (such as Gmail) is involved, send a preservation letter early requesting that data be retained in anticipation of a future subpoena or search warrant. The ISP list at https://www.search.org/resources/isp-list/ is a starting point for platform legal contacts. Calling a live person and building rapport is more effective than sending only an email.

**Deconfliction.** When an investigation involves infiltrating criminal communities, confirm with colleagues in other agencies or units that no one else is already working the same target. Failing to do this can result in investigators unknowingly interacting with each other's undercover personas, wasting effort, and complicating both cases.

**Note-taking.** Begin notes during triage using two tools in parallel. A paper scratch pad allows quickly noting key details without leaving the browser, which is especially important on a laptop with limited screen space. A digital notebook (such as Microsoft OneNote) is for pasting content copied from the browser. Note that Microsoft products do collect user information, so confirm this fits within operational security requirements. At the top of the legal pad, list investigative goals and planned first steps. In the digital notebook, create and title a new section using the date, incident type, or case number (for example, "OSINT Request Oct13_2019" or "Robbery 19-486544") and paste any written correspondence into it.

**Check whether OSINT is the right tool.** Before spending hours online, consider whether a short phone call (for example, to a postal inspector) would get the same information faster. Strong investigators use every available tool, not just online ones.

## Knolling Your Tools

"Knolling" is the practice of organizing and laying out tools before work begins, the way a surgeon's instruments are sanitized and arranged in advance. Preparation steps include:

- Confirm the VPN is active on the host machine and connected to a node geographically near the target's likely location.
- Launch VirtualBox and load the custom OSINT Linux virtual machine.
- Inside the VM, open the browser and log in to covert social media accounts used for research. Pre-authenticating saves time later.
- If new covert accounts must be created, disconnect from the VPN first. Freshly created accounts that run many queries quickly are highly likely to attract security challenges or suspension. Maintain a supply of semi-mature accounts for time-sensitive work.
- Open the custom OSINT tool pages built from earlier chapters.
- Create a new folder in the VM's shared directory, named to match the case (by date, target name, or case number). This folder receives manually captured digital evidence such as saved images and PDF captures. Pre-populating a template directory with subfolders for each custom tool category saves repetitive setup across cases.

## Closed-Source and Premium Data

Begin the research phase with proprietary and paid sources before open-source tools. These sources often quickly surface addresses, associates, and phone numbers that would otherwise require many open-source queries to uncover.

Commercial aggregators such as Accurint (LexisNexis), TLO, and Clear draw on credit histories and utilities. They are strongest for residential address history, landline phone numbers, employers, roommates, and family members. They work poorly for email addresses, usernames, and social media accounts.

Premium people-search services include Pipl, Spokeo, BeenVerified, Intelius, and WhitepagesPro. Pricing ranges from roughly $15 to $300 per month. Spokeo is among the cheapest ($15 to $20 per month) but has limited data and charges extra for printable reports. BeenVerified allows unlimited fairly extensive reports for $53 quarterly and produces print-friendly output, though it pushes users toward additional spending on "premium" data. Pipl Pro formerly offered some of the best results but has moved to a complex per-record pricing model and is among the most expensive. The key point: everything available from paid people-search sites can also be found for free through open-source tools, but with considerably more time and effort.

Government and law enforcement databases such as Department of Licensing records, criminal history systems, Department of Corrections records, and agency records management systems are particularly valuable because they often include a photo of the target, which can verify or rule out social media accounts. They also typically include recent associates, phone numbers, and residential addresses. Even addresses provided during previous contacts with government agents (such as a relative's home used to receive mail) are investigative leads worth following.

Save promising identifiers to the notepad. Drop generated reports into the digital notebook as PDFs. Copy or paste photos into the digital notes or the case folder.

## Open-Source Research and Collection

After exhausting paid and in-house resources, move into open-source tools. This stage involves the largest number of sites and requires careful tab discipline.

Start by running a Google search on the known identifiers, then open the custom OSINT toolset and use the category matching those identifiers (email tools, username tools, etc.). Also check any sites recommended by colleagues that are not yet in the custom toolset, and add effective new resources to the toolset for future use.

The core rule during browsing is tab discipline: deal with each open tab completely before moving to the next. The most common mistake, called "rabbit holing," is clicking on interesting leads before fully reviewing the current page. For each tab, scroll the full page and right-click any promising link to open it in a new tab. Check image results (either from the inline "Images for..." block in Google results, or via the Images tab). Only after fully reviewing the page and opening all promising leads should you move to the next tab. Use Ctrl+Tab on Windows or Command+Tab on Mac to advance through tabs, with Shift added to reverse direction. Close any tabs that are false positives or contain no useful results immediately, to control clutter and reduce workstation load.

When a page contains multiple strong new leads that each require their own full set of queries, open those leads in a new browser window rather than a new tab. Each new window represents an independent branch of the investigation and each strong lead gets its own page in the digital notebook. Add a + symbol to a page title to mark that lead as fully exhausted.

The digital notebook section for a case should include: a Scratch Page for quickly pasting links and reminders, a Request Details page for the original assignment and triage notes, pages for Premium and Government Data containing pasted reports or snippets, and one page per strong lead identifier (email address, username, phone number, etc.) titled with that identifier.

## Tab Management with OneTab

When all tabs in a window have been reviewed, use OneTab (one-tab.com), installed as an extension in both Chrome and Firefox in the VM, to preserve the session. To collapse tabs: right-click anywhere in the window, select the OneTab icon, and click "Send all tabs to OneTab." In the OneTab manager, click in front of the tab count to add a descriptive title such as "Google Target Name." Use Ctrl+F on Windows or Command+F on Mac to search saved tab sets by title later. To save the list in the digital notebook, click "Export/Import URLs" in OneTab, copy the URL list, and paste it into the appropriate notebook page. The export lacks titles but each set is separated by a blank line in the same order as the manager. Alternative tab managers with online sync or advanced features include Toby, Tabs-Outliner, Workona, and Graphitabs. The author notes that every browser extension carries a security cost and should be installed only when the functionality is mission-critical.

## Context Menu Queries

The ContextSearch extension (github.com/ssborbis/ContextSearch-web-ext) allows right-clicking on any highlighted text or link to immediately run a search against a configurable list of engines and custom queries. To add a custom query: open the extension settings by clicking the gear icon, go to the Search Engines tab, click Add, enter a name, and paste the target URL into the template field with `{SEARCHTERMS}` appended at the end. For example, adding a Dehashed query means highlighting the phrase "tom jones," right-clicking, and clicking the Dehashed option to get immediate results without switching windows. Context-based search complements but does not replace the custom toolset: it offers speed and convenience but lacks the full control and customizability of a dedicated tool page.
