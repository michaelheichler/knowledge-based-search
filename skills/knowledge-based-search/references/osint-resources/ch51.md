This unit covers the final stages of OSINT casework: removing metadata from reports, archiving completed cases, structuring reports for investigations of any scale, setting up isolated mobile infrastructure for long-running cases, and building organizational policy around online investigations.

## Metadata Removal Before Delivery

Strip embedded metadata from any document before sharing it. Adobe Acrobat and Microsoft Office both include built-in sanitization tools. In Acrobat, the "Sanitize Document" option applies a default removal pass. The manual metadata editor gives more granular control. In Office applications, the Document Inspector serves the same function. Back up the original before removing metadata, since the process can break formatting or corrupt the file. The Metadata Anonymization Toolkit (MAT, at github.com/jubalh/MAT) is available for Linux but is more likely to corrupt Microsoft and Adobe documents than the built-in tools. A low-tech alternative for non-evidentiary documents: print the document and scan it back to digital. The resulting file carries the scanner's metadata rather than the investigator's workstation or account details. This method is not appropriate for sensitive documents if the scanner is an enterprise copier that writes data to an internal hard drive.

## Case Archiving

Once a report is complete, collect analog and digital casework and archive it. Retention periods depend on organizational policy or client expectations. Bazzell retains handwritten notes for six months and digital documentation for at least three years, anticipating that a case may later be adjudicated or that the same target may resurface.

For teams using OneNote or similar digital notebooks, a clear system prevents clutter. One approach: maintain an archive notebook for each year with sections organized by month. Completed cases move to the archive. At month-end, review remaining sections for status. At year-end, tidy loose ends and consider purging notebooks untouched for more than three years, or export them to optical discs. Any system must comply with the agency's retention and audit policies.

## Report Structure for Investigations

The recommended report components are:

**Executive Summary (one page).** Presents only the most critical evidence in plain language, as if delivering an elevator pitch. No explanation of investigative technique is included. The example in the text concerns a worker's compensation fraud investigation. The summary lists the most damaging findings:

- June 12, 2017: subject claimed a back injury at work.
- June 11, 2017: subject received a Facebook Event invitation to a family gathering at Lake of the Ozarks on June 17.
- June 14, 2017: subject's chiropractor, Dr. Neil Stevens of Ladue, Missouri, ordered bedrest until further notice.
- June 18, 2017: the subject's sister posted a photo showing the subject lifting a keg of beer above his head.
- June 27, 2017: subject participated in a 5K race in St. Louis, placing 113th out of 1,237 with a time of 00:28:16.
- August 12, 2017: subject posted an Amazon review describing ATV hunting in rough terrain the previous week.
- September 22, 2017: subject and Dr. Stevens placed second together in a duck hunting competition in Boone County, Missouri. Online records confirm they were on the same team.
- Dr. Stevens' daughter and the subject's daughter are documented friends, with a recorded sleepover at the doctor's home on March 12, 2017.

Each fact is stated as a dated observation without injected opinion.

**Suspect Details.** A structured template capturing full name, home and mailing addresses, date of birth, telephone numbers, email addresses, usernames, and social network profiles across Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, Google, and other platforms, for the subject and immediate family members. This section is a quick-reference sheet for account identifiers and does not cite sources.

**Narrative Report.** A detailed account of what was found and how evidence was preserved. Screen captures are saved as PDFs and titled with a sequential number, the full URL (with slashes replaced by underscores), and a timestamp in YYYY-MM-DD-HH-MM-SS format. Example: `001-https_facebook.com_JohnDavis9 | 2017-11-17-10-15-11.pdf`. Each batch of captures is followed by a brief statement of why the content matters. Screen captures are not embedded in the printed report body. Clients receive them on a disc or drive. Cropped subsets that highlight specific content are saved as separate files with an "a" suffix, for example `045a-Cropped Messages.pdf`. Twitter searches use the native query syntax to isolate conversations between two accounts: `from:kdavis722 to:pstevens6655` and the reverse. The exported archive of up to 3,200 recent posts is saved as a CSV on the evidence disc. Any evidence supporting the subject is included and noted.

**Summary Report (one page).** States conclusions as facts the evidence supports, identifies whether the investigation is resolved, and flags any need for future monitoring.

**Digital Evidence.** A DVD or drive containing all screen captures, exported files, and the exported virtual machine image for the investigation.

## Virtual Machine Disclosure Language

Each narrative report should include a paragraph disclosing the investigation's technical environment. The suggested text states that the investigation was conducted inside a Linux virtual machine created on a specific date with security updates applied, that an original copy was preserved untouched, that a clone bearing the case number was the sole operating environment for the investigation, and that the exported VM file allows the entire environment to be recreated exactly as it appeared during the investigation. This language signals investigative discipline to prosecutors and defense counsel and may reduce scrutiny at trial.

## Large-Scale Investigations: Dedicated Mobile Infrastructure

Investigations spanning weeks or months and requiring strict isolation from other casework benefit from a dedicated mobile device and cellular data account for each case. The steps:

Purchase a refurbished unlocked Android device from a cell phone repair shop for approximately $20, or find similar units at grocery stores, pawn shops, eBay, or Amazon. The device is dedicated to the investigation and never used for anything else. Purchase a Mint Mobile SIM card (approximately $2.50 each from Amazon or mintmobile.com). Each SIM includes a $5.00 service credit and a one-week free trial. Three months of service costs $15 per month ($45 total). T-Mobile provides the underlying data network. Pay for both the device and SIM with prepaid gift cards or Privacy.com virtual cards.

Insert the SIM, download the Mint Mobile application over Wi-Fi, and register an account under an alias. Because the service is prepaid, no identity verification or credit check is required. The resulting local telephone number can be provided freely to platforms such as Facebook and Gmail that demand SMS verification and would otherwise suspend accounts tied to VOIP numbers or VPN connections. For applications that block Android emulators (Snapchat, Tinder), install the native app on this device and run a GPS spoofer as needed.

At the end of the investigation, remove the SIM and store both the SIM and the phone in a sealable plastic bag with holes punched for binder storage. Both are part of the investigation record and can be produced during discovery. The prepaid service expires on its own. The number is never reused. Bazzell recommends preparing this infrastructure before a case begins rather than ordering equipment while an investigation is already underway.

## Organizational Policy for Online Investigations

Every agency conducting online investigations should have a formal policy, no longer than one or two pages. Policy language should avoid naming specific platforms or technologies because those change rapidly. Policy should be framed around appropriate use of techniques and technologies in general terms. Online investigations policy belongs in the agency's Investigations section rather than its Social Media section, because investigative work aligns with covert and traditional investigative procedures, not with public-relations use of social media.

The suggested policy framework covers these subsections:

- **1.0 Statement of Intent:** Brief statement aligned with the agency mission, borrowing language from existing investigative policies.
- **1.1 Definitions:** Browser, Private (content transmitted with reasonable measures to limit access to specific individuals), Public (content accessible to a broad or non-specific audience), Post, Site, and Social Media.
- **1.2 Appropriate Use:** Investigative accounts used for agency purposes only. All activity compliant with local, state, and federal law. Legal orders obtained where required.
- **1.3 Approval Process:** Covert accounts and online identities reviewed and approved by a unit supervisor before use. Ongoing casework periodically evaluated for compliance.
- **1.4 Training:** Personnel must complete an approved Covert Online Investigations training course before creating or using investigative social media accounts. Supervisors ensure all personnel receive appropriate training.
- **1.5 Retention and Audit:** Data handled per existing digital evidence and retention policies. Non-standard measures require supervisor review and written justification. All documentation subject to oversight review by the Inspector General or equivalent body.
- **1.6 Agency Forms:** Authorization and Training Verification, Unit Compliance Log.
- **1.7 Acknowledgement of Technological Advancement:** Explicit statement that the policy is intentionally non-specific because technology evolves rapidly.
- **1.8 Revision History** and **1.9 Appendix:** Standard administrative components referencing related policies, procedures, and case law.

For smaller teams without a formal policy structure, converting the same content into a standard operating procedure in a memorandum format approved by a manager above the front-line supervisor achieves the same practical standard. If an agency restricts covert accounts, investigators should push for that restriction to appear in an SOP rather than in formal policy, since SOPs are easier to revise. Recommended language includes phrases such as "for lawful purposes," "consistent with agency training and standards," and "when feasible and reasonable."

For law enforcement, the key legal boundary is reasonable expectation of privacy. Courts have consistently supported online undercover tactics on the grounds that a reasonable person knows that others online may not be who they claim to be. Two situations where investigators can run into trouble: recovering an unlocked phone from an arrestee and impersonating them in ongoing social media conversations (requires a search warrant), and using a cooperating victim's account for direct or private messaging outside a group chat (some jurisdictions require a court order even with the victim's consent). Complexity increases when the victim and suspect are in different states or countries. Bazzell recommends consulting legal counsel familiar with the relevant local, state, and federal laws.
