Written by Sajad Rasool, this chapter introduces photographs and video as a research method for investigators, activists, journalists, and citizen documentarians. It is not a guide to photojournalism or photo-reportage. It teaches how to use visual media to collect, organize, and present evidence that supports an investigation.

## Purpose and limits of visual evidence

The goal of gathering evidence is to accurately recreate the story of what happened, is happening, or did not happen. Images help because they grab attention, explain concepts quickly, and engage audiences faster than written reports. Photography is also a double-edged tool: it can serve as an objective physical record, but it is always shaped by the photographer's choices about where to point the camera and what to include in the frame. Because of this, every image needs verification and context. The five basic questions to apply to any image are who made it, what it shows, where it was taken, when, and why.

Visual evidence is defined broadly as anything that provides information in image form (still or moving) about an incident, a problem, or a person being investigated. Images can come from the investigator's own camera (first-hand) or from other sources such as witnesses, bystanders, or social media (second- or third-hand). Taking images yourself is preferable because it counts as first-hand information.

Images rarely stand alone as proof. They must be accompanied by context and corroborated by other evidence. A quote from Diane Dafour, cited in Jon Nicholls's "The Photograph as Evidence," captures this clearly: "People tend to think that the image, in itself, is an evidence or that the image, in itself, is almost never an evidence. It has to be validated, legitimised, interpreted, read and delivered by an expert who is providing words."

Forensic Architecture, a research agency at Goldsmiths University of London, is cited as an example of an organization that uses imagery and visual evidence systematically, corroborating before-and-after visuals with digital modelling, witness statements, and social media data.

## Know your context

Before collecting visual evidence, understand the legal context. What is admissible, and what is legal to collect, varies by jurisdiction. Something admissible in the US may not be admissible in Kenya. If you are collecting evidence for a legal case, you must understand the standards for admissibility in the relevant jurisdiction, including chain-of-custody requirements for legal evidence.

## From questions to images

Research questions guide what images to collect and how. A good research question in visual (and non-visual) investigation is specific (directly related to the subject matter or event), actionable (possible to plan and research), and practical (possible to find answers given different situations and constraints).

Choosing the right questions before going into the field makes visual evidence collection more focused and ensures that what is captured can actually be turned into relevant evidence. This framing applies whether the purpose is documentation, civic art, publication, or a package of evidence for court.

## What, Who, and How

When documenting an event, every set of visuals should answer three questions:

- **What** action was committed? (For example: use of force on protesters, spilling of toxic chemicals, demolition of a home.)
- **Who** committed the action? (For example: police, army, a machine operator, a company official.)
- **How** did they carry it out? (For example: batons, stones, a specific machine, a chemical compound.)

Capturing "What" is instinctive. You see something wrong and point the camera at it. Capturing "Who" and "How" is harder and less intuitive. It requires photographing both sides of the action, the means used, and identifying details. For a police-on-protester event, this means: images of police beating people, images of people being beaten, images of police badges and weapons, images of any weapons used by protesters. For a house demolition, this means: the machine in operation, its registration number, the operator, the official issuing the order (with badge if possible), and the people being removed.

## Before and after

Collecting images before and after an incident strengthens the evidentiary record. For a demolition, a photograph of the house before it was destroyed establishes its prior condition. For a protest, footage showing how the situation looked before police moved in provides context. Ask bystanders and witnesses whether they have images taken before the incident. They may have captured relevant context without realizing it. Before-and-after documentation applies to property damage, environmental damage, use of excessive force, and construction sites, among other situations.

## Safety

Assessing safety comes before collecting images. Questions to ask:

- Am I documenting something that has happened or that is happening now?
- What equipment will I use?
- Is the location safe? Will taking photographs provoke local authorities or the community?
- Am I putting anyone else at risk, and how can I mitigate that?

Asking witnesses for images can expose your activity and its purpose. Some people present at an event may be there on behalf of potential perpetrators. Consider whether publishing images could endanger subjects, for example by identifying protest participants. Think about whether children's privacy is violated by photographing them in certain conditions.

The chapter includes a first-person example from the author: while interviewing the father of a boy killed during a police action at a protest, the author was ordered to stop and accompany police to a station. Rather than argue, the author complied, but kept the camera running in record mode. The lesson is to assess what is feasible from a security standpoint and to cooperate with authorities when resistance would be unsafe, while preserving evidence where possible.

Good community relationships help reduce many risks involved with field documentation.

## Essential practices

**Take many photos from many angles.** Details not immediately obvious at the scene may become apparent when reviewing images later. The act of going through photos and video after the fact often reveals more than was apparent in the moment.

**Charge devices and carry backup equipment.** Bring extra batteries, storage, and charging equipment. You cannot predict how long you will be in the field or what you will need to document.

**Know your role.** Are you an intentional documentarian or an accidental witness? Know what is and is not legal to photograph in the location and jurisdiction where you are working. Read the relevant laws and consult someone with practical local experience.

**First-hand vs. second-hand.** Take images yourself when possible. When you collect images from other sources, they count as second-hand (or third-hand) information and require additional verification steps.

## Ethics and consent

When taking a visual, three groups are involved: the photographer, the people photographed, and the audience. Ethical documentation requires informed consent from anyone whose face and identity you capture. This is a matter of their safety, respect, and right to privacy. Photographing or filming a victim without informed permission is described in the chapter as unethical, disrespectful, and a form of abuse.

Consent also establishes what will happen to the images after the fact. People who allow themselves to be filmed trust the documentarian to safeguard that trust. Sharing images of abuse can re-victimize, endanger, and shame the subject and their family. Always make sure subjects know they are being filmed and understand how the images will be used.

In public spaces such as protests, consent situations are more varied. In some contexts, raising a camera may be welcomed. In others, it may provoke hostility from protesters, police, or both. Each scenario requires its own assessment before recording begins.

## Technical elements of image quality

The chapter covers several elements that affect image quality and informational value:

**Light.** All photography depends on light. Without adequate light, image-making is not possible without specialized equipment. Light illuminates the scene whether natural or artificial.

**Moment.** Waiting for the moment when all elements in the frame are in meaningful interaction with each other produces images with maximum story and information value.

**Shot.** In video, a shot is a single continuous piece of footage from when you press record to when you press pause. Each shot should run at least 15 to 20 seconds. When filming action such as a vehicle moving through frame, capture the full action and let the subject exit frame before stopping. Having more footage to cut from is better than having too little.

**Composition.** The relationship between characters, objects, and empty space within a frame matters for storytelling. Composition depends on light, shadows, head room, nose room, and angle.

## The Rule of Thirds

The Rule of Thirds is a composition technique. Divide the frame into nine equal parts using two vertical and two horizontal lines. Place key subjects or action at the intersections of those lines rather than at the center, which produces a more balanced composition.

The chapter makes clear that photographic rules are not mandatory when documenting ongoing events. In fast-moving situations, capturing as many images as possible matters more than composition. An explicit instruction from the chapter: capture things as they are, from as many angles as you can, and do not adjust objects to improve composition.

## Five basic shot types

The chapter describes five shot types, each carrying different informational and aesthetic value:

1. **Extreme long shot (establishing shot).** Shows the full setting. Answers "What is happening and where?" In a protest context, it shows protesters, location, and police.
2. **Long shot.** Shows the full body of characters and incorporates all elements relevant to the scene. It answers "Who is present and what do they want?"
3. **Medium shot (mid shot).** Shows subjects from waist to head. Useful for showing individuals in context.
4. **Close-up shot.** Shows intimate details or emotions. Highlights important information about a specific person or object.
5. **Extreme close-up.** Captures minute but impactful details of a scene or action.

A complete evidentiary record should include all five types where possible.

## Managing visual evidence

**Establish location.** Each place where you photograph or film is a "location." Photograph any sign or marker that names the place. If it is safe to do so, keep GPS enabled on your camera or smartphone. GPS data embedded in image metadata helps establish geographic coordinates and can be used to prove where and when an image was taken. In sensitive investigations, GPS data can expose your whereabouts or the location of sources, so disable it when it creates risk. Recommended tools from the Guardian Project for managing location data include LocationPrivacy (for shared location links), ObscuraCam (a privacy camera app that blurs faces and removes location metadata), and ProofMode (which turns photos and videos into secure, signed visual evidence).

**Preserve and safeguard metadata.** Metadata is the information describing a file's properties: date and time taken, device used, and location. Before taking images intended as evidence, ensure that your device's date and time are set accurately. Accurate, unaltered metadata is evidence of what happened at a specific place and time. Metadata is also vulnerable to manipulation and must be protected.

Tools for capturing and preserving metadata:
- **eyeWitness** (https://www.eyewitnessproject.org/): a recording app for capturing verifiable photo and video documenting abuses.
- **Save** (https://open-archive.org/save/) by Open Archive: stores and shares mobile media while protecting the identity of the user. Free, open-source, available for iOS and Android.

Tools for viewing, verifying, or editing metadata:
- **Fotoforensics** (https://fotoforensics.com/): online tool for metadata checks and detecting image alterations. Do not upload sensitive material.
- **Jeffrey's Image Metadata Viewer** (http://exif.regex.info/exif.cgi): online metadata viewer (offline as of January 2023). Same caution applies.
- **Phil Harvey's Exiftool** (https://www.sno.phy.queensu.ca/~phil/exiftool/): desktop metadata viewer and editor. Safer for sensitive material because images stay on your own computer.
- **Reveal Image Verification Assistant** (http://reveal-mklab.iti.gr/reveal/index.html): an alpha-stage tool for image verification on the web.

Note on metadata and messaging apps: when sending images through apps like Signal, Wire, or Telegram, metadata may be stripped or altered. To retain metadata, upload to cloud storage or send by email.

**Safeguard your devices and data.** Data protection has two goals: prevent loss or damage, and prevent interception. Back up data as quickly as possible to cloud storage, external drives, or trusted team members. Encrypt sensitive data using full-disk encryption and encryption software such as Veracrypt or Cryptomator. Travel with devices powered down. For cloud storage, prefer a zero-knowledge provider such as SpiderOak. Safer camera alternatives include CameraV and Save by Open Archive.

## Stay alert in the field

Before raising your camera, assess the situation. Observe the people around you and learn how the space works. Let those present get used to your presence before you begin recording. Use judgment grounded in ethics rather than photographic expertise. If you cannot obtain consent before documenting, make an informed assessment of how to proceed and weigh the risks to people around you, to your sources, and to yourself.

The chapter closes by reiterating that visual evidence works best as part of a broader investigation that includes interviews, data, physical evidence, and other methods. Images are not substitutes for that broader body of evidence.
