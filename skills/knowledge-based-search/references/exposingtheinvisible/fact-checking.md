## What Fact-Checking Is

Fact-checking is the process of verifying whether a piece of information is backed by verifiable facts. The chapter distinguishes it from verification: verification is a broader discipline developed in journalism, while fact-checking is a specific application of verification. Both aim to confirm validity or expose falsehood.

The chapter is written by Deepak Adhikari, editor of South Asia Check, a Nepal-based fact-checking outlet founded in 2015.

## Three Types of Information Disorder

The chapter uses a framework to classify false and harmful information:

- Misinformation: false information spread without intent to harm. The person sharing it believes it is true.
- Disinformation: fabricated or deliberately manipulated content designed to deceive and cause harm.
- Malinformation: genuine information shared deliberately to damage someone. It is factually accurate but weaponized.

Understanding which type is in play matters because it shapes how a fact-checker assesses the actors and motives behind a claim. The chapter credits Claire Wardle, founder of First Draft, and Hossein Derakhshan as the sources of this framework.

## The Fact-Checker's Mindset

The chapter argues that tools alone are not enough. Effective fact-checking requires analytical thinking, healthy skepticism, and an orientation toward evidence. A fact-checker must assess the credibility of sources, understand the context in which a claim is spreading, identify who is behind it and what they stand to gain, and recognize the interconnected ways misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation reinforce each other.

One key discipline is avoiding amplification. Reporting on a false claim can increase its reach. South Asia Check archives problematic content without linking directly to the original source, reducing the risk that the fact-check itself spreads the falsehood further.

The chapter also notes that not everything can be fact-checked. Intentions, prophecies, and predictions fall outside the scope because they cannot be confirmed or denied with publicly available evidence. Fact-checkers should focus on claims where evidence exists.

Careful, repeated viewing of videos and photos is itself a method. The chapter notes that staged content often reveals itself through details such as camera operators directing actors, editing cuts, or inconsistencies in crowd behavior.

## The Fact-Checking Process

The chapter describes four main stages.

### 1. Finding Material to Fact-Check

Prioritize claims that carry significant public interest or that come from high-profile figures whose words carry influence. Statements by politicians, government officials, and prominent public personalities are prime candidates because false claims from powerful sources cause disproportionate harm. Determine whether a claim is a deliberate lie, an honest mistake, or parody before committing to a full investigation, since honest mistakes and parody may not require the same treatment.

### 2. Archiving

Before doing anything else, download relevant media and take screenshots of the original claim. Sources are frequently deleted once they attract scrutiny. Tools for preserving content include:

- Internet Archive and the Wayback Machine (archive.org)
- Archive Today (archive.vn, also archive.is)

Archiving preserves the record and allows fact-checkers to link to a stable copy rather than the live original, which also limits amplification.

### 3. Researching

Research involves several parallel activities:

- Keyword searches through credible sources, newspaper archives, and government websites to locate primary records.
- Reverse image search via Google Images to trace where a photo first appeared and whether it has been reused out of context.
- Video verification using InVID (a browser extension that extracts individual frames from a video for reverse image analysis) and the YouTube Data Viewer (from Amnesty International's Citizen Evidence Lab), which surfaces upload timestamps and thumbnail images.
- Geolocation using Google Earth, Google Street View, Bing Maps, and Here WeGo to confirm whether visual details in a photo or video match a claimed location.
- Source evaluation: check a source's institutional affiliation, methodology, citation record, and track record before relying on it.
- Expert consultation via LinkedIn and Twitter, conducted only after thorough background research on the expert to avoid amplifying a biased or unqualified voice.

### 4. Publishing Findings

A published fact-check should include:

- The original claim quoted verbatim.
- Links to archived versions rather than live sources.
- Expert biographies for any sources cited.
- An explanation of the methodology used to investigate.
- Visual aids such as screenshots, maps, and side-by-side comparisons.
- An explicit verdict: for example, false, misleading, or half-true.

Findings can also feed into longer investigations rather than standing as standalone fact-checks. For sensitive investigations, the chapter recommends maintaining thorough documentation in case of legal challenge.

## Case Studies

The chapter draws on cases from India and Nepal to illustrate how misinformation and disinformation operate in practice and how fact-checkers respond.

**Maharashtra lynching, July 2018.** In Rainpada village, Maharashtra, a mob killed five men from a nomadic community after WhatsApp messages circulated claiming they were child kidnappers. The messages included photographs. AltNews investigated and established that the images were from a 2013 chemical weapons attack in Syria, shared entirely out of context. The incident prompted WhatsApp to restrict message forwarding in India to a maximum of five chats.

**COVID-19 utensil-licking video, 2020.** A video circulated during the pandemic claiming to show Muslim men licking utensils to spread the coronavirus. AltNews, with investigation by Jignesh Patel, traced the video to July 31, 2018, and identified the people in it as members of the Dawoodi Bohra community, a Muslim community with communal eating practices designed to avoid food waste. The disinformation spread in the context of heightened anti-Muslim sentiment following a COVID-19 superspreader event linked to a Tablighi Jamaat gathering in New Delhi in mid-March 2020.

**Nepal loudspeaker ban claim, December 2020.** Indian social media users shared a document in Nepali, claiming Nepal's Supreme Court had issued an order banning mosque loudspeakers entirely. Cross-border fact-checking found the court had only issued an interim order asking mosques to reduce loudspeaker volume. The misrepresentation crossed a national border and required fact-checkers familiar with both the Nepali language and the legal context.

**Modi empty-field video, April 2, 2021.** An opposition party shared a blurred video of Prime Minister Narendra Modi appearing to wave at an empty field during an election period. Boom Live obtained the original footage and showed it contained a cheering crowd in the distance. The shared version had been edited and blurred to remove the crowd.

**Nepal rally photo, February 2021.** A photograph from a political rally in Kathmandu appeared to show an Indian flag among the banners of the Prachanda-Madhav faction of Nepal's Communist Party, fueling claims of Indian interference in Nepali politics. South Asia Check used reverse image search and keyword searches to establish that the Indian flag had been digitally inserted into the image. The investigators also found that those sharing the manipulated photo were aligned with Nepal's ruling party, suggesting a political motive to discredit the opposition.

**Staged Nepal accident video, August 2020.** A video viral among Indian social media users appeared to show journalists callously filming an injured road accident victim without helping. Repeated close viewing by AltNews revealed a camera operator directing actors in the scene, identifying it as staged content created to discredit journalists.

## Why It Matters

The chapter frames fact-checking as collaborative work essential for informed public decision-making in the digital age. Unchecked misinformation has led to real-world violence, as the Maharashtra case demonstrates. Disinformation targeting religious and ethnic minorities, when left unaddressed, compounds social harm. Cross-border disinformation requires cooperation between fact-checkers in different countries with different languages and legal contexts. The discipline exists to interrupt those harms by making the evidence visible.
