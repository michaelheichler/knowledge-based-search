Written by Marek Tuszynski and published March 2020, this chapter is a guide to using visual signs and symbols as investigative evidence, especially when direct information is missing, restricted, or deliberately hidden.

## Core argument

Signs and symbols are everywhere: on buildings, pavements, vehicles, clothes, devices, and online platforms. People use them to sort, register, disguise, mark territory, signal affiliation, and communicate resistance. Investigators can read this visual layer to gain evidence about actors, their roles, and their intentions, particularly in cases where conventional access to information is blocked.

The chapter draws a basic distinction between a sign (which extends language and usually carries a direct instruction or communication) and a symbol (which is more ephemeral, representing a process, function, or object rather than giving a direct command). Both are investigatively useful. The study of the meaning of signs and symbols is called iconography, and it has a long tradition in art history, archaeology, ethnography, and sociology.

A key caution runs throughout: correlation is not proof or guarantee of causation. A single sign may mean many things, or nothing, in a given context. Accumulated patterns are more reliable than isolated occurrences, but even patterns can mislead, because some signs are planted to confuse or intimidate, and meanings shift over time and across contexts.

## Three categories of signs and symbols

The chapter organises signs and symbols into three rough groups.

**Formal signs and symbols** are standardised by government or corporate rules. Examples include car registration plates, aircraft tail numbers, and product markings. Because they are linked to official documentation, they can be traced to ownership records, historical transactions, and other verifiable data.

**Informal signs and symbols** are intentional but often temporary and context-specific. Workers use them to make jobs more efficient: utility markings on pavements, symbols on trees for forestry crews, patches worn by formal or informal groups to signal belonging and recognition. Understanding the context in which they appear is crucial, because the same mark may mean different things in different places.

**Incidental or unintended signs and symbols** are a kind of visual metadata produced by tools or processes without deliberate intent. A printer leaves a unique machine identification code on every page it prints. A specific font, a distinctive way of constructing images, or a unique visual style can also function as an unintended signature.

## Mindset

The chapter emphasises a particular investigative attitude: expanding one's field of vision to the peripheral spaces where information hides. A single unexpected clue can open a story that would otherwise be inaccessible. At the same time, investigators must approach signs with scepticism, stay open to the possibility that they are false leads, and verify assumptions through multiple independent means.

The chapter does not offer a step-by-step formula. Instead, it presents a set of cases that demonstrate what this investigative mindset looks like in practice.

## Case studies

### Political graffiti in Beirut: Tala F. Saleh

While living in Beirut after the 2006 war with Israel, designer Tala F. Saleh documented graffiti systematically, street by street and wall by wall, throughout the city centre over four months in summer 2007. She photographed every stencil and sign, mapped their locations, accumulated images in a simple database, and deciphered logos and symbols to learn their political affiliations and contexts. She found that graffiti in Beirut was predominantly political: logos, slogans, and social commentary that effectively divided the city into politically affiliated neighbourhoods. Her analysis focused on style, medium, message, location, space, and frequency, and tracked how visual communication shifted and was appropriated over time. Her prior work on Beirut's pre-war graffiti (1975 to 2009) and on Western forms of political graffiti gave her the visual and critical skills to conduct the analysis. The result was her 2009 book "Marking Beirut: A City Revealed through its Graffiti." She concluded that this visual culture is a culture of unofficial propaganda and mass local communication, intended to keep inhabitants aware of political and spatial divisions, opinions, conflicts, and influences.

The chapter draws from this a broader lesson: the same accumulation method applies to online visual cultures, where memes, animated GIFs, emoticons, ASCII art, and hashtags carry equivalent layers of information about affiliation and identity. Online visual material also carries metadata (posting time, location, account age) in the same way that physical graffiti carries metadata in the form of style, paint type, location, and timing.

### Urban infrastructure in New York: Ingrid Burrington

Artist and writer Ingrid Burrington explored the telecommunications infrastructure of New York City by learning the informal visual vocabulary that workers use on its streets and buildings. By reading cable markings, manhole cover inscriptions, and other utility signs on pavements and buildings, she traced the underground networks of cables and identified the companies that own them. Her findings appear in "Networks Of New York: An Illustrated Field Guide to Urban Internet Infrastructure" (Melville House, 2016). The lesson the chapter draws is that learning the visual language of workers, who are focused on practical efficiency rather than on the politics of what they do, can reveal invisible structures and power dynamics. An important part of Burrington's method was talking to the people who make the signs, because they are the best source for explaining their own language. The same approach applies in non-urban settings: forestry workers leave visual symbols on trees when planning work, and the Center for Land Use Interpretation studies how human activities leave intentional and incidental marks on the surface of the earth.

### Military patches and the Pentagon's black budget: Trevor Paglen

American artist Trevor Paglen investigated the United States government's "black budget," a pool of funds listed officially under "Research, Development, Test and Evaluation" with project names but no disclosed spending amounts. The gap between total budget figures and disclosed numbers suggested about $30 billion was going to covert programmes. Paglen found no formal legal route to explore this. Instead, he turned to collectors of military memorabilia and discovered what one collector called "patch intel." Military patches, even those representing classified units, use symbols of stars, lightning bolts, strange creatures, and other devices to let personnel identify themselves and their affiliations. Paglen amassed nearly 60 patches and decoded their symbolism in his 2008 book "I Could Tell You But Then You Would Have to Be Destroyed By Me: Emblems from the Pentagon's Black World."

One example from the book: a patch from the Special Projects Flight Test Squadron contains a cluster of five stars plus one (indicating Area 51), a wizard mascot, an engineering symbol for an unknown radar value (indicating stealth), falling hollow aluminium spheres (used to calibrate radars), a lightning bolt (electronic warfare), and a sword shaped like the "Bird of Prey" Boeing stealth demonstrator aircraft.

The central insight is that pride drives people to create symbols of belonging even in secretive work. Something as ephemeral as pride makes soldiers rely on insignia, and wearing insignia shows others that one is part of something bigger than themselves. The patches do not fully disclose classified programmes, but they create a visual map revealing what some units work on, where they operate, and roughly how much taxpayer money is involved.

### Photography as access in East Jerusalem: Hagit Keisar

Artist and researcher Hagit Keisar was investigating house demolitions in East Jerusalem and was repeatedly refused access to the official archives documenting demolition procedures, because her intent was seen as critical. A breakthrough came when a retired house demolition inspector revealed that photography was a central tool of the inspectors' work. Keisar reframed her request: she asked to study how the archive used photography as a practice. The archive granted immediate access. This reframing not only unlocked the documents but also let her discover the internal visual language that inspectors used in their photographs, which in turn revealed the politics, rationale, and abuses of power embedded in their work.

### Surveillance programme code names on LinkedIn: M.C. McGrath

Security researcher and activist M.C. McGrath observed that people who work on covert intelligence programmes still need to move between jobs and write CVs that allow potential employers to recognise their skills. He scraped the LinkedIn profiles of approximately 27,000 to 30,000 people who used code names of US surveillance and intelligence programmes in their employment descriptions. Code names appear meaningless to outsiders, but within a professional context they function as recognisable references. The resulting database, called ICWATCH, launched in 2015 through his Transparency Toolkit project, mapped a significant portion of the intelligence community and its programmes using only information people had posted publicly. The lesson: do not be deterred by jargon, acronyms, or code languages that seem meaningless, because they carry precise meaning within a specialised professional context.

### Hashtags as evidence: Marc Owen Jones and Hadi Al Khatib

A hashtag is a metadata tag that functions like a code name or acronym in social media spaces. The chapter presents two cases.

Marc Owen Jones investigated an industrial-scale automated propaganda campaign in the Persian Gulf. He noticed that different Twitter accounts were posting identical tweets and using the same set of hashtags at the same times. By accumulating and analysing account data, including dates of account creation, number of followers, location, and biography, he identified a large network of bots promoting certain viewpoints and suppressing others. This work is documented in "Automated Sectarianism and Pro-Saudi Propaganda on Twitter."

The chapter also references "Disclosures of a #Hashtag," a piece published through Exposing the Invisible, as a further example of how hashtag analysis can map networks and social connections among participants in a given online space.

### Passenger Name Records: Paz Pena, Leil-Zahra Mortada, Rose Regina Lawrence

A Passenger Name Record (PNR) is a six-digit code assigned when a flight is booked, usually represented as a barcode on boarding cards. It looks innocuous, but it is an identifier containing a detailed data set including the traveller's name, address, ID or passport details, contact details, frequent flyer information, full itinerary, payment method, seat and check-in information, meal preferences, and sometimes reasons for travel. The PNR data is held mainly by two large systems: the Computer Reservation System (CRS) and the Global Distribution System (GDS). Tactical Tech's article "Booking Flights: Our Data Flies with Us" showed how decoding a boarding card barcode gives access to all of this personal information. The investigative point is that codes presented as unintelligible numbers are machine-readable records that, when viewed through the right lens, reveal detailed personal information.

### Reading redacted documents: Sam Raphael, Crofton Black, and Ruth Blakeley

Raphael, Black, and Blakeley (The Rendition Project, the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, the University of Westminster, and the University of Sheffield) spent four years producing the 2019 report "CIA Torture Unredacted," the most detailed public account of the CIA's post-9/11 torture programme between 2001 and 2009. Their key documents were heavily redacted, with some pages almost entirely blacked out and specific terms replaced with codes such as "country a" or "destination site b." They recovered hidden text by analysing the typography. The documents used Times New Roman at 12 point, a proportional font where each letter has a different width. Text was left-aligned with consistent character spacing. Dates followed a standard format of "Month, DD, YEAR." Only individual words and figures were redacted, not whole paragraphs. By combining these typographic constraints with comprehensive data sets of names, locations, and times, the investigators correlated and triangulated known information with obfuscated information to un-redact the documents to an unprecedented degree. A secondary consequence: the work taught those who perform redaction how not to do it in future.

### Visual clues and identity attribution: Brian Krebs and the Mirai botnet

Security expert Brian Krebs investigated the authors of the Mirai botnet, malware that caused widespread internet disruption in 2016. Tracking the pseudonym "Dreadiscool," Krebs found a meme the user had created: a modified image from the film "Pulp Fiction" with the heads of John Travolta and Samuel L. Jackson replaced by those of two hackers known as Vyp0r and Tucker Preston, alongside an anime figure of Yamada from the manga series "B Gata H Hei." On the same forum, the user had listed nine films they had watched, including "Mirai Nikki," another manga series. While that was a weak link on its own (anime is common among certain young hackers), it was one of several correlating clues. They led Krebs to establish that Dreadiscool had also used the pseudonym Anna Senpai, who had leaked the Mirai source code in 2016. Further correlations identified the real name as Paras Jha. Krebs published his findings, and law enforcement picked up the case.

The chapter uses this case to make a further point: visual clues are ephemeral. Once attributed, creators may use the same symbols differently to mislead investigators. Symbols also get appropriated by different groups and change meaning over time. The example given is Pepe the Frog, a cartoon character created by Matt Furie in 2005 in an obscure zine called "Boy's Club." It became associated with the alt-right in the United States, then was used as a symbol of resistance during the 2019 Hong Kong protests by participants who were unaware of its preceding associations, leading some observers to wrongly connect the Hong Kong protests with the alt-right.

## Absence as evidence

The chapter includes a section on what it calls "spaces that contain no information." Drawing on an analogy to astronomy (where the existence of a planet can be deduced by triangulating secondary observations before it is directly observed), the chapter suggests that gaps or empty spaces in datasets can focus investigative resources and reveal deliberate concealment. Looking for what is missing can be as productive as looking for what is present.

## General principles

The chapter closes with three principles the cases share.

First, effective investigators expand the periphery of what they look at, pursuing any visual clue even if it seems irrelevant or unrelated to the core investigation.

Second, investigators develop or seek out specialised expertise in the visual material they are working with. They talk to the people who make or use the signs, and they find collectors or analysts who study the same material for entirely different purposes (the example given is plane-spotters, who track aircraft for personal interest and not to investigate covert flights or corruption).

Third, investigators treat visual clues with reservations and scepticism. Interpretation, correlation, and triangulation can be corrupted at any point for unknown reasons. This method works best as a supporting framework alongside other, more direct forms of evidence collection and verification.

## Glossary terms defined in the chapter

**ASCII:** American Standard Code for Information Interchange. A code representing 128 English characters as numbers from 0 to 127.

**Bot (web robot, internet bot):** A software application that runs automated tasks over the internet.

**Graffiti:** Writings or drawings made on public surfaces, usually without permission, as artistic expression, political expression, or advocacy.

**Hashtag:** A metadata tag introduced by the # symbol, used on social networks to let users find messages with a specific theme.

**Iconography:** The study of the meaning of symbols and signs.

**Meme:** An idea, image, or text (usually humorous) that is created, copied, and spread rapidly by internet users, often with slight mutations.

**Metadata:** Information describing properties of a file, such as the date, location, and device used to create an image.

**Sign:** Extends language and communicates with people who have incorporated it into their vocabulary. Often carries a direct instruction.

**Symbol:** A representation of something (a process, function, or object) rather than a direct instruction. More ephemeral than a sign.
