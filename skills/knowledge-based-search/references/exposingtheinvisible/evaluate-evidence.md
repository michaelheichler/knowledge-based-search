By Mario Rautner. Published December 2020.

An investigation is only as strong as its evidence. Without evidence, claims have little substance and may expose the investigator to legal risk. This chapter explains how to develop a hypothesis, classify and analyse evidence, reduce bias, test competing explanations, and manage and publish findings.

## Developing a hypothesis

A hypothesis is "a supposition or proposed explanation made on the basis of limited evidence as a starting point for further investigation." Using one gives the investigation focus and provides something to test evidence against. The preferred term is "working hypothesis" because it is a work in progress that changes as new evidence appears.

Professor Mark Lee Hunter (founding member of the Global Investigative Journalism Network, GIJN) argues in "Story-Based Inquiry" that developing a hypothesis is the core of the investigative method. A starting hypothesis can look speculative but should be adjusted as proof accumulates. Failing to adjust it risks falling into conspiracy-theory thinking.

**What counts as proof.** Proof is defined by the Cambridge dictionary as "a fact or piece of information that shows that something exists or is true." The goal is to collect enough evidence to prove or disprove the hypothesis. Reaching 100% proof is rare. More often the collected evidence results in an approximation: after considering all possible explanations, the hypothesis is the most likely account of events. Investigators, citizen journalists, and civil society members have a different role from scientists or courts. The goal is not to prove the hypothesis at all costs but to remain open to alternative explanations. If an investigation reaches a dead end, the options are to stop, to adjust the hypothesis, or to publish what is known transparently without making unsupported claims.

## Types of evidence

Evidence may be direct or indirect, human or physical or digital, and gathered from desktop or field research. Most evidence falls into more than one category. The category matters when judging reliability and strength.

**Direct vs indirect.** Direct evidence speaks straight to the hypothesis and establishes facts: video, audio, photographs, eyewitness accounts, official documents, and records. Indirect evidence supports an assertion but cannot establish facts to the same degree. It includes statements from people who did not observe events directly, statements by spokespersons, and any evidence requiring an inference. An inference is an opinion or conclusion formed from evidence that does not directly speak to that conclusion. Indirect evidence supports a hypothesis only when a large amount of it accumulates and no other logical conclusion is possible. A key point: what is indirect evidence for one assertion can be direct evidence for another. A company spokesperson saying "we do not discharge toxic chemicals" is indirect evidence that the company caused no pollution, but it is direct evidence that the company denied causing pollution.

**Human, physical, and digital.** Human sources include eyewitnesses, victims, government officials, scientists, spokespeople, and experts. Digital and physical evidence are always linked to human evidence at some level, since people develop, create, or analyse everything. Physical evidence includes paper documents, books, contracts, invoices, environmental samples, and photos and videos in their raw formats from cameras. Physical evidence is generally more trustworthy than digital counterparts because it is harder to manipulate. For the purposes of this guide, digital evidence applies only to information found on the internet: online articles, reports, databases, social media, blogs, and discussion forums. Nearly everything in the digital world can be manipulated including images and video. Metadata of photographs taken by others can be altered easily and should not be relied on as evidence unless confirmed from the original source or through tools such as TinEye, Google Images, or Yandex Images, or by geolocation and time verification. Deepfakes (computer-generated photos and videos that are convincing) emerged in 2017 and their use is spreading. William A. Galston at Brookings Institution has argued that deepfakes can distort democratic discourse, manipulate elections, erode trust in institutions, weaken journalism, exacerbate social divisions, and inflict hard-to-repair reputational damage. Tactical Tech's Deepfake Lab provides interactive examples of how this technology works.

**Desktop and field research.** Desktop research covers anything gathered using a computer: scientific or newspaper articles, databases, satellite imagery, digital maps, social media, and documents received via Freedom of Information requests. Field research requires leaving the office and includes interviews, library and archive visits, and environmental sampling. Not all scientific papers are equally reliable. The strongest research appears in peer-reviewed journals such as Nature or Science Magazine, where other researchers in the same field verify each article before publication. Pre-prints published before peer review is complete carry more risk. Research funded by companies may favor those companies' interests. Beall's List, created by librarian Jeffrey Beall, identifies potentially predatory open-access journals and provides criteria for judging a journal's credibility.

**Evidence classification.** The chapter uses a running example: "Company X is discharging toxic water into the river." For that assertion, a lab letter showing elevated river toxicity is direct, physical, and field evidence. A recorded interview of a company representative on the internet is indirect, human, and desktop evidence. A CEO admission captured in an undercover recording is direct, human, and field evidence. The same evidence may shift category depending on the specific assertion being tested.

## Analysing evidence

**Strength and accuracy of sources.** The same information can be strong or weak depending on who provided it and why. Source motivation matters as much as the content. A company spokesperson denying pollution provides weak evidence of innocence. The same spokesperson admitting to pollution provides medium-to-strong evidence. The chapter provides two paired tables showing how source strength shifts between a denial claim and a positive claim.

For "Company X is NOT polluting the river": anonymous letter (weak), company spokesperson (weak), local politician social media post (weak), study commissioned by the investigated company (medium), independent scientific analysis with verified independence (strong), local council report with water test figures (medium), report by local business council (weak), interview with a resident (weak).

For "Company X IS polluting the river": most of the same sources shift upward. A study commissioned by the company concluding there is pollution becomes strong. An independent scientific analysis remains strong. A local council report showing elevated toxicity becomes strong. A company spokesperson admitting pollution shifts to medium. The pattern shows that source strength is not fixed. It depends on whether the source has an incentive to make the statement.

Some sources carry built-in review processes that make them generally stronger. Peer-reviewed scientific articles are stronger, though one should check funding sources and whether a paper is a pre-print. Reports from media organisations affiliated with the International Fact Checking Network or GIJN may have fact-checking in place. Government information is more trustworthy from less corrupt and more transparent governments, but official information alone rarely suffices to contradict a hypothesis.

**Cognitive bias.** Investigators are not immune to the tendency to believe what they want to believe. Author Angela Saini wrote that "Scientists who imagine that bias lies in others, not themselves, fail to recognize that to live in the world today is to be drip-fed assumptions and prejudices that guide our thoughts and actions." Cognitive bias was first defined through psychological research in the 1970s. Citizen investigators face particular risk because they often work alone, have personal attachment to outcomes, and lack the review processes of professional organisations.

Key types of cognitive bias for investigators:

- Confirmation bias: favouring information that confirms existing beliefs and discounting contradictory evidence. A German experiment found journalists were as susceptible to this as citizens.
- Sunk-cost effect: continuing an investigation because of effort already invested, even when contradictory evidence appears.
- Availability bias: giving more weight to information recalled most easily, often the most recent, which can cause errors in long investigations.
- Anchoring bias: giving the most weight to the first piece of information encountered, treating it as a baseline against which all later evidence is compared.

Techniques to reduce bias: seek a colleague's second opinion on evidence interpretation. Review conclusions after some time has passed. Actively attempt to disprove conclusions.

**Mitigating information risks.** A rule common in journalism requires at least three sources independent of each other confirming the same information before publishing. The chapter pairs each type of evidence with its specific risk and a recommended mitigation action. A lab result showing river toxicity does not link the pollution to any specific company, so the investigator should talk to the lab directly and plan further research to trace the source. A mayor's press release dismissing pollution from a company has a self-interest motivation, so the investigator should look into campaign contributions and other links between the mayor and the company. A CEO admission captured by undercover recording may invite legal threats, so the investigator should show the material to a trustworthy lawyer before proceeding.

## Analysing competing hypotheses

The best way to reduce bias is to actively seek evidence that disproves the hypothesis. Testing alternative explanations increases objectivity. The formal technique for this is Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH), originally developed by the CIA in the 1970s. The ACH process: generate possible hypotheses, build a matrix with hypotheses as columns and pieces of evidence as rows, mark each cell as C (consistent with the hypothesis) or I (inconsistent), then rank the hypotheses. The hypothesis with the most I marks is least supported.

The chapter illustrates ACH with the pollution example. Two competing hypotheses: "Company X caused the pollution" and "something else caused the pollution." The CEO's undercover admission and the pipeline effluent tests are inconsistent with the "something else" hypothesis. Most other pieces of evidence are consistent with both. For most citizen investigations, applying common sense to alternative interpretations is sufficient without using formal ACH software.

When strong evidence contradicts the working hypothesis, the only ethical action is to accept the hypothesis is wrong. The investigator then either forms a new hypothesis that accounts for the new facts or ends the investigation. Before stopping, it is worth reviewing the methodology, tools, evidence collected, and any remaining alternatives.

A practical tip: write a version of the investigation with every statement referenced by footnotes or endnotes. This helps locate evidence quickly and ensures no claim appears without support.

## Managing evidence

**Information security.** Information security is the practice of defending information from being accessed or damaged by unwanted others. It is especially important for citizen investigators, journalists, and activists who are often targeted by subjects of their investigations. Key resources: "Safety First!" in this Kit, Security In A Box, and Surveillance Self-Defense by the Electronic Frontier Foundation. Investigators should encrypt hard drives, protect communications with confidential sources, and keep frequent backups on a separate encrypted device stored in a different location. Encryption matters everywhere, not only in politically unstable regions.

**Storing digital evidence.** Keep digital copies on separate encrypted hard drives stored away from the computer. If personal or legal risk is high, keep some copies in a safe location outside the home, while recognising that storing evidence with others may put those people at risk.

**Storing physical evidence.** Keep original documents and scan them to create encrypted digital copies. Environmental samples, especially water samples, degrade quickly and must be sent to a laboratory immediately rather than kept at home.

**Evidence sheet.** Long investigations produce large amounts of evidence whose relevance may not be clear until later. A summary evidence sheet helps manage it. Recommended fields for each piece of evidence: a unique ID (for example "I 0001" for an image or "A 0001" for audio), date created, file name, storage link, type (audio, image, letter, lab test, social media post, etc.), longitude, latitude, general location (company name, village, archive), source name (the person or entity that provided the information), description of what the evidence shows, and notes on accuracy concerns or source weaknesses.


## Glossary

- **Cognitive bias:** A systematic error in thinking that occurs when people process and interpret information, affecting judgement and decision-making.
- **Desktop research:** Research carried out at home or in a workplace, including computer-based research and remote interviews.
- **Evidence:** A wide range of information sources and investigation results that in totality support or contradict a hypothesis. Direct evidence establishes the main fact immediately. Indirect evidence establishes facts from which the main fact can be inferred.
- **Fair use:** A legal term regulating the extent to which copyrighted material can be republished by third parties.
- **Field research:** Evidence gathering that takes place outside the office, including research in natural environments, on-location interviews, and archive visits.
- **Hypothesis:** A supposition or proposed explanation made on the basis of limited evidence as a starting point for further investigation.
- **Metadata:** Information that describes properties of a file (image, document, sound recording, map, etc.). The visible content of an image is its content. The date, location, and device it was taken on are its metadata.
- **Source:** A publication, record, or person that provides information relevant to the investigation or hypothesis.
