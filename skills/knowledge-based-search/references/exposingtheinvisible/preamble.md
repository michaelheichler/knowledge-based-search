The preamble introduces the Kit and positions investigative work as something any curious, motivated person can do. It is structured in four short sections: "So you're here," "Investigation is a mindset," "Risks and rewards," and "This kit is yours." Published April 2019.

## So you're here

The chapter opens by telling the reader directly: "You are already an investigator." The entry point is curiosity, existing skills, or a problem that demands answers. The Kit promises to show how to move from curiosity to investigation to action.

The Kit is described as a collective development by researchers, activists, journalists, developers, and artists who want to share investigative knowledge. Its scope covers a range of purposes: uncovering corruption in local politics, documenting product supply chains, mapping environmental damage, or simply verifying information rather than accepting it at face value.

The guiding metaphor is a puzzle. The pieces are scattered and some may be missing, but you do not need every piece to understand the big picture. The Kit aims to show how to collect relevant pieces, assemble them, and draw meaning from an incomplete picture.

The chapter also addresses readers who have never investigated formally. It points to examples of people who became investigators by accident: amateur photographers, trainspotters, artists, coders, activists, and children researching on the internet. The title "investigator" is not reserved for professionals.

## Investigation is a mindset

The chapter defines an investigation as "a process of collecting and analysing information from different sources and drawing conclusions that address an initial question, problem or assumption." That process builds a body of knowledge about a person, organisation, place, event, or crime.

Investigations serve a clear purpose, which might be seeking the truth, understanding a pattern, or creating or countering a narrative. The key step is turning a body of knowledge into a body of evidence by interpreting information and giving it meaning. Setting purpose, gathering information, and extracting meaning form the central structure of any investigation.

Investigations are explicitly non-linear and iterative. A fixed step-by-step guide cannot capture the process. Paths reach dead ends, requiring investigators to rethink strategies, try alternative methods, or search elsewhere for additional evidence.

The chapter dismisses the stereotype that investigations belong only to detective-style work or professional investigative journalists. It notes that access to information and free research tools have made it easier than ever for a wide range of people to investigate. Activists and civil society groups investigate to inform campaigns and policy. Artists visualise corruption, human rights abuses, and environmental harm. Programmers analyse data manipulated by politicians. Citizen journalists and open-source investigators are advancing digital investigation.

## Risks and rewards

Before starting, an investigator should be cautious and aware. Risks include personal safety concerns, legal risks, dead ends, and situations that exceed one's skills. Anticipating challenges is healthier than meeting them unprepared.

The chapter lists specific areas to consider before beginning: whether to work alone or with others, whether alternative methods exist if the initial plan is too risky, the technology and devices used in the process, and the physical environment in which one works. A key warning: just as traces of events or individuals can be found, an investigation itself leaves traces that can put the investigator, collaborators, sources, or information at risk.

Once potential risks are identified, mitigation strategies can be planned. The chapter acknowledges that not everything can be predicted and that each investigative experience is unique. Regardless of whether the environment is low-tech, restrictive, or simply the open internet, the Kit's tips and techniques are intended to help assess risks and identify safeguards.

## This kit is yours

At a time of data saturation and widespread misinformation, the chapter argues that identifying the sources of problems and countering misleading narratives matters at both a personal and community level. If facts are valuable, then the process of uncovering and documenting them is also valuable.

The chapter acknowledges a gap: journalists, civil society organisations, and professional investigators often cannot keep pace with the speed at which misinformation and corruption spread. This means individuals sometimes need to investigate for themselves. There is now an abundance of publicly available information that can be collected from offline and online sources using accessible tools, and for every technique developed to mislead, there are growing numbers of open-source tools to verify or debunk.

The Kit's specific coverage is listed directly. It includes tools to access websites removed from the internet, content not appearing in search engines, and metadata from videos and images. It covers approaches to investigating companies and public funds, supply chains and product origins, and how to use maps for investigating places and events. It addresses field research preparation, interviewing people, observing places, assessing risk, and collecting and using information safely and ethically. It also covers approaching data from unexpected angles and discovering unfamiliar information resources.

Case studies and first-person examples from contributors are included to illustrate techniques and help readers find parallels with problems in their own communities. Investigators are described as "continuous learners" whose growing arsenal of tactics expands what they can do.

The chapter closes by framing the Kit as a shared resource: yours to use, adapt, share, localise, and contextualise. It is also open to contribution. The Kit is described as an "ever-evolving resource" intended to enable a new community of investigators to emerge. The final word is an invitation: "Join us."
