title: How to Ethically Engage with Vulnerable Sources
source: Exposing the Invisible, The Kit (kit.exposingtheinvisible.org)

Written by Stavros Malichudis, an investigative reporter and editor with over a decade of experience, this guide offers concrete steps for researchers and journalists working with vulnerable sources throughout an entire investigation, from initial design through post-publication.

## What a Vulnerable Source Is

A vulnerable source is any individual whose participation in an investigation carries meaningful personal risk. Examples include undocumented workers and people on the move, people who lost a loved one in an accident or property in a natural disaster, and whistleblowers exposing misconduct inside a company, state agency, or international organization. Their risks include job loss, retaliation, community rejection, and arrest. Engaging these sources is not only an ethical obligation but also produces better research, because vulnerable voices offer perspectives that prominent voices overlook.

## Pre-Reporting Phase

**1. Understand who is vulnerable.** Before approaching anyone, identify who among your potential sources fits into a vulnerable category and think through what specific risks they carry.

**2. Research before making contact.** Gather background on the person and the community they belong to before any approach. Learn relevant cultural customs (greeting practices, hospitality norms) and basic facts of the individual's story. Prior research allows more targeted questions, reduces the chance of forcing a source to re-explain traumatic events unnecessarily, and signals that you took the effort to learn about them. Malichudis describes this practice in his work on the Crete care home story, where 64 residents died in a single year: having already read prior media interviews with grieving relatives, he arrived able to ask more precise questions and handle traumatic memories with greater care.

**3. Plan the initial approach carefully.** The best first contact often comes through a trusted intermediary, such as an NGO you have worked with before, a mutual acquaintance, or a community member who can vouch for your integrity and ethical standards. Checking for common social media connections is one method to find such an introduction. A warm introduction smooths trust-building and gives the source early reason to believe you are credible.

**4. Recognize the power imbalance.** Researchers hold structural power over sources. That imbalance can arise from differences in wealth, legal status, social standing, age, race, ethnicity, nationality, or religion. Keeping this imbalance consciously in mind shapes how you design an investigation and how you treat each interaction.

## Research Phase

**1. Explain yourself and the process fully.** Early in the relationship, and as clearly as possible, cover every major aspect of the project:

- The nature and level of involvement: how many meetings or calls you need, who else (editors, photographers, fact-checkers) may contact them, and over what period.
- Timing and duration: when the work will be published. In some cases, publication should wait until a source has left a housing unit, a job, or any other environment where early exposure would endanger them. Agree on timing early to avoid frustration and surprise. Malichudis cites an investigation into EU-funded asylum housing where a family agreed to cooperate only after it was confirmed the story would run after they had moved out.
- Format: whether the source will be quoted directly, paraphrased, recorded for the final piece, or recorded only for personal note-taking. Informed consent before any recording or photographing is mandatory.
- Publication venue: where and in what language the story will appear.
- Fact-checking: explain that all information must be independently corroborated, that this is not a sign of distrust, and that some details, however personally significant, may not appear in the final piece if they cannot be verified. The Pylos shipwreck investigation is cited as an example: survivors disagreed on whether they waited one hour or half an hour for a rescue vessel, and those discrepancies still required verification despite the extreme suffering involved.

**2. Make clear that sources retain control.** Sources have the right to designate any information as on the record, off the record, or on background. These terms should be explained directly:

- On the record: the source can be named and quoted directly.
- Off the record: the information cannot be published or attributed in any form. This must be mutually agreed before the information is shared.
- On background: the journalist can use and quote the information but cannot name or otherwise identify the source. Attribution uses a generic description such as "a source familiar with the incident" or "an employee on the field."

Sources also have the right to pause or end an interview at any time. Researchers should resist filling silences or pushing forward when a person is visibly distressed.

**3. Give the relationship time.** Trust cannot be forced on a schedule. Malichudis recounts tracking unaccompanied migrant children in Greece in 2019. His editor's advice was to "just hang out with them," spending afternoons without recording equipment or note-taking, letting things unfold naturally. Sources who sense genuine interest in their story often become advocates, introducing researchers to other sources and vouching for their credibility. Investigations with vulnerable sources require patience.

**4. Explain the possible consequences of participation.** It is the researcher's duty to inform sources of risks they may not anticipate. In an investigation of labor exploitation at a Greek call-center company, one source insisted on speaking on the record. Malichudis informed him that doing so might effectively blacklist him in that industry. The source took days to deliberate, then chose to proceed because he planned to work in a different field. The decision was his, but providing the risk context was the researcher's responsibility. When a source shares potentially identifying information, asking "Are you sure you want to share this?" is appropriate and empowering.

**5. Do not create expectations.** Vulnerable sources may be in urgent financial need or waiting on documents that could change their circumstances. Researchers must not offer payment or other material benefits in exchange for information, and must not promise outcomes they cannot control. Be direct that the personal impact of a story on one individual's situation may be limited, while also explaining that the investigation contributes to broader accountability. Practical help that stays within a researcher's actual reach (pointing toward NGOs offering legal support, sharing knowledge of labor inspection processes) is acceptable. Overpromising is not.

**6. Ensure physical safety when meeting in person.** Assess whether being seen with you creates risk for the source, then plan meetings accordingly:

- Be aware of surroundings and confirm you are not being followed.
- Choose discreet indoor locations. If at a cafe, sit facing the entrance and avoid outdoor seating.
- When using a taxi or ride-sharing app, give a destination a short walk from the actual meeting point rather than the exact address.
- Consider leaving your mobile phone behind or disabling GPS.
- Share your safety steps with the source. This builds trust and may produce useful feedback.

**7. Ensure digital safety.** Secure communications are as important as safe physical meetings:

- Keep devices password-protected.
- Use encrypted messaging applications. Signal is specifically recommended for its strong security and is the app favored by researchers and academics.
- Enable auto-delete for messages (available in Signal and WhatsApp).
- Avoid standard phone calls and unencrypted SMS for sensitive communication.
- Use services with end-to-end encryption by default.

Additional resources named in the guide: Security in a Box (securityinabox.org), the Safety First guide on the Kit site, the Exposing the Invisible Risk Assessment guide, and the Holistic Security manual by Tactical Tech.

**8. Handle documents securely.** Physical transfer of documents (postal mail, direct delivery) avoids digital metadata entirely and prevents edit tracking. The guide cites the account in Jessica Bruder and Dale Maharidge's book "Snowden's Box: Trust in the Age of Digital Surveillance": the largest national security leak of the digital era moved through the US Postal Service. When publishing or sharing a document from a source, genuine anonymization is required. Covering text with a black rectangle that can be digitally removed is not sufficient. Metadata (which includes creation date, device information, sender and recipient details) must also be addressed. The guide cites a 2022 example: Greece's National Transparency Authority released a report on illegal pushbacks of asylum seekers with text blacked out to protect source identities, but basic digital design tools were enough to strip those redactions, exposing the identities of the people who had informed the inquiry. Original, unaltered file versions should always be preserved in case of court proceedings or other legal needs.

**9. Show empathy in interviews.** Rapidly shifting between topics, a standard journalistic technique, can re-traumatize vulnerable sources. Plan the sequence of questions before a sensitive interview so that the source moves through difficult territory in a logical order and is asked to recount painful specifics as few times as possible. A parent discussing the death of a child should ideally recount the details of that death only once during the entire conversation.

**10. Keep showing up.** Once a research phase ends, contact with sources often stops abruptly. Sources can feel exploited and sense they were only a means to an end, particularly when their personal circumstances have not changed. The Mati fire (Athens suburbs, summer of 2018, 104 deaths) drew heavy media coverage for up to three months before outlets moved on. Malichudis's colleague Tasos Telloglou of Inside Story continued visiting Mati long after the rest of the media had left. When Malichudis later joined Telloglou to work on follow-up stories, he was immediately trusted by the community solely because of his association with Telloglou, who had earned that trust through persistent presence. The guide contrasts this with "parachute journalism," the extractive practice of arriving, gathering material, and disappearing.

## Post-Publication Phase

**1. Send the story directly to sources.** Do not let them discover publication through social media or third parties. Send it personally, thank them again for their contribution, and explain the story's significance.

**2. Ask for feedback.** After sending the story, ask sources how they experienced it. Do they feel their concerns were framed accurately? Do they find the portrayal of their reality fair? Major changes after publication may not be possible, but the conversation matters.

**3. Remain accessible.** Researchers who vanish after publication make vulnerable sources distrust not only them but journalists in general. Malichudis observes that sources among people on the move in Greece regularly ask him why journalists who previously had coffee with them, entered their homes, and looked at their childhood photos stopped replying to messages after their stories were filed. Sources who have experienced that pattern become harder for all future researchers to reach.
