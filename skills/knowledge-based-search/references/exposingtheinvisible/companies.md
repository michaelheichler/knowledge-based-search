## Purpose

Company research is a component of almost any investigation involving money, influence, or accountability. The chapter teaches how to find out who owns and benefits from a company, how companies conceal their real owners, and where to look for corporate records across jurisdictions. The key premise is that you rarely investigate a company for its own sake. You investigate something a company has done or someone who benefits from it.

## Two Investigation Modes

The chapter identifies two starting points. The first is linking a company to specific actions: a factory may be polluting a river, or a company may be routing profits offshore to avoid tax. The goal is to collect evidence connecting the company to the harm and to the people responsible. The second is linking a company to specific interests: a company is quietly buying historical buildings, or a public official may be profiting through a network of businesses. The goal is to trace who benefits, how, and on what timeline. In practice these two modes often converge.

## What a Company Is

A company is a legal entity created under state or local law. It can own property, enter contracts, and file lawsuits as if it were a person. Two types exist almost everywhere.

Private limited companies (LLC in the United States, GmbH in Germany and Austria, Ltda in Brazil, S.A. de C.V. in Latin America) are privately owned and give their member-owners limited liability for company debts. Stock corporations issue shares that can be traded publicly or privately. Shareholders receive portions of profits, and stock corporations are used for larger, more complex businesses. Most countries also require foreign companies operating locally to register. Most have some form of partnership in which the owners manage the firm and assume its liabilities.

The law firm DLA Piper maintains a comparative document listing company types by country. Wikipedia has a list of legal entity types by country.

## Formation Documents

Because companies are legal persons, they must file a notice of existence with a company registration office (a company register) in their jurisdiction of incorporation. These formation or incorporation documents are always public records, available online or by written or in-person request. Most jurisdictions also require annual reports, though the content varies considerably. Irish companies filing with the Companies Registration Office (cro.ie) include detailed financial statements. Annual filings in many other jurisdictions simply confirm that the company still exists. Many nations require formation documents to list managers, incorporators, or even beneficial owners. Secrecy jurisdictions, including Seychelles, the Isle of Man, Vanuatu, and the State of Delaware in the United States, require none of that and do not disclose it publicly.

## Finding Formation Documents

If you know the jurisdiction, start with the official company register for that country or state. The OCCRP Investigative Dashboard (id.occrp.org) indexes worldwide registers. Wikipedia has a list of company registries. Not all registers are fully online. Some are not online at all. In those cases, call or visit the registrar, ask a trusted local contact, or file a Freedom of Information request. Alaveteli (a project of mySociety) hosts an index of FOI platforms by country. AccessInfo Europe documents FOI rights across more than 120 countries. The FOI Advocates Network (FOIAnet) maintains guides and tutorials on the topic.

Country examples: Mexico allows online consultation of incorporation documents. New Zealand, the UK, the Netherlands (openkvk.nl), and Romania all have online registers. Ireland's register is detailed. Many registries disclose directors, administrators, and secretaries but not shareholders. That is still a lead worth following.

If you must pay fees for historical records such as past ownership or tax reports, journalists and lawyers in some countries can obtain this data for free. Building relationships with them can help.

## Searching Across Jurisdictions

When you do not know where a company is registered, several databases allow searching across many jurisdictions at once.

Free resources: OpenCorporates (opencorporates.com) has officer data for more than 200 million officers in more than 120 jurisdictions. OCCRP's Aleph (aleph.occrp.org) indexes worldwide company registries, court registries, land cadasters, and other official resources, available free or for a fee. The ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database (offshoreleaks.icij.org) contains records from the Panama Papers, Paradise Papers, Luxembourg Leaks, Bahamas Leaks, and other investigations. The database is available for local download.

Paid resources (often accessible through libraries and universities): Bureau van Dijk's Mint Global and Orbis, LexisNexis, Westlaw, Dun and Bradstreet, FactSet Equity Ownership, and Bloomberg Terminal. Large media outlets, research NGOs, and business schools often hold subscriptions.

For publicly traded companies, stock exchanges require disclosure of financial details and major shareholders. In the United States, the SEC requires owners of more than five percent of a company's shares to file disclosure forms, searchable via SEC EDGAR (sec.gov/edgar). European exchanges include the London Stock Exchange Group, Euronext, and Nasdaq Nordic. Yahoo Finance is a free aggregator of analyst reports. Reuters, Nasdaq, and Wall Street Journal Markets offer additional coverage at cost.

## Finding Companies Connected to a Person

OpenCorporates is the best starting point for searching by officer or director name. Because OpenCorporates scrapes or purchases data periodically rather than in real time, verify findings against the official registry. OpenCorporates also indexes official gazettes, which can contain information not yet filed with the registrar, such as changes of director names. For each company found, record all other officers and addresses linked to it, then do a reverse officer search in the official registries of the jurisdictions where those companies are registered. Many registries allow this. Run the same names through OCCRP Aleph and the ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database.

Reverse address searches are equally important. Search every address from incorporation documents in a search engine, in OCCRP Aleph, and in OpenCorporates. A registered address that appears on the filings of hundreds or thousands of companies is a signal. One two-story building in Wilmington, Delaware appears on the incorporation documents of more than 300,000 companies, all using CT Corporation as a registered agent. Also search phone numbers and fax numbers listed in company documents to check whether they connect to other companies.

To search by address in OpenCorporates, a free account is required. After logging in, select the officers radio button and click search (the search bar may be left blank). On the resulting page, click advanced options and select address.

For politicians, ministers, and candidates for elected office, many jurisdictions require wealth declarations or statements of economic interest disclosing assets, income, and debts, including company shares and membership interests. Check whether a subject was active in a jurisdiction requiring such filings. ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer (projects.propublica.org/nonprofits) allows searching for individuals serving on boards of US-based non-profit organizations.

If a company lists a post office box (PO Box) as its address, in the United States the post office will disclose the name of a company-registered PO Box. Call the post office where the box is located and ask for the "box agent." An FOI request to the Postmaster is an alternative. Private mail boxes (UPS, Mail Boxes Etc.) are less cooperative but are required to file information with the Post Office.

Check spouses and close relatives. A subject's spouse, children, or other close relatives may own companies that provide masked benefits to the subject.

## Collecting Background on What a Company Does

Connecting a person to a company is rarely the finding itself. The finding is what the company did. To understand a company's activities, the chapter recommends several steps.

Check the company's website to understand how it describes itself, its products, and its risks. Conduct a news search using Google News (news.google.com) or LexisNexis. The Internet Archive (archive.org) offers full-text searches of millions of texts and television closed-captions. Look for analyst reports on Yahoo Finance or via paid aggregators. These exist mainly for larger companies listed on stock markets. Review lawsuits: court dockets and filings in connection with legal proceedings are often the most candid available source on corporate conduct. Search by company name and by the names of individuals of interest. Employee forums and review sites such as Glassdoor can offer limited perspective. Also consult consumer complaint reports, corporate social responsibility reports, environmental impact reports, and public procurement databases.

## Beneficial Owners

A beneficial owner is the person who financially benefits from a company regardless of whether they hold legal title. This is the person most important to identify in company-related investigations. The chapter emphasizes this because beneficial owners are often deliberately hidden.

Beneficial ownership is concealed in several ways.

Secrecy jurisdictions do not require disclosure and may not list beneficial owners in public records at all. Examples include Seychelles, the Isle of Man, Vanuatu, the State of Delaware, Nevada, Panama, and Cyprus. The Financial Secrecy Index published by the Tax Justice Network (fsi.taxjustice.net) ranks jurisdictions by secrecy level and the scale of their offshore financial activity. The United States ranks second most secretive nationally. Nevada is one of its most secretive states.

Nominee or proxy directors are real persons who appear in official documents as directors but act on behalf of true owners. In OCCRP's Proxy Platform investigation, billions of euros circulated through Eastern Europe in an illegal parallel system built on phantom companies and proxy persons, many of whom were unaware their names appeared in official documents. A director appearing across many unrelated companies is a red flag.

Formation agents are law or accounting firms that register companies, file tax returns, and sometimes act as listed directors for clients whose identities they conceal. They hold no real stake in the companies they help create. OCCRP's Offshore Crime Inc. investigation (2010) documented a New Zealand-based family, the Taylors, who operated business registry firms from the 1960s onward and helped set up more than 2,500 offshore companies. Some of those companies were used by Russian crime networks, the Sinaloa cartel, and Hezbollah. The investigation relied substantially on open public records, supplemented by interviews and undercover reporting. The New Zealand government closed the Taylors' firms after the reporting, though they later re-emerged elsewhere.

Shelf companies are companies pre-registered by law or accounting firms and sold as ready-made products to buyers who want to avoid the registration process. They are maintained in good standing but conduct no actual business. They are not illicit by nature but can be used to obscure a beneficial owner or to give a company a fictitiously longer operating history. In Nevada, one firm sold shelf companies with stand-in directors, and at least one was used as a front for an illegal payday loan operation run by James Fontano and partners. Fontano acted as a listed director for hundreds of companies to help clients conceal themselves from public view.

Shell companies are inactive companies with no significant business activity, no staff, and no physical office, formed to hold and transfer assets such as money, real estate, yachts, or art. Like shelf companies, they are not illicit by nature. Coming across one is a reason to investigate further, not a finding of wrongdoing.

Resources for tracing beneficial owners include OCCRP Aleph, the ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database, the Financial Action Task Force guidance on beneficial ownership (fatf-gafi.org), Tax Justice Network materials, the OECD Beneficial Ownership Toolkit, and Transparency International's Anti-Corruption Glossary (transparency.org/glossary/term/beneficial_ownership).

## Practical Notes

The chapter repeatedly stresses staying organized: keep records of information uncovered, do not follow side tracks too far from the main purpose, and strengthen evidence by cross-checking across multiple databases. No single database is comprehensive or current. The method is to run multiple search cycles across platforms and official registries to fill gaps. Offshore companies are often used to mask ownership, launder money, or evade taxes, but many are simply conducting ordinary activities with no hidden purpose. Do not draw conclusions before verifying. Successful investigations do not always stop wrongdoing permanently. Persistence and follow-up are part of the work.

## Key Terms

- **Beneficial owner**: person who financially benefits from a company, regardless of legal title.
- **Secrecy jurisdiction**: country or territory that does not require disclosure of beneficial owners or managers, often combined with low or no taxation.
- **Formation agent**: firm that registers companies and acts as a front for true owners.
- **Proxy person / nominee director**: individual who appears in documents as a director but acts for a hidden owner.
- **Shelf company**: pre-registered inactive company sold as a ready-made product.
- **Shell company**: inactive company formed to hold and transfer assets, with no staff or operations.
- **Reverse search**: searching a database by a non-primary field, for example searching a company registry by officer name rather than company name.
- **Wealth declaration**: mandatory financial disclosure statement filed by politicians, ministers, or candidates, listing assets, income, and debts.
- **Official gazette**: government publication that can contain corporate changes not yet filed with the registrar.
