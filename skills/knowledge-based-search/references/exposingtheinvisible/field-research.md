## Overview

This chapter, written by Mario Rautner, covers how to plan, run, and evaluate a field investigation safely and effectively. Field research is defined as any information collection or verification that requires leaving your home or workplace: interviewing witnesses, observing events, collecting environmental samples (water, mud, plants), or taking photographs and recordings in support of an investigation.

Field research is resource-intensive. While digital research can move quickly, field investigation requires arranging transport, conducting the work on site, and returning home to analyze results. A trip may take half a day or several months. For this reason, the chapter advises investigators to exhaust all desktop research and remote leads before going into the field.

Physical evidence gathered in the field (film, video, sound recordings) is often harder to manipulate than information gathered online and can communicate the urgency behind an issue more powerfully. The chapter illustrates its guidance through two hypothetical case studies based on real activities.

Example 1 involves a team investigating herbicide contamination of drinking water in soy-growing regions of Argentina. Doctors reported elevated cancer rates and birth defects, and researchers suspected the herbicide (applied by planes over large areas) was reaching community groundwater. The field research involves collecting water and plant samples, sending them to an accredited laboratory, and conducting filmed interviews with affected community members.

Example 2 involves an investigator trying to identify and interview a confidential source inside a local council suspected of awarding a public renovation tender improperly to a family member of a councilor. The goal is to obtain documents and testimony that can establish evidence of corruption.

## Why field research is done

Field research can accomplish things that desktop research cannot. Common purposes include:

- Corroborating satellite images or other digital material with physical evidence
- Collecting environmental samples (water, soil, air)
- Obtaining, reviewing, or copying documents not available remotely
- Meeting and interviewing sources and witnesses in their own environment
- Identifying new sources
- Expanding understanding of evidence already gathered
- Confirming or refuting an initial hypothesis or existing evidence
- Verifying product information such as barcodes or manufacturer names for supply chain research
- Gathering photos, video, or audio for documentaries or articles

Physical samples and recordings often communicate urgency better than data alone. The chapter uses oil polluting the ocean as an example: satellite images and scientific data can document it, but physical evidence of pollution affecting marine life is typically more persuasive.

## Skills field research requires

Good field research draws on curiosity, adaptability, patience, and caution. Four specific abilities matter:

- Adaptability to rapidly changing circumstances, including under stress
- Understanding of risks, and the ability to de-escalate or soften difficult encounters (the chapter gives the example of a security guard confrontation that could be turned into an opportunity to gather additional information)
- Realistic self-assessment of capabilities, including willingness to admit gaps in skill and postpone work that exceeds current ability (a "heroic attitude" is described as counterproductive and risk-increasing)
- The ability to blend in, whether with interviewees or in difficult-to-access locations

## The research cycle

All field research follows four phases: plan, act, analyze, and reflect.

In desk research these phases often blur together. In field research they are sharply defined, because investigators are frequently outside familiar environments.

Planning covers objectives, logistics, and risks and should happen well before departure. Acting is the fieldwork itself. Analyzing means reviewing all videos, images, recordings, and notes after returning. Reflecting means comparing results to original objectives, which usually reveals new questions and starts a new cycle. The chapter notes that research in practice looks less like a clean loop and more like a process that repeatedly restarts planning at different scales.

## Planning

### Familiarization with location

Before going, study the geography of the target location using map applications (OpenStreetMap, Google Maps, Google Earth, OsmAnd). Check internet forums and specialized travel sites for location-specific information. Do not skip preparation even for locations you already know, because the investigation itself may create new conditions or risks.

### SMART objectives

Research objectives should be SMART: Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Realistic, and Timebound.

Examples from the text: "collect photographic evidence of a coal vessel arriving in a seaport in a few days" and "meet up with and obtain documents from a confidential source helping you to investigate suspicious decisions your local council has made on a tender for a building project."

Clear objectives help focus activities, reduce wasted resources, break the investigation into discrete steps, and guide the choice of methodology.

For Example 1, the objectives are:
- Obtain 50 water samples from public and private groundwater wells over three weeks following high herbicide use
- Follow scientific sampling protocols and ship samples to an accredited lab
- Carry out and film interviews with affected community members

For Example 2, the objective is to identify someone at the council who is involved in project tenders and persuade them to share information about the school renovation tender.

### Activity planning

For complex projects, write down the strategy, the questions to answer, expected challenges, possible solutions, team responsibilities, anticipated expenses, and a timeline. There is no required format.

For the herbicide project, planning takes many months. Key components include:
- Identifying communities relying on groundwater (through desktop research and phone calls)
- Arranging battery-powered refrigeration in a motorhome to transport cooled samples
- Selecting an accredited laboratory and obtaining price quotes for 50 samples
- Learning sampling protocols from the laboratory
- Identifying shipping companies equipped to handle scientific samples
- Compiling equipment lists (cameras, SD cards, spare batteries, microphones, car chargers, GPS)
- Developing a detailed questionnaire so every interviewee is asked the same questions
- Preparing release/consent forms

The chapter cites the CCME "Protocols Manual for water quality sampling in Canada" as an example protocol document and the WHO consent form template as a model for consent forms.

For the council source project, key tasks include:
- Using LinkedIn, Twitter, and other social media (accessed via Tor and private settings) to identify relevant council staff without being tracked
- Contacting selected people through personal rather than work channels
- Choosing a discreet interview location away from the source's workplace
- Ensuring recording equipment works and that documents can be photographed (a smartphone is often sufficient)
- Obtaining written or recorded consent before recording
- Researching whether tender documents are obtainable through Freedom of Information requests

The chapter cites Nieman Lab's 12 principles for protecting confidential sources as a reference on source protection.

### Risk assessment

Field research carries more physical risk than desk work. The excitement of being in the field can impair judgment. A simple daily checklist of command-phrased items (each no more than one sentence: "do this," "pack that") helps maintain consistent preparation.

Risk assessments should identify and rate risks across nine categories:

- Environmental risks: weather forecast, equipment tolerance to rain or extreme heat
- Terrain-related risks: appropriate footwear and clothing, risk of falling, ability to operate cameras in darkness
- Research goal risks: handling hazardous materials, circumstances that could prevent completing tasks
- Legal risks: whether planned activities are treated as criminal in that context
- Personal safety risks: potential confrontations with authorities, security guards, or workers
- Personal identity and health risks: risks tied to citizenship, race, health conditions, or known allergies
- Logistical risks: suitability of travel and accommodation to terrain and context
- Evidence and source risks: protecting collected evidence with encrypted storage, assessing risks to whistleblowers
- Family and friends risks: when investigating sensitive issues, people or organisations with a lot to lose may take an interest in your personal environment, putting family and friends at risk

For each risk, note both the severity (on a scale of 1 to 5) and the likelihood. These are not the same thing. A mitigation plan should address how to prevent, respond to, and resolve each identified problem. If personal or legal risks are severe and cannot be adequately mitigated, the chapter recommends not proceeding with the fieldwork at all.

The chapter references Tactical Tech's Holistic Security guide (holistic-security.tacticaltech.org), particularly the Prepare chapter, and the UNDP's "Risk Management for NGOs" as frameworks for risk assessment.

For Example 1, risks and mitigations include:
- Confrontation with angry farmers or industry representatives (severity 2, mitigated by conducting the work openly without a cover story and using de-escalation techniques, with Right Response's de-escalation guide cited as a resource)
- Theft or loss of equipment or data (severity 4, mitigated by carrying minimal gear, making cloud backups, avoiding high-risk travel, and not traveling alone)
- Equipment failure in remote areas (severity 4, mitigated by carrying backups for all equipment)
- Spoiled, broken, or contaminated samples (severity 2, mitigated by following sampling protocol closely, filming each sample as it is collected, and maintaining a chain-of-custody record)
- Heavy rain or drought affecting sampling results (severity 2, mitigated by consulting the lab and scientists about timing and monitoring local weather patterns)

For Example 2, risks center on the source:
- Risk to their job (severity 1, addressed by full transparency about intentions and letting the source decide the extent of their involvement)
- Risk of being seen passing information (severity 3, addressed by choosing meeting places distant from their workplace and using encrypted communication via Wire or Signal)
- Risk that the source is unwilling or unable to provide the needed information (severity 5, addressed by identifying alternative sources and other evidence avenues)

When sources include confidential or vulnerable people, the risk assessment must include the risks they face, and those risks must be discussed with them.

## Before you go

### Practice interviews

If interviewing is part of the research, simulate it with people in similar situations before going to the real location. If the work will take place near a port, public building, or construction site, test the approach at a comparable but safer location. Role-play various scenarios with a trusted collaborator.

### Practice your methodology

The same applies to specific techniques. If you have never taken environmental samples, used a drone or hidden camera, or conducted supply chain fieldwork, practice in unrelated, low-risk settings before the actual investigation.

### Simulate what could go wrong

Review the risk management plan and act out problem scenarios with trusted people. One person can play an angry security guard. If working alone, practice with the trusted friend who will also serve as your emergency contact.

### Ensure legality

Legal rules differ by country and sometimes by state within a country. Secretly recording a conversation can be legal in one jurisdiction and illegal in another. Research the rules on photography, video recording, and entry to public and private property. Seek legal advice if uncertain. Note the difference between what is actually illegal and what is likely to attract law enforcement attention, since legal proceedings can take years and derail an investigation even if you are eventually cleared. Journalists, NGO staff, and other researchers active in your area of interest can often provide both legal guidance and lawyer referrals.

### Cover stories

A cover story is a fabricated reason, justification, or persona used during field research. Cover stories range from simple explanations to elaborate setups with backstories that can withstand online verification. The chapter explicitly recommends that beginners avoid cover stories. Effective ones require experience and preparation.

Five elements of a good cover story:

- Confidence: this is the most important element, and determines how much an investigator can achieve with a given story.
- Practice: do not attempt complicated cover stories without experience in simpler ones. There is a significant learning curve.
- Plausibility: the story must make sense in context. A "birdwatcher" cover at a farm is plausible. The same cover at an industrial plant may not be.
- Corroborating evidence: carry materials that support the story. A birdwatcher might carry a local bird guidebook and have photos of local birds on the camera. A businessperson should look like one.
- Background knowledge: know enough about the role to use appropriate terms consistently. Ignorance can also serve as a cover in some contexts, allowing the investigator to ask for help and thereby obtain relevant information.

If questioned by law enforcement or border control, check whether lying to officials is illegal in that jurisdiction. When working in a group, agree in advance on the conditions for breaking cover to prevent one person's decision from exposing the rest.

### Plan your entry and exit

Exit planning is more important than entry planning, because that is when things are most likely to go wrong. Know the surrounding area before arriving. Use GPS and mapping tools in advance. Apps such as OpenStreetMap, Google Maps, Google Earth, and OsmAnd are useful, including in remote or poorly mapped areas. Learn how to use geographic coordinates before departure.

If working in a team, agree on one or two meeting points within walking distance of the research site in case the group is separated. If working alone, arrange a contact who will take agreed-upon steps if you fail to check in at prearranged times.

## In the field

### Technology

Equipment matters, but only if you know how to use it. In many field situations there is only one opportunity to capture evidence. Practice with every device before the investigation. Avoid bringing more technology than necessary.

Common tools:

Smartphones can serve as video and photo camera, sound recorder, GPS device, mapping tool, and communication device. Bring at least one spare power bank. Limitations include no optical zoom, poor performance in low or no light, inability to record sound at a distance, and inability to communicate without a network signal. The app Camera V (by Guardian Project and Witness) captures and encrypts photo and video proof on a smartphone or tablet while preserving metadata including location, time, and device information.

Superzoom cameras (consumer cameras with long-range lenses) can record license plates and other details from a great distance, reducing the risk of getting too close to a subject. Manufacturers include Canon, Nikon, and Panasonic. Prices range from around 300 to over 1,500 USD, with used equipment available more cheaply. Buy a camera with a GPS sensor and always enable geolocation. A tripod is needed to avoid blur in video and stills. Superzoom cameras can replace binoculars: the chapter links a video demonstrating 60x zoom capability.

Drones are increasingly affordable and useful for aerial views of large-scale environmental issues such as logging, water pollution, or fire damage. They are loud and conspicuous. Check local no-fly zones, understand local legal restrictions, and fly high enough not to attract easy attention.

Balloon and kite-mounted cameras are quieter alternatives to drones, particularly in populated areas. Public Lab (publiclab.org) provides resources on kite and balloon aerial photography and other field sampling methods.

Two-way radios (walkie-talkies) are useful when there is no cell reception or when operating a phone is impractical. They require only one button to operate once set up. Use them with headphones. Range degrades quickly in dense urban areas and forests. Walkie-talkie apps like Zello use cellular or internet networks and are not substitutes for actual radios.

Handheld GPS devices are rugged, waterproof, and durable. A useful technique is to photograph the GPS device displaying its coordinates next to the subject of interest. This supplements GPS metadata embedded in photos, which can otherwise be manipulated.

Always carry multiple charged spare batteries for every device.

Some investigations require specialized equipment: mobile chemical testing kits, air pollution monitors, environmental sampling kits, miniature cameras, hidden cameras, or night vision cameras. Choose gear to match the investigation and avoid over-equipping.

### Photo, video, and audio evidence

Photographic and video evidence is powerful but can be manipulated. Common subjects include building exteriors and interiors, supply chain movements, events, people, animals, documents, products, and documentation of your own activities to prove what you have done.

For sound recordings, built-in microphones in phones and cameras are often insufficient. External microphones serve different purposes: some pick up sound at a distance, some work best at close range, and some cancel background noise. NPR Training's article "Which Mic Should I Use" is cited for guidance on selecting microphones.

Enable timestamps and geolocation on all devices. Ensure that time zones are correctly set so metadata accurately reflects when and where evidence was captured.

Image metadata can reveal the camera used, linking it to other photos taken with the same device, and can expose a home address if photos taken there are shared. Preserve metadata as evidence but be careful about where and how images are distributed.

### Witness statements

Witness statements are rarely fully impartial and must be corroborated with evidence from additional, independent sources. Reasons statements may be unreliable:

- Recollection can be inaccurate, deliberately or not
- PR staff and spin doctors may try to steer you in particular directions
- Subjects of investigation may lie or withhold information to protect their reputations

The more independent witnesses who corroborate one another, the closer the result is to the truth. Filmed statements are stronger evidence than audio alone. Audio is stronger than written notes taken afterward, but written notes are still worth taking when recording is not possible.

### Documents

Documents obtained during field research must be handled carefully. Copies from libraries, archives, or Freedom of Information requests can be used freely. Removing documents from buildings may have significant legal consequences depending on jurisdiction. In some jurisdictions, material left as trash is considered abandoned property and may be taken. Photographing documents is weaker evidence than possessing originals. Research these legal rules during planning.

### Environmental samples

If sampling is part of the investigation, follow relevant protocols closely. Failure to follow protocol can degrade sample quality or contaminate samples, producing false results. Analysis must be done in accredited laboratories. Consult the chosen laboratory in advance for guidance on field best practices.

### Safety in the field

Dedicate at least one hour of safety research for each day of planned fieldwork. In addition to the pre-departure risk assessment, maintain these habits throughout the investigation:

Collaboration: avoid doing all but the most basic research alone. Working in a team adds a safety layer.

Behavior code: when working with locals or a mixed team, agree on a common-sense code of behavior and a model for mediating disagreements before tensions arise.

Check-ins: ensure someone back home knows your location and activities. Check in at least once a day. Location-sharing apps can substitute but may pose risks under surveillance.

Duress code: agree in advance on a word, phrase, or name that signals distress when used in normal communication. It must sound benign to outsiders. Share it with the check-in contact.

Emergency reaction: agree with the check-in contact on the steps they should take if you miss a check-in or signal distress.

Identification: prepare materials to prove who you are. For non-undercover work, bring past project samples or an accreditation letter. Carry copies of citizenship documents and travel itinerary in non-digital form in case devices are lost. Show identifying documents only when trust-building requires it and be selective, because some people may be hostile to an investigator.

Information safety: use dummy memory cards to display harmless material if questioned. Store actual evidence on encrypted storage devices (USB, external drive, or an encrypted cloud folder). Keep multiple copies in different locations and with trusted contacts.

Health: plan for personal health needs and allergies. Inform collaborators and make emergency plans.

Travel insurance: use providers with coverage for investigators, journalists, and researchers. Coverage should include lost items, travel disruptions, medical evacuation, non-medical evacuation to a secure location or embassy, and repatriation.

For Example 1, the scientific requirements for temperature control meant the team used a motorhome with adequate refrigeration. Every sample was filmed and photographed as it was collected. GPS coordinates of each well were recorded. Each sample was assigned a number linked to a database containing GPS coordinates, collection time, on-site test results (pH, temperature), and background information about the site. Community interviews were recorded and linked to the corresponding samples.

## When you get home

Organize all raw material immediately after returning, while memory is fresh. Tasks include copying videos and photos, downloading GPS coordinates, scanning documents, and shipping environmental samples to labs.

Create a database that lists images with file names, recording locations, GPS coordinates, and descriptions of content. This process is called data logging. For video, log timestamps with descriptions of key moments. For audio, transcribe key passages. Without this step, archives quickly become disordered and evidence can be difficult to locate.

Geolocated photos can be imported into Google Earth using software such as Geosetter.

On evidence integrity: back up everything in a format that preserves both data and metadata, such as EXIF (Exchangeable Image File Format). Consider setting media or hardware partitions to read-only so backups cannot be altered or deleted accidentally or intentionally. Do not alter the EXIF or other metadata of original images or documents. Altering this information damages the accuracy and credibility of the evidence.

Notes, databases, and time logs from data logging often contain sensitive information. Develop processes for encryption, archiving, storage, and destruction where needed, especially after the investigation concludes, to protect collaborators and sources.

For Example 1, the team took hundreds of photos, each linked to a specific water sample and GPS location. Many hours of interview footage were transcribed into a database. Cooling requirements meant the team had to organize multiple shipments of samples to the laboratory during the fieldwork itself.

For Example 2, the confidential source provided printed tender documents. The investigator needed to determine whether those documents could be traced back to the source, including through printer logs at the council and through Machine Identification Codes. Machine Identification Codes are small yellow dots (or other patterns) embedded by printers and copy machines on documents they process. They are not visible to the naked eye but can be identified under scrutiny, allowing forensic identification of the specific device. A BBC article is cited in the text explaining how these codes can expose whistleblowers.

## What next

After fieldwork, review whether the research objective was achieved and whether conclusions can be drawn. It may be necessary to repeat the research, adjust strategies, set a more realistic goal, or look for different kinds of evidence.

The chapter states plainly that it is normal not to succeed on the first attempt. Some reasons are outside your control (a source did not appear, weather intervened). Most of the time, lessons can be extracted and applied to the next cycle. Reflection points may include the need to improve a cover story, build confidence, upgrade equipment, or develop specific skills. Teams should be willing to criticize each other's work honestly.

For Example 1, all water samples tested negative for the herbicide, contrary to expectations and to the existing scientific literature. The reasons were unclear, possibly sample degradation, weather effects, or an absence of recent spraying. The community interviews nonetheless documented consistent and serious concerns about large-scale herbicide use. Some years later the herbicide was declared illegal in many countries, including some where the research took place, based on evidence of its cancer risk. The chapter presents this as an outcome where the field research contributed to a larger picture even without a definitive laboratory result.

For Example 2, the investigator was able to meet the potential source but the evidence obtained was too weak to prove corruption. Given the small number of people with access to the relevant information, the risk to the whistleblower was judged too high to continue along this line. The chapter presents this as a legitimate outcome: the investigator must now identify alternative approaches to the evidence.

## Glossary

**Backstory:** An explanation for what you are doing and why. Can be truthful or partially fabricated. Provides a plausible background supporting a cover story.

**Chain of custody:** A documented, chronological record of how material (documents, samples, other evidence) is handled and by whom, including every transfer of control and every analysis. Gaps in the chain of custody undermine the trustworthiness of evidence.

**Cover story:** Similar to a backstory but generally fabricated or distorted. May include a made-up persona or narrative. A good cover story has a plausible backstory.

**Corroborate:** Confirm and verify information from multiple independent sources.

**Corroborating evidence:** Additional evidence that supports other evidence already obtained.

**Data logging:** Keeping collected information organized in a database with details such as file names, contents, source, time obtained, image descriptions, GPS coordinates, and timestamps.

**De-escalation:** Using behavior and language (including body language) to defuse the aggressive behavior of others.

**Duress code:** A word, phrase, or sentence that signals distress. It sounds benign to outsiders but communicates to a trusted contact that something is wrong. It functions as a safeword rather than a code word.

**Environmental sample:** A sample of material collected from an environmental source such as water, air, or soil.

**Freedom of Information (FOI) legislation:** Laws governing citizens' access to information and specifying what is considered public interest information and what may be withheld. Not all countries have or apply FOI legislation.

**Freedom of Information request:** The formal process of requesting access to information from public institutions or companies under FOI legislation.

**Field research / field investigation:** Any research requiring presence at a specific location or a hands-on approach outside the investigator's normal workspace.

**GPS:** A US system of navigational satellites allowing users to determine their position on earth.

**Handheld GPS device:** A single-purpose device providing precise location information, sometimes capable of mapping a physical area. Typically rugged and waterproof.

**Jurisdiction:** A geographic area governed by a specific legal authority (local, regional, or national government).

**Machine Identification Codes:** Watermarks in the form of small yellow dots (or other patterns) left by printers and copiers on every document they process. Not visible to the naked eye. Allow forensic identification of the device that produced the document, which can expose whistleblowers.

**Metadata:** Information describing the properties of a file. For an image, content is the visible picture and metadata includes date, time, location, and device.

**Release/consent form:** A form granting permission to use information or media from an interview, or documenting that the researcher explained the details and possible risks of participation to the subject.

**Topographical map:** A map showing the altitude of the landscape, useful for navigation in wilderness or non-urban areas.
