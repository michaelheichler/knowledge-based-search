Authors: Nuria Tesón, Ankita Anand, Jess Lempit, Megha Rajagopalan.

This chapter covers long-term source relationships: how to build them, maintain them, protect them, and end them when necessary. It distinguishes sources (people who provide information over time, not necessarily interviewed on the record) from one-off interviewees, though it notes that the general guidance on identifying sources, conducting background research, assessing bias, reaching out, and communicating safely in the Interviews section of The Kit applies to both.

## Creating an agenda and database

An investigator needs a standing network of contacts, not just people relevant to a single story. This network is sometimes called an agenda or database: a set of sources who can provide information and tip-offs over time.

There are two main ways to grow it. First, seek out contacts deliberately by beginning with informal interaction, a coffee, or a visit to someone's workplace. Second, convert interviewees into long-term sources by staying in touch after a specific interview or investigation ends.

Contacts should be stored safely and in an organised way. A topic-specific list might include environmentalists, lawyers, political activists, human rights researchers, and officials. The chapter warns against discarding lower-ranking people: they may rise to positions of responsibility or gain proximity to those in power, which makes them useful later. Security forces can also be sources, but they require especially high safety measures around confidentiality.

If your reputation grows, people may approach you with information unsolicited. In those cases you should still establish their reliability and biases, ask about their intentions, and set clear boundaries for the relationship.

## Building trust

Past work is the best trust-building tool. If previous investigations show that you protected your sources' privacy and safety, new contacts are more likely to cooperate. The chapter emphasises being genuine and acknowledging the risk sources take by maintaining a relationship with you. Building a reputation as someone who cares about the people involved matters as much as building a reputation for investigative skill.

## Keeping contact: the Law of the Three Calls

The chapter names a specific principle called the law of the three calls. Rather than contacting sources only when you need something, you maintain regular, low-pressure contact.

The three calls work as follows:

1. A message checking how the source is doing, with no request for information.
2. A follow-up to ask about their work and life and suggest meeting for coffee, again with no investigative purpose.
3. A call when you actually need their help for an investigation. By this point the relationship is warm enough that the request feels natural.

The result is that sources may also initiate contact on their own when they have something relevant, because you are not associated only with needing things.

## Is your source compromised?

Past trustworthiness does not guarantee future trustworthiness. The chapter states that you must assess a source's credibility and reliability each time you meet them, not only before the first meeting. The specific factors that matter include who the source is, the context they are operating in, and changes in their circumstances.

A concrete example: a source who was helping in one country may later have been threatened, or their family or organisation may have been pressured to report on you. Warning signs include a sudden increase in questions about your personal details (where you live, who your partner is, who gave you a particular piece of evidence). When behaviour changes in that way, the source's status as reliable should be treated as changed.

## Protecting sources

Confidentiality is described as the key principle. Revealing a source's identity compromises both the source and the relationship.

The relationship must be built on mutual trust, and sources need to know you will not deceive them. The chapter notes that investigators have the right to protect sources even when asked to identify them by a judge, but this right varies by country. In the United States specifically, civil and criminal courts can issue subpoenas requiring disclosure of confidential source identities. An investigator who refuses may face fines or jail time. Knowing the law in your own country and the country you are working in is therefore essential.

Practical protection measures include using encrypted communications and password-protecting contact databases. The chapter directs readers to the digital security advice in the Interviews section for guidance on protecting devices, communications, and information from unwanted access.

A safety note in the chapter states that sources will often expect the investigator to know how to safeguard communications and interview transcripts, and that the source is typically the person most at risk if a mistake is made.

## Maintaining boundaries

Long-term relationships with sources can blur the line between professional and personal. The chapter describes two risks that result from this.

First, if the relationship becomes a friendship, the investigator may become biased and lose objectivity and credibility. Second, a source may share useful information while talking as a friend rather than as an informant. In that case, the investigator must ask directly whether the information can be used and must not use it without the source's consent. The chapter also warns that the source may feel pressured to agree, so the investigator should consider the risks and not use their position of power to extract information that was shared in confidence.

The same dynamic can run in reverse: a source who has become a close friend may feel entitled to ask the investigator to do something uncomfortable, or may use the closeness to mislead or manipulate.

Practical guidance on managing this:

- Avoid revealing personal details about your own life.
- Be friendly but professional.
- Know the difference between someone trusting you because of shared friendship and someone trusting you because of your work.
- Continue to verify whether close sources are still reliable.

The chapter also states that investigators have the right to cut a source off, even one who has helped in the past. Discomfort, boundary violations, or risk to the investigator, their organisation, or their work are sufficient reasons. Women investigators are noted as being more vulnerable to unwanted advances that result from sources misreading professional interest as personal.

## Risk assessment

Risk should be assessed continuously whenever you are dealing with sources. The chapter gives a set of practices.

For communications: use encrypted channels whenever possible. If you must use a phone that is not secure, establish a code with the source to signal that the call may be monitored, and avoid disclosing the subject matter or meeting location on that call.

For in-person meetings: avoid locations you visit frequently or places where either of you might be recognised by people you know.

For identity protection: consider using pseudonyms to hide source identities. Keep files encrypted.

For high-risk situations: prepare a proof of life document. This is a document containing confidential information and sometimes passwords, held by someone you fully trust, so that person can determine whether you are still alive based on your online activity or lack of it. The chapter points to Rory Peck Trust resources on proof of life notes and templates for further guidance.

## Glossary

The chapter defines four terms:

- **Agenda/database**: a network of sources you can rely on for information and tip-offs over time.
- **Anonymous source**: a source whose information you publish without naming them, in a way that nobody can link the information back to them.
- **Encrypted communication**: communication through applications or email that use encoded information, readable only by someone with the correct password or encryption key.
- **Interview subject/interviewee**: the person you are interviewing.
- **Proof of life**: a document with confidential information and sometimes passwords, held by a trusted person, used to determine whether the investigator is still alive.
