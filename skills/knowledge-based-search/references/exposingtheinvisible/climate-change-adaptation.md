Written by Leopold Salzenstein and published in June 2023 (based on a 2021 article by the same author), this chapter argues that climate disasters are political events shaped by human decisions, not simply natural phenomena. It lays out a framework for investigators and journalists to scrutinize how governments, companies, and institutions prepare for and respond to climate hazards, rather than focusing only on emissions reduction.

## The case for investigating adaptation

Most climate journalism concentrates on cutting greenhouse gas emissions. The chapter argues this is insufficient. The climate crisis is already here. The 2021 IPCC Sixth Assessment Report confirmed, using more than 14,000 peer-reviewed studies, that human responsibility for warming the planet is "unequivocal." The World Meteorological Organization reported in 2023 that droughts, floods, and heatwaves affected communities on every continent, and that Antarctic sea ice fell to its lowest extent on record.

Even with aggressive cuts to emissions, disasters will grow stronger and more frequent. This means that climate change adaptation (decisions and actions taken to prepare for and respond to current and future climate impacts) must accompany mitigation. Some scholars treat mitigation itself as a subset of adaptation, since reducing emissions limits future exposure to hazards.

The chapter identifies a specific and underexplored investigative angle: examining whether communities are being informed, involved, and protected.

Key investigative questions the chapter poses:
- What are states, cities, international organizations, and corporations doing to prepare for climate impacts?
- Who makes these decisions?
- Who is left behind, and why?

## Disasters are political events

The chapter's central claim is that disasters are not natural. They arise at the intersection of three factors:

1. Exposure to a hazard (the extent to which a community is in the path of storms, floods, fires, heatwaves, earthquakes, etc.)
2. Local vulnerabilities (physical, social, economic, and environmental conditions that make a context susceptible to damage)
3. Coping capacity (the ability of individuals, communities, and institutions to respond and recover)

The contrast between Haiti and Chile illustrates this. A 7.1 magnitude earthquake struck Haiti on 12 January 2010, killing approximately 220,000 people. Six weeks later, a stronger quake (8.8 magnitude), followed by a tsunami, struck Chile and caused roughly 500 deaths. The difference was not the force of the earthquake, but each country's vulnerability and coping capacity, both of which are products of political decisions and power relations.

Haiti's vulnerability to earthquakes and cyclones is rooted in its history of "slavery, revolution, debt, deforestation, corruption, exploitation and violence," per historian Alex von Tunzelmann. A Nature study attributed a large share of the Haiti earthquake deaths to the country's lack of earthquake-resistant construction.

A 2007 peer-reviewed study found that corruption in a country's public sector correlates positively with the risk of dying from an earthquake. The study authors' summary: "earthquakes don't kill people, collapsing buildings do."

Similarly, the February 2023 7.8 and 7.5 magnitude earthquakes in Turkey and Syria damaged over 100,000 buildings, caused more than 10,000 collapses, and killed more than 50,000 people, with building quality a central factor.

A storm in the middle of a desert causes no disaster, because a disaster requires the destruction of something we value: human life, livelihoods, property. While hazards may be natural, disasters rarely are.

## Investigating exposure to climate hazards

Climate hazards include hydro-meteorological events (heavy rainfall, floods, droughts, storms, heatwaves, cold spells, wildfire) and geological hazards (earthquakes, landslides, tsunamis, volcanoes), which are also influenced by climate change. Biological hazards such as epidemics and pests are shaped by environmental changes including global warming.

The relation between climate change and specific hazards is not straightforward. Climate change typically modifies the intensity or frequency of a hazard rather than causing it directly. The IPCC AR6 report found that climate change has increased the number of heatwaves and worsened intense rains and extreme storms, while reducing the frequency of cold spells. A video investigation by Le Monde found that while climate change did not necessarily increase the overall number of fires globally, it made fires more intense and violent.

Two investigative tools for analysing exposure:

Climate scenarios predict changes in the atmosphere, land, and oceans given a certain level of greenhouse gas emissions. They can be used to investigate future risks such as sea level rise or climate-driven migration. Investigators typically cannot build their own climate models and should collaborate with scientists and weather agencies.

Attribution studies (also called "extreme event attribution") are scientific analyses of how a specific past disaster was influenced by climate change. As of August 2022, scientists had published more than 400 peer-reviewed attribution studies. Examples include a study showing climate change made the 2018 Sweden heatwave more likely, and one finding climate change increased Puerto Rico's exposure to extreme hurricanes such as Hurricane Maria in 2017. Overall, Carbon Brief found that 70% of extreme weather events analysed were made more likely or more severe by climate change.

The chapter cautions that focusing only on exposure has limits. Exposure-only coverage can make disasters seem inevitable and apolitical, shifting focus away from accountability. It can also contribute to climate anxiety. The chapter cites a 2021 Imperial College London report linking experience of floods, cyclones, heatwaves, and wildfires to negative mental health impacts. Crucially, the World Meteorological Organization reported in 2021 that while extreme climate events increased since the 1970s, fewer people are dying from climate disasters, because of improvements in vulnerability and coping capacity. This makes those two elements essential investigative territory.

## Investigating local vulnerabilities

Vulnerability refers to the conditions that make a community, person, or system susceptible to harm from hazards. The chapter divides vulnerability into four overlapping categories.

### Physical vulnerability

Physical vulnerability includes building codes, vegetation cover, population density, construction materials, land use, and the location of critical infrastructure. In Brazil, favelas (historically neglected informal neighbourhoods) are most impacted by landslides and flash floods because they are built with low-quality materials, on steep slopes, and far from hospitals.

Investigation angles: Are housing regulations accounting for future climate projections? Are building permits being granted in current or future high-risk areas? Do different parts of the population have unequal access to hospitals or evacuation shelters? Are flood embankments creating new vulnerabilities or benefiting only some people?

### Social vulnerability

Social vulnerability reflects how membership in different groups affects disaster risk. In Bangladesh, farmers' gender and religion influence how protected they are from climate extremes. In Sweden during COVID-19, immigrants were more heavily affected partly because government crisis communication was not translated into their languages. Segregation, human rights violations, gender inequalities, and illiteracy all increase vulnerability.

Investigation angles: Are minorities left exposed to climate extremes? Do decision-makers account for different needs related to gender, ethnicity, or disability when designing adaptation policies? Are disasters used to justify segregation or population displacements?

### Economic vulnerability

Economic vulnerability covers inequalities in financial capacity to cope with disaster: income levels, debt, insurance coverage, access to credit, and national disaster funds. Hurricane Katrina in 2005 primarily affected low-income and largely Black residents of New Orleans. The city's mayor issued an evacuation order 19 hours before the hurricane struck, but local government did not organise public transportation for the approximately 100,000 people who did not own a car. An NPR investigation in 2021 found that heat deaths of workers in the United States had doubled since the 1990s, driven by both climate change and policy failures.

Investigation angles: Are insurance systems prepared for future climate disasters? Are climate shocks used to justify unequal economic reforms? Do some actors (insurers, climate protection service providers, government institutions) benefit from disasters?

### Environmental vulnerability

Environmental vulnerability refers to environmental factors that increase susceptibility to hazards. Forests and wetlands can protect people from natural hazards. Deforestation, industrial pollution, and intensive agriculture increase the intensity and impact of hazards. Sandstorms in China are worsened by deforestation and related practices. The Kontinentalist found that unequal tree cover in Asian cities exposes different populations unequally to urban heat.

Investigation angles: Are municipalities or corporations destroying forests that protect from hazards? Is water or food being wasted in ways that will impair coping with future climate extremes? Is climate change creating new, unsuspected environmental risks?

During Hurricane Katrina, economic vulnerabilities meant that low-income residents could not afford housing on higher, less flood-prone ground, so they were more exposed when the levees broke. This illustrates how different types of vulnerability interact.

## Investigating coping capacity

Coping capacity is the ability to withstand and recover from shocks. Investigators should scrutinize the measures put in place across four overlapping phases of disaster management.

### Risk mitigation

Risk mitigation involves long-term actions to reduce the likelihood or consequence of disasters. Examples include adopting climate-resistant crops, diversifying livelihoods, building flood protections, using prescribed fires to prevent uncontrolled wildfires, and designing cities to handle sea-level rise. Reducing greenhouse gas emissions is one mitigation measure.

Investigation angles: Are critical infrastructures (dams, bridges, electric grids, nuclear power plants) maintained and prepared for future climate scenarios? Are farmers adapting their techniques? Are adequate coordination systems in place for disaster management and adaptation?

### Preparedness

Preparedness involves measures put in place before a disaster to improve coping. According to a study from the US National Institute of Building Science, every $1 invested in disaster preparedness saves $13 in response costs. Individuals can stock supplies at home. Cities and companies can plan and rehearse for disasters. States and donors can fund preparedness programs. The chapter notes that investigations themselves are a form of preparedness, since uncovering gaps in adaptation systems helps society prepare for the next disaster.

Investigation angles: How many emergency supplies are in stock? Are shelters well located and ready? Have organisations prepared climate response plans and implemented them? Are early warning systems in place and functioning?

### Response

When a disaster strikes, the systems built during preparedness are activated. First responders are usually nearby residents, followed by firefighters, police, ambulances, and humanitarian organisations. The chapter holds that responders must be held accountable alongside government.

When Hurricane Maria struck Puerto Rico in October 2017, the island's emergency-supply warehouses were nearly empty and FEMA was understaffed. Maria displaced approximately 130,000 Puerto Ricans and destroyed an estimated $94.4 billion worth of properties. While the government initially reported 64 deaths, several investigations uncovered a much higher death toll.

Response to one disaster can itself cause another. Following the 2010 Haiti earthquake, Nepalese UN peacekeepers allegedly brought cholera into the country, triggering an epidemic that killed approximately 10,000 people.

Investigation angles: Is response inefficient or leaving some groups out? Is response generating conditions for a new disaster? How does response affect long-term physical, environmental, economic, or social vulnerabilities?

### Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation begins once the most urgent needs are met. People recover, houses are rebuilt, and displaced populations return. Rehabilitation presents an opportunity to "build back better" and address underlying vulnerabilities. Recovery is often unequal, benefiting some more than others. Power relationships can shift, finance and procurement decisions are made, and lessons may or may not be learned.

Investigation angles: Are climate scenarios taken into account when rebuilding? Are procurement and reconstruction contracts awarded fairly and transparently? Is the process actually building back better?

## Scale and cross-border dimensions

Climate adaptation can be investigated locally (a city's flood-risk plans), nationally (disaster insurance systems), or cross-border (EU policies affecting firefighter budgets). Climate adaptation is inherently local, because communities adapt to their specific risks. This local character, however, can be exploited by national or federal governments to evade accountability. The chapter cites Spain, where the central government and a regional government blamed each other for the mismanagement of a lagoon where thousands of fish were dying.

At the same time, local stories about climate adaptation tend to connect with audiences. The Norwegian public broadcaster NRK's "Chasing Climate Change" project, depicting climate change's current impacts in Norway, attracted one million page views in a country of five million people and won several awards.

Climate adaptation stories also have cross-border relevance. Forest fires similarly threaten Russia, Australia, Turkey, and the United States. Each country's experience can inform others. German planners can learn from Bangladesh's flood management. Chilean and Japanese earthquake preparedness offers lessons for Haiti and Turkey. One country's coping capacity can increase a neighbour's vulnerability, since hazards rarely stop at borders.

Cross-border investigations can ensure that better adaptation measures spread across regions, that one country's strategies do not export risk to neighbours, and that unnecessary deaths are prevented.

The chapter closes by encouraging collaboration between journalists, scientists, activists, citizen investigators, and technologists, and between journalists and artists, photographers, or filmmakers who can make evidence accessible and engaging.

## Glossary of key terms (as defined in the chapter)

These definitions are sourced as cited in the chapter. Sources include NASA, the IPCC SR1.5 glossary, and the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) terminology.

**Climate change:** Change in the usual weather found in a place.

**Climate change adaptation:** The process of adjustment to actual or expected climate and its effects, to moderate harm or exploit beneficial opportunities.

**Climate change mitigation:** Human intervention to reduce emissions or enhance the sinks of greenhouse gases.

**Coping capacity:** The combination of all the strengths, attributes, and resources available within an organisation, community, or society to manage and reduce disaster risks.

**Disaster:** A serious disruption of the functioning of a community or society due to hazardous events interacting with conditions of exposure, vulnerability, and capacity, leading to human, material, economic, or environmental losses and impacts.

**Exposure:** The situation of people, infrastructure, housing, production capacities, and other tangible human assets located in hazard-prone areas.

**Greenhouse gas emissions:** Emissions of gases responsible for warming the atmosphere, such as water vapour (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrous oxide (N2O), methane (CH4), and ozone (O3).

**Hazard:** A process, phenomenon, or human activity that may cause loss of life, injury, or other health impacts, property damage, social and economic disruption, or environmental degradation.

**Mitigation:** The lessening or minimizing of the adverse impacts of a hazardous event.

**Preparedness:** The knowledge and capacities developed by governments, response and recovery organisations, communities, and individuals to effectively anticipate, respond to, and recover from the impacts of likely, imminent, or current disasters.

**Recovery:** The restoring or improving of livelihoods and health, as well as economic, physical, social, cultural, and environmental assets, systems, and activities, of a disaster-affected community or society, to avoid or reduce future disaster risk.

**Response:** Actions taken directly before, during, or immediately after a disaster to save lives, reduce health impacts, ensure public safety, and meet the basic subsistence needs of the people affected.

**Vulnerability:** The conditions determined by physical, social, economic, and environmental factors or processes which increase the susceptibility of an individual, a community, assets, or systems to the impacts of hazards.
