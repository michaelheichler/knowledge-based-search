## Overview

This chapter is a first-person account by Miguel Pinheiro, a journalist and multimedia documentarian with a background in science, film, and photography. Pinheiro argues that documenting marginalized communities ("invisible populations") requires different methods, ethics, and framing than standard investigative journalism. He draws on four of his own projects to show what goes wrong and what works, and situates the work within an international human rights framework for cultural and linguistic rights.

## The Subjectivity of Storytelling

Pinheiro opens with the legend of the Flying Dutchman, a ghost ship condemned to sail forever. The legend has been retold many times. Heinrich Heine and Richard Wagner each shaped distinct versions. In Wagner's opera the captain becomes a figure of tragic longing and redemption, different from earlier versions of simple divine punishment. Pinheiro uses this to establish a core claim: truth is subjective, depending on the personal imagination of whoever witnesses it. Different versions of the same story are not an error to correct. They are expected and legitimate.

He reinforces this with a quote from the Portuguese poet Fernando Pessoa: "The poet is a pretender. He pretends so completely that he even pretends that the pain he actually feels is pain." The point is that readers bring their own experience to a text, and the emotion a story produces is partly the reader's own, not only the author's intention.

Photography and film are described as a "double-edged sword." They appear objective because they capture what is physically in front of the lens, but every frame reflects the documentarian's choices about what to include, what to exclude, and how to frame the subject. This subjectivity is not a flaw to hide but a condition to acknowledge.

## Who Are Invisible Populations

Pinheiro defines invisible populations as communities whose voices rarely reach wider audiences because of geographic, social, or cultural isolation. His own work concentrates on Indigenous peoples, maroon communities (African descendants who escaped enslavement and built independent settlements), and riverine populations in the Amazon. These groups are often covered only when their suffering serves a dramatic narrative, or not covered at all.

The concept of biocultural diversity organizes much of the chapter. Biocultural diversity is the integrated inheritance of biological and cultural systems (the knowledge, languages, practices, and relationships with land that a community carries). Biocultural heritage refers to what is at stake when that inheritance is lost. Pinheiro argues that protecting this heritage is a human rights matter, not merely a conservation or anthropological interest.

## Case Study 1: The Healing Shaman

Pinheiro profiles Claudio Suarez, a Peruvian healing shaman from the Amazon Rainforest who practices traditional Ayahuasca ceremonies. Ayahuasca is a plant-based medicine that Indigenous peoples have used for centuries. It contains DMT (Dimethyltryptamine), which is classified as a controlled substance and banned in most countries, despite its sacred and medicinal status in the cultures where it originated.

At the time of the project, Ayahuasca had become part of a billion-dollar psychedelic industry alongside LSD and psilocybin. Silicon Valley practitioners promoted microdosing. Pharmaceutical researchers published on therapeutic applications for depression and anxiety. An article titled "Ayahuasca: Caramel Gold?" published in Science for the People magazine in August 2021 is among the sources Pinheiro draws on. The tension at the center of this story is that the commercial and scientific mainstream was extracting and monetizing a tradition while the original knowledge holders (people like Claudio Suarez) remained invisible.

Pinheiro spent nearly a week with Claudio before conducting any formal interview. He then balanced Claudio's own account with commentary from a neuroscientist and an NGO director, both of whom provide context that helps audiences unfamiliar with Indigenous medicine understand the story without dismissing it. This approach (surrounding the central subject's voice with legitimizing sources) is one of the chapter's explicit recommendations.

## Case Study 2: The African Trap

Pinheiro describes a failed three-month multimedia project in Cape Verde, an island archipelago off the West African coast. His original framing emphasized social problems: drug addiction, crime, and gender inequality. A local collaborator challenged him directly: "Why do you come all the way to someone else's land, only to speak about what's not right over here?" That question forced Pinheiro to recognize he had been reproducing a stereotype rather than engaging with the community's actual life.

He applied this lesson in a subsequent project called "Afro Rio," a photo exhibition and video documenting African artists living in Rio de Janeiro. He reframed the subjects as cosmopolitan contributors to Brazilian urban culture. The same Candomblé tradition (an African diasporic religion that developed in Brazil through the mixing of African, Indigenous, and European practices, with its own deities called Orishas) that had been treated as marginal became a living creative force. The contrast between the two projects illustrates how a documentarian's assumptions about what is newsworthy directly shape whether a community is represented fairly.

## Case Study 3: Afro Amazonia

Pinheiro's ongoing project "Afro Amazonia" documents maroon communities in the Amazon facing converging threats: deforestation, dam construction (including the Belo Monte Dam, described as the world's third largest hydroelectric dam, located in Brazil), palm oil cultivation, and land conflicts. The project combines historical documentation, environmental reporting, and recovery of stories that have no mainstream media presence.

The chapter quotes a Xipaya female chief whose story is part of the project "Terra Preta." She argues that the slogan "Save the Forest" is insufficient because it erases the people who live there. The emphasis must be on saving the inhabitants of the forest, not only the ecosystem in the abstract.

## Case Study 4: The Last Speaker

Pinheiro documents Odete Kuruaya, whose Indigenous name is Iawá, described as the last fluent speaker of the Kuruaya language. The Museu Paraense Emilio Goeldi, the main scientific research institution in the Xingu region of Brazil, had already listed Kuruaya as an extinct language. The language had become a form of social stigma: speakers avoided it because of prejudice, and transmission across generations was deliberately suppressed. A younger generation is now attempting to recover and preserve it.

Pinheiro cross-checked the community tip about Iawá against the Museu Goeldi's archives online, which stated the language was no longer spoken. The archive contradicted the tip, which raised the investigative priority rather than discrediting the source. He then embedded with the family for several days before conducting the formal interview.

The statistical context comes from a 2014 report by Loh and Harmon: approximately 25% of the world's roughly 7,000 languages face extinction. For comparison, mammal extinction risk is estimated at 21%, reptile at 15%, and bird at 13%. Indigenous peoples are the primary speakers of most endangered languages. Pinheiro's documentation of Iawá was published on YouTube, and the finished story (published in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Portugal, and Brazil) gave the Kuruaya family negotiating power with the private company responsible for compensation from the Belo Monte Dam.

Miles Davis is quoted in this section: "How did Columbus discover America when the Indians were already here?" The quote frames the point that dominant historical narratives systematically erase the prior presence and knowledge of Indigenous peoples.

## Human Rights Framework

The chapter situates cultural documentation within a succession of international agreements:

- 1966: The UNESCO General Conference established a formal link between human rights and culture.
- 1982: The Mexico Declaration broadened the definition of "culture" beyond high art to encompass the full range of a community's practices, values, and ways of life.
- October 2000: UNESCO adopted the Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity.
- The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (Article 25) affirms Indigenous communities' rights to maintain their spiritual relationship with their traditionally owned lands.

Pinheiro argues that these agreements make cultural and linguistic rights integral to universal human rights, not optional or secondary concerns.

## Methods and Principles

Across the four case studies, Pinheiro distills several explicit practices.

**Time and access.** Invest significant time with subjects before selecting a main character or conducting formal interviews. Pinheiro spent nearly a week with Claudio Suarez before any formal interview, and several days with Odete Kuruaya's family before recording. The subject's own voice, memories, and beliefs are described as the primary source of information.

**Source depth.** Research goes beyond web search. Pinheiro names these source types: subject interviews, local interviews, previous news pieces, science and media articles, public archives, historical books, and striking visuals as evidence.

**Contextual framing.** Build context around the central narrative. Voices from scientists, NGO directors, or other recognized experts help audiences accept unfamiliar claims. "It is easy for an audience to discard something new, so it is important to build a context around your story, to be more easily embraced."

**Resisting sensationalism.** The Cape Verde project failed because it was driven by funder and market expectations rather than the community's actual life. Red flags include framing that emphasizes deprivation, crime, or victimhood at the expense of a community's own priorities and framing driven by what "bleeds leads."

**Participatory documentation.** Involve community members in the process. Give them credit, ownership, and the power to represent themselves. Recognize that some communities will refuse participation out of fear of government or police attention, and that refusals must be respected.

**Alternative media channels.** When mainstream outlets will not cover invisible populations, alternative and independent channels become essential for reaching audiences and creating pressure on institutions.

## Central Argument

The chapter argues that invisible populations have stories, rights, and knowledge whose loss constitutes a human rights failure. Multimedia journalism can amplify these voices, but only if the documentarian gives up the impulse to impose dramatic or stereotyping narratives. The work requires time, ethical discipline, contextual framing, and a genuine orientation toward the subjects' own perspectives rather than external expectations about what those communities represent.
