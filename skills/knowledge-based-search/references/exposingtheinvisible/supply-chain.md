## What a Supply Chain Investigation Is

A supply chain is a set of steps that commodities undergo on their way to becoming products used by consumers or industry. Steps include places where products are transformed (an electronics plant turning transistors and copper lines into circuit boards) and places where products change hands through transport (loading circuit boards onto a truck for shipment to a computer manufacturer).

Supply chain investigations collect evidence linking each stage of that journey, from the origin of a raw commodity to the final consumer product. They can change the behavior of entire industries when done with thorough, reliable evidence. A supply chain investigation into a large palm oil supplier led Unilever to suspend that supplier over deforestation claims. Iceland Foods committed to removing palm oil from its own-brand products by the end of 2018. Findings can affect not only a company's reputation but also its financial status, particularly when illegal or controversial activity is revealed. Controversy does not require illegality: even the legal use of rabbit fur can be problematic for a fashion brand.

Most supply chain investigations are carried out by journalists or NGOs, but determined independent investigators and communities directly affected by an issue also conduct them.

## Why Supply Chains Are Hard to Trace

Everything around us is part of a supply chain, but the nature of supply chains means we rarely know how commodities are produced or where they originate. Companies tightly guard information about their suppliers and customers to protect competitive advantages. Supply chains are often complicated networks or webs of actors, processes, and movements. While thousands of links may connect the components of a single laptop, investigations usually focus on a specific segment.

**Traceability** is the passing of information from one stage in the supply chain to the next, covering the origin of ingredients or raw materials, the alterations they incurred, and the history of transportation and production. Some companies have systems for this, but many chains are so long and complex that even the companies directly involved cannot achieve full traceability.

**Certification schemes** (organic, fair trade, sustainable timber, palm oil, coffee) signal that products were manufactured, produced, and traded following specific standards, including observation of human rights, traditional land rights, and environmental rules. Investigators should verify these claims rather than accept them at face value.

## The Standard Actors in a Supply Chain

- **Producer**: the company, person, or group that extracts, grows, or mines raw materials (for example, the owner of a timber plantation or a bauxite mine).
- **Initial processor**: the company that carries out the first transformation of the product (a timber mill turning logs into planks, or a bauxite refinery).
- **Further processors**: companies that carry out additional transformations. Some chains have multiple further processors. Others have none.
- **Importers, exporters, distributors**: companies responsible for moving products into different countries by operating or hiring shipping or trucking services.
- **Manufacturers**: companies that carry out the last transformation before the product is sold to consumers or industrial users (a furniture maker, a soft drink can producer).
- **Retailers**: companies and individuals responsible for selling products to consumers or industrial users (a hardware shop, a supermarket).

## Where to Start and How to Proceed

An investigation can start at any point in the supply chain where unethical or illegal behavior is suspected. In most cases, investigators follow the trail downstream from that entry point. If a mobile phone assembly plant is suspected of using child labor, the investigation most likely continues downstream toward the brand selling the phones, not upstream toward the metals used in manufacture. In some cases, research goes upstream: residents near a new manufacturing plant may want to find out whether the commodities entering the plant are of controversial origin.

Research is most likely nonlinear. Multiple tools will be needed. Work will involve extensive online research and sometimes field research. Company websites, maps, transport tracking services, customs and product databases, corporate records, and stock exchange websites are all potential sources. Field research and collaboration with others may be needed to collect evidence and witness accounts.

The unpredictable nature of supply chain research means it is not always possible to anticipate how long it will take. Linking two steps can take one day of database work or many months and dozens of strategies. Starting with basic searches and testing tools before tackling complex or sensitive cases is the recommended approach.

Re-evaluate assumptions and verify data at every step. What initially appears likely may have another explanation.

## Building Actor Profiles

Once an entry point is chosen, establish a list of companies and people of interest. For each actor, research:

- Physical operations and locations (satellite images, Google Maps, Google Earth to identify transport routes and facility positions)
- Company type, ownership structure, and shareholders (company registries, stock exchange, company website)
- Financial information (company filings, annual reports, stock exchange if listed)
- Registered office and operational addresses (corporate records, company website)
- Board members, directors, and other businesses they control (Investigative Dashboard, company registries, company website)
- Bank connections, loans, and mortgages (corporate records, court records, annual financial reports)
- Employee feedback and grievances (glassdoor.com, comparably.com, careerbliss.com)
- Historic job postings via archive.org/web
- Subsidiaries from annual reports, sustainability reports, company presentations, and shareholder filings

Company websites, activity reports, supply chain due diligence reports, stock exchange filings, media articles, and social media profiles of the company, its board, and its staff are starting points. Professional networks such as LinkedIn allow searching for people and companies and identifying possible connections.

**Key platforms for corporate research:**

- Investigative Dashboard (OCCRP): https://id.occrp.org/ provides a global index of public registries for companies, land, and courts. The platform also hosts a searchable database of millions of documents from public sources, leaks, and past investigations at https://data.occrp.org/.
- Open Corporates: https://opencorporates.com/ offers searchable company records worldwide.
- ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database: https://offshoreleaks.icij.org/ contains documents from the Paradise Papers, Panama Papers, Offshore Leaks, and Bahamas Leaks.

## Tracking Trade Flows: UN Comtrade

UN Comtrade (https://comtrade.un.org/) provides international trade statistics. A Comtrade search can reveal which countries export or import a given commodity, narrowing the field of smelters, ports, or manufacturers to investigate. Products are organized by HS (Harmonised System) codes.

## Tracking Ships: AIS Vessel Tracking

All vessels over 300 tonnes (gross) are required by law to emit AIS (Automatic Identification System) radio signals carrying speed, direction, and exact location. This data is captured by other vessels, by stationary receivers in ports and on coastlines, and by satellite receivers.

**Primary tracking platforms:**

- MarineTraffic: https://www.marinetraffic.com/ provides near real-time and historical movement data.
- Vesseltracker: https://www.vesseltracker.com/

Always search more than one vessel-tracking website to trace the same ship, as some platforms may provide more free information than others.

AIS receiver hardware can be purchased from Amazon or specialized websites such as Milltech Marine. Investigator forums such as ShipSpotting can provide guidance on setting up receivers.

## Customs Data

Customs data consists of shipping manifests that vessels carry. Governments collect this data for trade statistics, and private companies sell access to it. Before purchasing, identify the HS codes for the products in question. If one country in a trade relationship does not make customs data available for purchase, try to obtain the same information from the trading partner country.

## Identifying Products: Barcodes

- EAN (European Article Numbers): 13 digits, used both within and outside Europe.
- UPC (Universal Product Code): 12 digits, used in the US and Canada. The leading zero signifies US or Canada registration.
- The first three digits of an EAN indicate the GS1 member company that accepted registration of the code. The location of that company is not necessarily the same as the manufacturing location.
- GS1 is a non-profit that maintains the global barcode standard. Website: https://www.gs1.org/. Searchable barcode database: http://gepir.gs1.org/index.php/search-by-gtin.

## Identifying Food Producers: EU Hygiene Labels

EU food hygiene regulations cover all stages of the production, processing, distribution, and sale of animal products intended for human consumption. Every individual establishment that puts such products on the market must have a registration number that uniquely identifies it. This code appears on the product label as a combination of letters and numbers inside an oval.

The EU maintains an online database of these codes, covering companies from more than 80 countries. This database allows investigators to identify the actual producer rather than the brand owner.

## The Hypothetical Bauxite Investigation

The chapter illustrates the full process with a fictional case. A news report describes villagers in a South American country forced at gunpoint to work in a bauxite mine run by a company called Brown Gold. The goal is to determine whether minerals from that mine end up in consumer products.

The entry point is where the mineral leaves the mine. Initial online searches yield little beyond the company name, registration number, and founders. Background research then covers bauxite's role as the main source of aluminum, the aluminum production process and its impacts, the largest global bauxite and aluminum producers, and the main industrial uses of aluminum.

**Finding the smelter.** Bauxite is processed in smelters. A Comtrade search shows that the country where Brown Gold operates exports bauxite to several countries. Further research reveals that bauxite travels primarily by ship or train. Maps show deepwater seaport locations and rail connections between mines and ports. A subsidiary of Brown Gold named BG Shipping is found in corporate records. Its warehouses are located at a port connected to the mine by rail.

**Tracking vessels.** Monitoring vessels on MarineTraffic over several weeks identifies ships that dock adjacent to the BG Shipping facility. One vessel travels directly from BG Shipping to the port facility of a smelter called Caliper Alumina.

**Customs data.** Purchased customs data shows that a company called Beverages Inc. bought aluminum from Caliper Alumina. The Beverages Inc. website features photos showing cans of soft drinks produced by prominent brands, including a company called Spark Drinks.

**Retailers.** Retailers selling Spark Drinks are identified through their online stores and by visiting stores in person, completing the chain.

Even at this point, questions remain. Investigators should ask whether the supply chain links can be confirmed by additional means, and whether the chain extends further before the product reaches a store.

## Risks and Security Considerations

Supply chain research often comes with significant risks. Because this research can affect company profits and earnings, investigators should secure their work and consider operating pseudonymously.

**Digital security:**

- Use Tor Browser (torproject.org) for anonymized browsing.
- Use DuckDuckGo (duckduckgo.com) instead of Google to reduce tracking.
- Use a VPN (Virtual Private Network) to avoid revealing your IP address.

**Physical safety.** Successful supply chain research often requires field research in addition to online work. Field research can be high-risk, particularly in areas where corruption is widespread, journalists have little protection, or violence occurs frequently.

**Data access and jurisdiction.** Companies typically have many customers and suppliers, creating many forks in the investigative trail. Make choices carefully, based on facts rather than instinct. Supply chain bottlenecks are worth special attention: where a small number of companies hold dominant market share, the number of paths to investigate is reduced. If one country in a trade relationship does not publish customs data, try to buy the equivalent data from the trading partner country.

In most cases, it is not possible to prove a supply chain link all the way to a specific final consumer product. This dilution of the chain is common and is not necessarily a failure. Proving that a company is connected to controversial practices at any point in a chain is often sufficient for the investigation's purpose.
