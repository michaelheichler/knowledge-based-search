## Overview

This chapter covers the foundational concepts of an investigation: what evidence is and how to assess its quality, how to document work so it holds up to scrutiny, how to verify information rigorously, how to manage the emotional demands of the work, and how to choose a starting method. The chapter sets norms that apply to all investigation types regardless of medium or topic.

## Evidence

The chapter uses a broad, non-legal definition of evidence: information that is material to the question, problem, person, or process being investigated. Evidence is proof that something happened, or proof that it did not happen. An investigation is a process of organized evidence collection that seeks to be as close to the truth as possible.

The chapter uses two analogies. The first is residue: the past leaves behind dust, footprints, documents, videos, audio recordings, witnesses, scents, and paperwork. Collecting evidence means finding and verifying these traces. The second is a jigsaw puzzle: each connection between pieces brings the investigator closer to the full picture. Every piece added gives at minimum a clearer framework, a partial image, or a new source.

### Checklist for good evidence

The chapter lists fifteen qualities that distinguish good evidence. Not every piece will meet all of them, but pieces meeting many should be prioritized.

1. **First-hand.** The investigator witnessed it directly, has access to the people involved, or has access to official documents about it.
2. **Documentable and preservable.** The path leading to the evidence can be mapped, every research step can be recorded, and the evidence can be saved in a form others can later access, confirm, and understand.
3. **Timely.** It was created or documented close to when the event occurred. A large time gap makes evidence less traceable.
4. **Verifiable.** The investigator and others can check and confirm it is real and accurate.
5. **Origins confirmable by a third party.** Someone other than the investigator and the source can confirm the evidence is real. If only the investigator can confirm it, that is a vulnerability. Exception: if protecting a vulnerable source requires secrecy, keeping it confidential may be the only viable option.
6. **Content confirmable by a third party.** An expert, another witness, a research organization, or an official document can support the claims.
7. **Shareable.** The investigator can show the evidence to support arguments. If it must remain confidential, additional means of proof are needed.
8. **Documented chain of custody.** Every step is logged, all findings are saved with exact source details, dates, times, and places, and the evidence can always be traced to its original source.
9. **Uncorrupted and tamper-proof.** No unauthorized party can access or modify it.
10. **Metadata included.** For images or video, metadata (author, location, time) is preserved so the investigator can confirm where and when something was taken and that it has not been modified.
11. **Connected to other evidence.** It links to other findings from multiple sources to support the argument or strengthen the narrative.
12. **Exposes information gaps.** Good evidence opens new questions and points to where information is weak or missing. This is useful, not a failure.
13. **Does not expose human sources to unmanageable risk.** If the evidence could be traced to a vulnerable source, the investigator should consider whether other corroborating evidence exists that does not pose that risk. Source safety comes first.
14. **May challenge preconceptions.** Evidence that contradicts expectations or changes the investigator's mind is a sign of quality and impartiality.
15. **Speaks for itself.** People can understand the evidence and its meaning without an elaborate surrounding narrative.

## Documentation

The chapter introduces the term "investigative hygiene" for the practice of keeping thorough, consistent records of every step in an investigation. Its purpose is to create a verifiable record that can withstand scrutiny and criticism.

Documentation serves the investigator directly: it helps recall prior conclusions, retrieve leads that seemed useless before but gained importance later, and spot threads that were not followed up. It also matters for external purposes: publishing findings, speaking to law enforcement, pursuing a case in court, handing evidence to human rights defenders, enabling a collaborator to continue the work if the original investigator must step away, or working with other partners.

The chapter notes a risk: investigation logs can benefit adversaries if the information is compromised. Investigators must assess risks at the start and throughout the investigation, use encrypted storage and devices, and follow physical and digital security practices. It references Tactical Tech's Security in a Box (securityinabox.org) and Security Checklist (securitycheckli.st) as starting points.

### Documentation tips

**Interview notes.** Record the date, start and end times, location, and names of participants. Note what was discussed and any planned follow-up meetings. Audio recording is acceptable but requires consent from the other party. Identities of vulnerable sources must be protected.

**Contact information.** For each contact, record not just their name and contact details but how the investigator came to know them, each conversation, and a summary of what they provided. For sensitive sources, use an alias in all notes and working documents. For businesses, phone numbers, or email addresses under research, note where each first appeared.

**Process log.** Keep a dated log of every investigative step and its outcome, including every tool and technique used. The chapter gives examples: "On 3 January 2018, I and my research partner captured satellite images using Google Earth and documented the location of the company's construction sites" and "On 14 March 2018, I tried calling different public phone numbers associated with the company to see if I could get records, but I was diverted to voicemail."

**Narrative outline.** As information accumulates, create a rough narrative outline of what has been figured out so far. This reveals what is missing, what needs verification, and what new directions to consider. Visual maps of data and the investigative process can also help.

**Evidence log.** Maintain both a physical and a digital log of all evidence, including dates, origins, storage location, security measures, status, who collected it, and who has interacted with it. Keep copies in more than one place to avoid total loss.

## Verification

The chapter frames verification as a central requirement: "If it's not verified, it's not valid." It situates this against the contemporary information environment, citing fake tweets during the 2017 Kenyan election that mimicked BBC and CNN stories, and fabricated information in India spread via WhatsApp that led to people being killed or beaten.

The chapter presents a hierarchy of evidence types from weakest to strongest, in the context of a running example about a minority group being denied service at a local authority.

1. **Word of mouth.** A neighbour heard about it. This is rumour and requires moving closer to the facts.
2. **Expert statement.** A researcher who tracks such incidents confirms it is frequent. This begins validation but still requires first-hand information or additional sources.
3. **Second-hand accounts.** People directly affected describe their experience. This is closer to the facts but needs corroboration from other witnesses or existing documentation.
4. **Research documents or reports.** A credible NGO report describes the occurrence based on interviews with community members. Useful, but still needs corroboration.
5. **Official documents.** A signed and dated official paper documents the incident. Strong, because it identifies the institution and responsible parties, but witness or expert statements strengthen it further. Note institutional bias: an office may want to hide data, especially if it implicates them.
6. **Photos, video, audio.** A recording of the situation. If real, it can be linked to location, time, and people involved. Requires checking EXIF data and metadata, verifying the source, and verifying the contents. Do not use as evidence without contacting those involved to confirm accuracy.
7. **First-hand evidence.** The investigator is present during the event and may film it. The strongest form of evidence. The investigator has the opportunity and responsibility to document thoroughly and to establish contact with others involved to allow later confirmation.

### When to verify

Always, and iteratively. Verification is not a one-time step. New evidence may cast reasonable doubt on previously verified evidence, making re-examination necessary. Documentation enables retracing steps for repeated verification.

### Challenges of verification

The chapter identifies personal assumptions and unconscious bias as among the biggest obstacles. Investigators may seek sources that confirm existing beliefs rather than those that contradict them. Assumptions about a source's credibility can also interfere, because even trusted and reliable sources can be wrong, even unintentionally.

A further challenge is finding creative approaches. To verify whether a public official was at a location he denies visiting, checking his own social media accounts may not be enough. Checking the accounts of his relatives and friends for relevant messages, comments, or images may provide confirming or denying evidence.

### How to verify

The chapter recommends starting by writing out all questions and doubts about the gathered information, as well as identifying personal assumptions and potential bias. Useful self-questions include: What do you know with 100 percent certainty? If it is an image, who took it, when, on what camera, in what place, and how do you know it has not been modified? Could anyone be trying to mislead you with this image? Are there groups that benefit from spreading this information?

Verification has three phases.

1. **Verifying the source.** Where the information came from and where it originated.
2. **Verifying the content.** Whether it is exactly what it claims to be.
3. **Verifying its relevance.** Whether it fits into the investigation.

For each medium (images, video, audio, written testimonies, webpages), there are specific tools and techniques. The chapter points to the Verification Handbook (verificationhandbook.com) and the UNESCO handbook "Journalism, Fake News and Disinformation" for in-depth guidance.

Sharing findings with trusted colleagues for an external check is recommended, but only when those colleagues can be trusted and when both parties know how to communicate and share information safely.

## Emotions and Bias

### Personal emotions

Mental health issues including depression and Post Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) are common among investigators working on difficult topics. Cumulative stress and the sense of responsibility toward subjects, colleagues, family, and oneself should not be underestimated. The chapter states that self-care and safety should always come first. If capacity is reduced by mental state, the investigation will suffer. Investigators have the right to take a break or step away from work that is harming their wellbeing.

Investigators are also described as commonly feeling that the people they are covering need them to keep going, producing a sense of responsibility that can make stepping back feel like a luxury. The chapter names this pattern without condemning it, noting it is also a driver of continued work.

### Bias

Emotional involvement with a story is described as natural and unavoidable, particularly when speaking with sources the investigator sympathizes with. Unconscious biases make some stories more compelling and others less so. The chapter does not ask investigators to be fully detached but asks them to be aware of their biases and to act fairly and honestly. Pushing one point of view at the expense of others, or neglecting important context because of personal bias, can cause an investigation to fail and can damage the investigator's reputation.

Practical advice for managing bias during an interview or stressful stage: focus on the evidence collection process and the questions, remember that the investigator is a recipient of the evidence provided, and try to stay calm and collected.

### Holistic security

The chapter introduces the term "holistic security," defined as a method and way of work and life that takes into account physical and emotional factors together. It is promoted among human rights defenders, gender activists, investigative journalists, community organizers, and citizen investigators. It goes beyond physical and digital safety habits to include ways of staying physically and mentally aware, caring for oneself and others, and regularly reassessing the relevance, impact, and personal toll of the work. The chapter recommends the Holistic Security guide from Tactical Tech (holistic-security.tacticaltech.org).

## Starting Where You Are

The chapter advises beginning an investigation from the investigator's most comfortable and safe point of entry. If an investigator is skilled at social media research or database searching, starting there makes sense. If the virtual space is unfamiliar, starting with observing and documenting physical events, taking photos, making detailed notes, and talking to people is equally valid. Beginning with an unfamiliar method is counterproductive.

### Types of investigation methods

**Desk research and digital investigation.** Digital investigation uses digital tools and media to investigate. The subject can be digital (a YouTube video) or analogue (a politician's real estate assets). Online databases let investigators collect, filter, connect, and verify information beyond what traditional physical archives offer. The chapter cautions that digital research carries safety risks, particularly when those being investigated are watching for surveillance.

**Combining digital and traditional methods.** For some investigations, pen and paper is more appropriate. Combining traditional and new techniques can be effective. The chapter notes that evidence not available digitally can sometimes be found by asking donor-funded NGOs, which are often required to conduct background studies and collect datasets that are never published. One example: an organization providing psychological support to former militia fighters may have data on how many returned to their home villages. Another: an environmental NGO may have data and interviews on river contamination and illness that were not published elsewhere. Explaining why the data is needed may prompt the organization to share it or assist the investigation.

**Field research.** Some investigations require collecting first-hand information directly: recording testimonies, gathering samples, observing events and places, or going out to talk to people. The investigator is responsible for documenting field evidence and confirming its accuracy. Field research carries personal safety risks that must be assessed at every step. Knowing when to ask for help, reconsider an approach, or set a project aside is described as a healthy asset for an investigator.
