---
title: Eight Breakable Rules of Investigative Writing
source: Exposing the Invisible, The Kit (kit.exposingtheinvisible.org)
---

Written by Ricardo Gines and published November 2022, this chapter presents eight rules that conventionally govern investigative and journalistic writing, explains why each rule holds in most cases, and then gives concrete examples of when breaking each rule serves the story better. The framing is that mastering the rules is a precondition for breaking them deliberately rather than accidentally.

## The premise

Writing is hard even for experienced investigators. All forms of media, whether newspaper articles, podcasts, television, or nonfiction books, require a competent narrative. Standard rules provide a clean, reliable narrative. Once a writer understands why those rules exist, they can choose when to violate them without losing control of the piece. The chapter quotes George Orwell's instruction: "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous."

## Rule 1: Do not use the first person in narration

The default reasoning is that a journalist's subjectivity can obscure facts and distract the reader from the story. First person ("I" or "we") is justified when the journalist is a central link in the narrative.

The chapter quotes Roman Anin's piece "The Brotherhood of Killers and Cops" (Novaya Gazeta and OCCRP, 3 September 2018), which opens with "I" because Anin personally met Aslan Gagiyev, the alleged leader of a criminal group called the Family, accused of 60 murders. Anin's meeting in Vienna in October 2016 is the thread that connects the story. A "we" construction is also acceptable when a reporting team is physically present as witnesses, as shown in an excerpt from Crina Boros, "Romanian Trucker, Polish Wage, Dutch Workplace" (Investigate Europe, 4 October 2018), where reporters travel with truckers in the Netherlands.

## Rule 2: Write in the active voice

Active voice is shorter, faster to read, and makes the actor clearly responsible for the action.

Passive voice is appropriate when the actor is unknown or when what was done to people matters more than who did it. The chapter draws on E. D. Morel's account of mutilations by Congo soldiery, as reproduced in Anya Schiffrin's "Global Muckraking: 100 Years of Investigative Journalism from Around the World" (New Press, August 2014, pp. 17-21). The passive constructions there place the suffering rather than the perpetrators at the center. A second example comes from Lennart Dodoo's "Liberia: Kosiah 'Loved Looting'" (FrontPageAfrica, 11 December 2020): "Many people were killed en route to Sorlomba." The point is that people died, not who killed them.

## Rule 3: Lead with the most important information

In a news piece, putting key facts in the first paragraph serves readers who may not finish the article. The rule is weaker for features, long-form investigations, and nonfiction books, where a reader will continue past an atmospheric opening. The chapter notes that platform matters: a high-traffic publication can afford to maintain suspense because readers will stay, while a less-known outlet may need to front-load facts to hold attention.

Gay Talese broke this rule in "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (Esquire). Talese could not interview Sinatra directly because of press agent restrictions, so he opened by placing Sinatra silent in a dark corner of a Beverly Hills bar, watching couples dance, saying nothing. The important facts arrive later. Rodolfo Walsh's "Operacion Masacre" (1957), credited as the first major nonfiction novel, opens with the author reluctant to leave a chess game in a cafe in La Plata, and explicitly states his initial disinterest in the shootings he will document. Walsh even quotes himself saying: "Peron doesn't interest me, the revolution doesn't interest me. Can I go back to chess?"

## Rule 4: A story must have a beginning, a middle, and an end

The three-act structure (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution) is the inherited default from ancient theater. A linear timeline is often subverted when a different ordering better serves the evidence.

The chapter cites OCCRP's "The Riviera Maya Gang: Global ATM Bandits," which covers a Romanian-led criminal group that used Bluetooth skimming devices inside ATMs in Cancun, Mexico. The article jumps across years (2008, 2009, 2012, 2014, 2015, 2020) with no strict chronology because the organizing principle is the accumulation of proof, not the sequence of events. A contrasting approach appears in Jacques Pauw's "Bloody Trail of the South African Police" (Die Vrye Weekblad, 19 November 1989): the piece opens with Captain Dirk Coetzee's full admission of murders and terror attacks committed at the Vlakplaas unit through 1982, then proceeds as a cold register of killings. The resolution comes first, the documentation follows, and there is no traditional rising action.

## Rule 5: Answer who, what, when, where, how, and why. Leave no questions unanswered

The five Ws confirm that a story is fully reported. But when the journalist genuinely does not know an answer, or when the unanswered question is itself the point, leaving questions open is more honest and more powerful.

Liu Binyan's "People or Monsters?" (1979, The People's Daily) investigated a major corruption case in Heilongjiang province, China. It uses deliberate question marks, partly because Binyan lacked answers, and partly because some answers were politically impossible to state directly. Ken Saro-Wiwa's "The Coming War in the Delta," collected in Schiffrin's "Global Muckraking" (pp. 117-121), exposes contradictions in Nigeria's oil policy by posing a series of questions about whether Nigeria operates as a capitalist or socialist state, and ends with the rhetorical question: "Is anyone listening?" A third example, Tatiana Escarraga's "Los pueblos que se trago el carbon" (El Tiempo, 22 June 2013), uses questions voiced by a displaced indigenous community in Colombia to convey the uncertainty of people being forced off their land by the mining sector: "What will remain of our village? Our traditions? Our beliefs?"

## Rule 6: Keep writing simple. Use short paragraphs and short sentences

The chapter notes this rule is culturally specific, applying mainly to English-language nonfiction. Non-Anglo-Saxon literary traditions are often more baroque. Even Hemingway, associated with plain brevity, broke this rule.

The main example is Douglas Preston's "El Dorado Machine" (New Yorker, 29 April 2013), a feature about using LIDAR (a laser-based aerial mapping technology that fires 125,000 infrared pulses per second) to discover ruins of an ancient civilization in the jungles of Honduras and Nicaragua. Preston generally follows the short-sentence rule, which aids fact-checking by making each sentence a discrete unit. But longer sentences appear in three situations: to extend the flow and rhythm of a descriptive passage, to capture a character fully in a single image, and to explain technical processes accurately. The LIDAR sensor description requires complex syntax to remain precise: beams reflect off the canopy, some penetrate gaps and reach the forest floor, and software strips the vegetation reflections to produce a topographical map. A second example from Yasha Levine's "Google's Earth" (The Guardian, 20 December 2018) shows the same necessity when tracing a company's government contracting relationships across multiple agencies and contractors including Lockheed Martin and SAIC (Science Applications International Corporation, informally known as "NSA West").

## Rule 7: Dialogue does not belong in investigative reporting

Most investigative writing presents facts in linear temporal accumulation, with no dialogue. Dialogue is appropriate when direct conversation conveys testimony or the emotional weight of what happened in a way that paraphrase cannot.

Luiza Vasiliu's "It Was An Experiment" (Casa Jurnalistului, 9 December 2016) investigates an orthopedist in Romania who used an uncertified implant on a child born with a limp. Vasiliu uses direct dialogue with the patient to let the reader hear the patient's own desire for the doctor to feel her pain.

The more extended example is Horacio Verbitsky's "La Confesion" (Pagina/12, 3 March 1995), an interview with Adolfo Scilingo, a naval officer who admitted to participating in the "death flights" of Argentina's military dictatorship (1976-1983), during which prisoners were thrown alive and naked from aircraft into the ocean. The dialogue is stripped down: Verbitsky asks short questions, Scilingo gives short answers. The exchange establishes that between fifteen and twenty people were killed this way every Wednesday for two years, a total of between 1,500 and 2,000 people. The dialogue format makes the admission land with more force than a summary would.

## Rule 8: Every investigation must conclude

A conclusion closes the circle and signals resolution. But some investigations have no resolution to offer, and forcing one falsifies the reality.

Three types of non-conclusion are described. The first is a story where no solution exists: Gareth Jones's dispatch from Ukraine ("15 Hours to Wait for the Shops to Open," The Daily Express, 7 April 1933) documents the famine under Stalin, closes with homeless children on a train, and ends with the sentence "The train rolled on to Moscow." The second is the unsatisfying but realistic ending: Ken Silverstein's "Oil Boom Enriches African Ruler" (Los Angeles Times, 20 January 2003) documents how oil earnings of Equatorial Guinea enriched President Obiang's family, then ends with a Guinean official's dismissal: "It's a different culture." The corruption is proven but no consequence follows. The third is the deflating personal ending: Harold Strachan's "Behind Apartheid Bars" (Africa Today, June-July 1965) closes with a prisoner who coped with apartheid jails by sleeping up to fifteen hours a day. The chapter invokes Raymond Carver's short stories as a literary parallel for abrupt, unresolved endings.

## Definitions

The chapter defines four terms used throughout. A news piece is the most basic genre, explaining what happened, to whom, how, where, when, and why. A feature is a longer article that is fact-based and accurate but allows more creative expression. A cronica is a Latin American journalistic genre combining true reporting with literary technique, described by Gabriel Garcia Marquez as "a true tale." Long-form investigative stories are defined by GIJN as involving systematic, in-depth, and original research, often using public records and data, with a focus on social justice and accountability.
