## Overview

Written by Ankita Anand, this chapter argues that no investigator should work alone. Collaboration is treated not as an option but as a structural requirement for safe, effective, and impactful investigative work. The chapter covers why collaboration matters, how to build and manage a team, how to handle practical and safety considerations before and during an investigation, and what to do after publication.

## Why collaboration is necessary

The central claim is that "lone wolf" investigations are no longer a good idea, if they ever were. No single person can master all the skills an investigation requires. Distributing work across multiple people improves both the quality of findings and the reach of publication. Networks of collaboration make uncovered information and its outreach stronger.

Collaborators need not all be investigators. A team can legitimately include data analysts, economists, graphic designers, visual artists, photographers, website developers, journalists, activists, academics, and researchers. Each contributes to different phases, from background research and verification through to publication and awareness-raising. Local facilitators are especially valuable because they grant access to people, places, and documents that an outsider cannot reach alone.

Collaborations operate at multiple levels: between a local citizen and an NGO researcher, or across borders in regional and global networks.

## Benefits of collaboration

The chapter lists and explains the following benefits.

**Different skills.** It would take one person a lifetime to master all the skills used in investigations. A team lets several uniquely skilled people work toward the same goal simultaneously.

**Wider audience reach.** Each collaborator brings their own professional network and community. Multiple people distribute findings in a non-linear way to more people, increasing impact.

**Circumventing travel restrictions.** Visa rules, pandemic-induced restrictions, and budget limits can prevent an investigator from reaching a location. Connecting with people already there is often the most viable alternative.

**Sharing the work.** A large investigation can become overwhelming once the initial excitement fades. A partner or team helps investigators stay focused and reminds them they are not alone.

**Accountability.** A voluntary project without organizational support risks being shelved. Having collaborators creates mutual accountability that keeps the project alive.

**Workplace equality.** Local investigators often do the hardest work but receive the least recognition. The chapter insists they should be treated as equal collaborators. A journalist deserves a joint byline and shared payment. An unnamed associate who helps with logistics deserves compensation for time and expenses. Activists or citizen investigators can be connected to resources that support their ongoing work.

**Authenticity.** "Parachuting," where an outsider arrives, investigates quickly on untested assumptions, and leaves, risks misrepresenting local realities. Partnering with someone who has deep local knowledge prevents exploitation and misrepresentation. If policymakers act on an inaccurate account, entire communities suffer.

**Perspective.** One person embedded in a situation takes many facts for granted. An outsider raises questions the insider would not think to ask, which helps the investigation explain things a wider audience needs to understand.

**Safety.** Attacks on whistleblowers have increased even in places previously considered safe. Working in a group offers protection in numbers. If a report is suppressed in one country it can be amplified elsewhere. The chapter names Forbidden Stories (forbiddenstories.org) as a program that continues and publishes the work of journalists facing attacks, and The Signals Network (thesignalsnetwork.org) as an organization enabling safe and ethical collaboration among media and whistleblowers. An outside investigator who plans to leave after publication faces less long-term personal risk than a local who must continue living in the area. A local investigator may enjoy more trust in their community, while the outsider may gain access by virtue of being a foreigner.

**Gender and cultural diversity.** Teammates of different genders allow access to different people and places. A survivor of sexual violence may prefer speaking with a woman. Certain religious places may admit only men. LGBTQI+ investigators may gain easier access to their communities. Beyond access, a diverse team respects the interviewee's right to speak with someone close to their lived experience, which enables deeper interviews. Cultural diversity also brings unique analytical perspectives.

## Building the team

The chapter advises listing the skills, sources, and resources already available and identifying what is missing. Key questions to guide team assembly include:

- What documents, places, or people are inaccessible, and who can gain that access?
- Is there a language barrier requiring a reliable local investigator or translator?
- Is the community closed to outsiders, requiring a trusted local activist or civil society ally?
- Does the investigation involve hostile situations requiring someone with hostile environment or de-escalation training?
- Will sources send sensitive information digitally, requiring a digital security specialist?
- Will findings be presented online to a young audience, requiring an interactive platform designer?
- Will there be strong visual material on site, requiring a filmmaker or photographer?
- Could those exposed file defamation cases, requiring a lawyer familiar with similar issues?

The chapter also recommends thinking beyond the immediate investigation team to subject matter experts who assist for part of the project, and a looser network of friendly advisors available for informal consultation.

## Finding collaborators

Several methods are recommended. Direct knowledge of investigators, or knowing people who know them, is the most natural starting point. Organizations that have worked on the same issue can suggest additional collaborators. Authors of published work on the topic may be willing to join.

Social media platforms, including LinkedIn, Twitter, Instagram, and Facebook, are useful for making contact. Many investigators list email addresses in their Twitter bios, accessible without creating an account. Affiliate organizations can provide introductions.

Conferences, workshops, and other investigation-related events are described as among the best settings to meet collaborators and launch proposals.

Named platforms for finding collaborators:

- Help A Reporter Out (HARO) at helpareporter.com
- Hostwriter at hostwriter.org
- Global Investigative Journalism Network (GIJN) at gijn.org
- Unbias the News at unbiasthenews.org (Hostwriter's project that invites collaborative and cross-border stories for publication)
- Project Facet at projectfacet.org
- Dataharvest (European Investigative Journalism Conference) and its associated ARENA discussion group and topic-based networks at journalismarena.eu
- GIJN's online helpdesk, which includes advice on identifying networks and collaborators

Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn keyword searches using terms such as "investigation," "investigative," or "OSINT" (open source intelligence) can surface relevant groups. Some groups are private and will vet applicants. An investigator can also start their own group.

## Funding

All team members must be compensated fairly. Organizations that support collaborative investigations financially include Clean Energy Wire, Hostwriter, Journalismfund.eu, the European Journalism Centre, and n-ost. The Pulitzer Center and GIJN also provide lists of grants and fellowships. Many grants allow team applications even when they do not explicitly require them. The chapter advises writing to a grant organization to ask if a team application is permitted.

One common approach is to secure a grant first and then recruit collaborators, which reassures potential joiners that the project is real and funded. The drawback is that a single applicant carries all the early labor, which can create an informal hierarchy. A better approach, where possible, is to involve a collaborator from the proposal stage. Joint proposals produce more realistic budgets because local collaborators know what is actually obtainable in their region, and timelines reflect both schedules rather than one person's optimistic estimate.

## Choosing collaborators

CVs and bios describe what a person has done, not who they are or how they work. The chapter recommends video or voice calls before selecting anyone. Topics to explore include work interests, working style, ethics, motivations, expected outcomes, experience with collaborative projects, and how the person handles conflict. Intuition should not be dismissed because of impressive credentials.

## Written agreements

A jointly prepared written agreement is described as mandatory. Questions the agreement should address:

- How and when will the team communicate and share updates?
- What are each person's deliverables and deadlines?
- How frequently will findings be shared? (This protects the project if a member withdraws.)
- What is the backup plan if something cannot be delivered?
- How long before a non-response allows the team to proceed without that person or restrict their data access? (If a member has been detained or locked out of their systems, restricting access protects the data and the investigator.)
- What is the final aim: sharing with an affected community, submitting to a tribunal, or media publication?
- What happens to the data and evidence after the investigation ends?

## Shared code of conduct

In addition to the agreement, the chapter recommends developing a shared code of conduct covering ethics, diversity, and inclusion. This should be drafted collaboratively, ideally in a dedicated meeting. Topics to address include acceptable and unacceptable behavior, consequences for breaking the code, how to give constructive feedback, how to handle tension and frustration, and how to respect each other's privacy and safety. Larger teams can assign one or two members to help resolve conflicts. The chapter cites Tactical Tech's Gender and Security resource "Zen and the art of making tech work for you" as a reference for setting up conduct agreements.

## Before the investigation begins

**Briefing.** New members must be brought fully up to speed, including the backstory behind the proposal, why the story matters personally to the initiator, and the rationale behind every budget category. Budget transparency matters because unexplained allocations create resentment, particularly if the grant is not split equally or if the time investment appears to exceed the compensation.

**Defining roles.** A clear division of labor avoids later confusion. Teams should decide early whether to appoint a project coordinator responsible for soliciting updates and sending reminders.

**Logistics.** When there is no organizational infrastructure, team members must divide logistical tasks such as scheduling and currency exchange. Some investigators refuse to handle these tasks, so the division must be negotiated explicitly.

**Credits.** How each person is acknowledged should be settled before the project begins. Unresolved credit questions breed resentment and strain the project. The initiator taking the lead on this conversation signals good intent and transparency.

**Ethics and methodology.** The team should agree on shared ethical standards before work begins, including positions on contested techniques such as sting operations or pressing sources. Tool choices also carry ethical implications: open source tools may have limitations but often better protect data privacy than commercial alternatives. The chapter references Tactical Tech's article "Technology is Stupid: How to choose Tech for Remote Working."

**Schedule.** Teams should agree on working hours, when members do not work, and how to handle unexpected developments on weekends or holidays.

**Health.** Physical requirements for site visits should be assessed. Members with dietary needs or health conditions should prepare accordingly.

**Budgeting.** The team should agree on a more detailed budget breakdown than funders may require. Unexpected expense categories will arise, and the chapter recommends checking with funders before making unplanned expenditures. The team should also connect directly with the funder's accounts department to understand billing rules. Investigators should raise logistical realities with funders before signing memoranda of understanding, and funders who regularly support investigations generally try to be flexible if the team communicates openly and honestly.

**Letters of intent.** Many funders want letters from media outlets expressing interest in publishing the findings. The chapter addresses common hesitations about requesting these letters. A letter of intent is not a binding publication agreement, and the investigator is not locked into publishing with that outlet. Publications with limited travel budgets benefit from investigations that arrive already funded. Smaller outlets can be approached if larger ones are unresponsive. If an editor is too busy to write a letter, offering a template of one or two sentences is acceptable.

**Post-production.** The team should agree in advance on which platforms will carry the findings, evaluated by reputation, distribution capacity, publication timelines, fees, and response times. If the team plans to create its own publishing space, this must be planned throughout the investigation rather than left to the end.

**Contingency plans.** The team should decide in advance what to do if a member goes missing, withdraws, or fails to deliver. One mitigation measure is for the project coordinator to collect updated notes, data sets, and expense receipts from investigators at regular intervals.

## Safety and risk assessment

The chapter treats safety as a core part of collaboration planning, not an afterthought. It introduces the "Do No Harm" principle: sources and collaborators must not be negatively affected by the investigation.

**Risk inheritance.** Risk is not confined to individuals. If investigators are in a low-risk environment but interview someone in a high-risk one, they inherit that person's risk level before and after the interview, with risk increasing again at publication. Conversely, investigators with a low-risk profile can raise the risk of high-risk contacts through ordinary behaviors, such as using a mobile phone openly, that would be dangerous in a different context.

The key formulation is: "a team is always as safe as its highest risk member." All members must understand everyone's vulnerabilities, and the team must build a safety assessment and mitigation plan that addresses those risks.

Safety planning considerations include:

- Location-specific risks for each site the team plans to visit, recognizing that a place may be safe for one member and dangerous for another depending on gender, religion, language, or local reputation.
- Whether low-risk members should take the public lead while high-risk members keep a low profile, or whether the team should split up to keep high-risk members away from certain activities entirely.
- Exit plans for emergencies (stampedes, physical aggression, accidents), including vehicle choices, local emergency contacts, embassy numbers, and hotel details.
- A designated contact at home who holds all the team's travel details and will investigate if they go silent beyond an agreed time.
- A security signal code: an innocuous question with a pre-agreed answer that signals safety or danger without alerting observers.
- Accommodation choices based on whether visibility or inconspicuousness is safer, and whether mixed or same-gender lodging draws less scrutiny.
- Safety assessment of local helpers and interviewees, prioritizing their safety.
- Data storage and daily backup protocols.
- A prepared consistent answer for questions about who the team is and what they are doing.
- Health insurance details and a shared document with each member's allergies, blood type, and pre-existing conditions.
- Legal issues around entering a location or transporting data across a border, requiring a legal advisor familiar with the relevant region.
- Mental health provisions, including access to professionals during traumatic coverage, funded where possible through the grant budget.

The chapter recommends Tactical Tech's Holistic Security Manual (holistic-security.tacticaltech.org) as a detailed resource integrating self-care, well-being, digital security, and information security for collaborative projects. The manual was designed for human rights defenders but is equally relevant for investigators.

## Traveling as a team

Face-to-face meetings are described as irreplaceable for team building and mutual support. When interviewees see a team that spans multiple locations and will publish in multiple places, they sometimes respond more fully. At the same time, a foreign investigator's presence may raise suspicions in some interviews, and the local investigator's judgment on when others should stay back should be followed.

## Crossing borders

The chapter advises patience, calm, and cooperation at border checkpoints. Language barriers can cause miscommunication, and relaxed body language reduces the impression of suspicion. Investigators traveling as media should be prepared to describe their work in broad terms and should check local media accreditation requirements in advance, since details are often not clearly available online. Carrying an extra document when uncertain is better than arriving without it. Paying visa fees online in advance, if permitted, reduces uncertainty at arrival.

A cooperative traveler who has followed all procedures correctly and made a single honest mistake is treated differently from one who appears to have a pattern of irregularities, which is why thorough preparation matters.

## During the investigation

**Regular communication.** Teams should keep each other updated even when there is no major development. Meetings should produce written minutes. Secure communication tools such as Signal or Wire are recommended over standard phone calls or WhatsApp. Safer email services such as riseup.net or ProtonMail are also recommended.

**Timeline.** Long projects lose momentum as other demands grow. Teams should find ways to maintain a sense of community: sharing relevant reading, scheduling check-ins, and looking for opportunities to reconnect when energy flags.

**Interviews.** When a team member conducts an interview, they should be briefed on what prompts the source to open up and what topics to avoid. In some cases a partner or a foreign investigator can usefully ask questions that would be unwelcome from the person with an existing relationship to the source.

**Sharing resources.** Findings, source details, and new questions should be shared on agreed secure platforms. Collaboration is not competition. The chapter also encourages sharing contacts and resources beyond the immediate project to build lasting professional relationships.

**Social media.** Sharing partial findings or team photographs during an investigation can alert subjects, endanger sources, or create security risks. Boarding passes in particular carry information that can be exploited. Any social media sharing should be agreed by the whole team first.

**Documenting methodology.** A group of investigators each with distinct skills represents an unusual occurrence. The methodology is worth documenting periodically, both for credibility and because it often makes for compelling reading alongside the investigation itself.

**Check-ins.** The chapter recommends a regular space for team members to share how they are doing personally, not just professionally. Genuine concern for well-being builds loyalty, belonging, and commitment.

**Acknowledging contributions.** Individual efforts can get lost in team work. The chapter encourages specific, named recognition: thanking the colleague who organized the shared files, naming the person who kept the group emotionally grounded.

**Cultural sensitivity.** Team members should educate themselves about each other's backgrounds before working together. It is not the responsibility of any team member to teach colleagues basic facts about their culture.

## After the investigation

Publishing results is cause for celebration, but impact does not always arrive immediately. The chapter advises teams not to let a slow uptake undermine their sense of achievement.

After rest, the team should identify specific communities and individuals who would benefit from seeing the findings but may not have encountered them yet. The chapter suggests presenting findings in innovative formats: community presentations, arts or political theatre, translations, or new publications.

All contributors should be credited publicly, with the exception of those who requested anonymity. Findings should also be shared with advisors and supporters who gave feedback during the project.

The chapter insists that all team members be paid on time. Late payment is disrespectful and often damages future working relationships.

A team that has completed one investigation together carries trust that makes future collaborations easier and more likely to receive funding.
