By Nuria Teson, Ankita Anand, Jess Lempit, and Megha Rajagopalan. Published October 2019, updated November 2021.

## Overview

Interviews are not a single conversation but a process that spans background research, trust-building, risk assessment, question preparation, the interview itself, and follow-up. Human sources are often indispensable to investigations, adding testimony, evidence, context, and human depth. The chapter covers how to find, approach, interview, and stay connected with sources safely and ethically.

## Sources vs. Interviewees

The chapter draws a firm distinction between these two terms. An interviewee is someone you speak to for a current investigation and may not contact again. A source is someone you invest effort in building a relationship with, for current and future work. Some interviewees become sources over time, and the relationship dynamics differ accordingly.

## Risk

Risk runs through every stage. Human interactions are unpredictable, so preparation is the core safeguard. Risk is described as inherited: if a source lives under threat or works on a dangerous topic, the investigator absorbs a portion of that risk during and after contact. Risk rises again at publication and remains elevated when investigating people in positions of power who may become aware of the investigation. Risk assessment is not a one-time task. It must be revisited continuously as the investigation evolves.

Gender shapes risk. People perceived as women face distinct risks and also distinct opportunities, including access to spaces or people that men cannot reach. Investigators should research local cultural and social norms around gender before any interview. Practical adjustments, such as wearing a wedding ring or dressing in a way that matches local norms, may reduce unwanted attention.

## Finding Sources and Interviewees

The chapter lists practical methods:

- Monitor social media. Use topic-based lists on platforms like Twitter (with tools such as TweetDeck, per a Bellingcat guide). Avoid liking or following accounts if the association could create risk. Note possible biases or willingness to speak.
- Subscribe to newsletters from organizations active in the relevant field.
- Attend press conferences, open meetings, conferences, and public lectures. Collect business cards if safe to do so.
- Follow activist blogs and local citizen pages, which can provide leads and introductions even when they have low readership.
- Visit official websites to identify ministries or departments relevant to the investigation and request interviews with officials.
- Seek access to closed or private groups of specialists, NGO workers, journalists, or activists, usually through trusted contacts. Be prepared for background checks from those groups.

Any online content found during research should be archived immediately, using tools like the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine, because material can be removed.

Less obvious sources, people who hint at deep knowledge without presenting themselves as experts, may be among the most valuable. Time spent with them can also open doors to other interview subjects.

## Digital Security Basics

Before initiating contact, investigators should establish basic security habits.

For research: use a privacy-focused browser or search engine (DuckDuckGo, Firefox), and Tor Browser for private and undetectable searches. Use a VPN on both laptop and mobile to hide your IP address. The chapter recommends checking VPN providers on ThatOnePrivacySite and suggests reputable free options including Bitmask, Riseup VPN, Psiphon, and Lantern. Use strong passwords and change them periodically. Encrypt sensitive data on all devices using full disk encryption (available on Linux, Mac, and Windows) and VeraCrypt for specific folders. Lock devices when unattended. Be aware of border-search rights when crossing into other countries (the EFF's Border Searches guide is recommended).

For communications: use encrypted email (PGP) whenever possible. For calls and messages, use Signal or Wire in preference to WhatsApp or unencrypted channels. When forced to use unencrypted phone calls, share only the minimum information and agree to provide details in person. A burner phone (disposable, unlinked to your identity) is an option if you suspect your regular phone is monitored.

## Types of Sources

The chapter classifies sources along three dimensions.

By role in the investigation:
- Primary sources provide direct evidence or testimony: victims, witnesses, perpetrators, government officials, whistleblowers.
- Secondary sources support the investigation without direct testimony: tip-offs, journalists, fellow investigators and NGOs. Witnesses can occupy either category depending on reliability.

By position relative to the investigation:
- Vulnerable sources have experienced trauma or belong to threatened groups: survivors of trauma, survivors of sexual violence, minors, communities under threat, people in marginalized or persecuted groups.
- Adversarial sources may not be sympathetic and can become aggressive: hostile sources, perpetrators, law enforcement. These should generally be interviewed last to prevent interference.

By type of information they can provide:
- Background information: academics, scientists, NGO workers, lawyers, other experts.
- Understanding or interpretation: experts and academics who can contextualize data.
- Direct evidence: witnesses, security forces, activists, government staff.

Sources providing direct evidence may be biased, willingly or not, and may face risk due to their proximity to the topic.

## Planning and Organizing Interviews

Start with interviews that provide background before moving to those focused on evidence. Work from easier to harder subjects so that knowledge accumulates before approaching adversarial interviewees.

Diversify across age, gender, caste, class, and belief. Do not speak only to group leaders. Seek out other members. Multiple perspectives reduce bias and strengthen evidence.

Before contact, assess risk carefully. Ask whether the source has spoken publicly on the subject before, whether they fear government surveillance, how technically capable they are, and how vulnerable or scared they may be.

Prioritize ordering to prevent sources from interfering with each other, creating conflicts of interest, or tipping off subjects of the investigation prematurely.

## Power Dynamics

The investigator typically holds power over the interviewee because the investigator controls how the testimony will be used. This asymmetry requires clear personal ethical rules before interviews begin.

When interviewing people with less power, be aware that they may see you as an opportunity, fear you, or feel unable to refuse. Respect refusals. Do not use privilege to coerce anyone into speaking. If a person says no, acknowledge the refusal, try an intermediary if appropriate, and leave contact details for later.

When interviewing people with more power, watch for attempts to control your questions, offer favors, manipulate, or threaten. Do not feel obligated to accept favors. Company executives and high-ranking officials who are inaccessible directly may be reachable through lower or middle-level staff.

## Making Contact

Introduce yourself and your purpose honestly. Deception is unethical and may put sources at risk if they later feel misled. Avoid the word "investigator" if it creates alarm. "Researcher" is acceptable. Never hide the purpose of your work. Show a website or published work to establish credibility. Share only as many details of the investigation as safety allows.

Use encrypted channels from the first contact whenever possible.

Allow ample lead time before deadlines. Offer appointment times that suit the interviewee. Schedule each interview to build on the previous one, and leave time between them to review findings and reassess.

## Logistics and Location

Location matters. Public places are not always best. A location can reveal as much as the interview itself: a site connected to the interviewee may spark memories or documentary evidence, provide talking points, and build trust. However, revisiting a traumatic location can also cause re-traumatization, so proceed carefully. A lawyer's office or NGO headquarters can serve as a neutral, safe environment.

## Travel Safety

Conduct a travel risk assessment before entering another country, taking into account your race, gender, sexual orientation, and citizenship, since risk is tied to identity. Embassy travel guides from the US, UK, Canada, and Australia provide country ratings, though these tend to be conservative. Risk management firms (Riskmap, Kroll, International SOS) also publish assessments, but check their funding and incentives. UN crime statistics offer supplemental data.

Inform someone you trust of your itinerary. If kidnapping or detention is a real risk, complete a proof-of-life document: a form with personal details and answers only you know, instructions for a trusted contact on what to do if you go missing, and optionally passwords to your social media accounts. The Rory Peck Trust offers a sample form.

Verify vaccination requirements, passport validity (more than six months remaining, multiple blank pages), and visa requirements.

## Preparing Questions

Define clear goals for the interview: giving a human face to the story, gathering testimony as evidence, or building a legal case.

Write a list of questions but treat it as a flexible guide, not a rigid script. The list helps maintain focus if emotions run high during a difficult interview.

Use varied question types. Closed questions (yes or no) are useful mainly to confirm previously gathered facts. Open questions invite elaboration. Factual questions establish basic information. Follow-up questions build on an answer without interrupting. Provocative questions can prompt a reaction or force a commitment, but use them judiciously. Avoid leading questions, which push the interviewee toward a desired answer.

For difficult subjects, mark sensitive questions in advance and prepare alternative phrasings. Officials and politicians often deflect. Asking the same question in several different ways can work around deflection.

Respect the interviewee's time. Interviews ideally should not run beyond one hour unless both parties agree to continue. Save the most significant questions for when they are needed.

Language matters for accuracy and respect. Understand relevant terminology before the interview, particularly for topics involving gender identity, race, religion, or social status.

Prepare emotionally. Set aside personal biases. If interviewing vulnerable sources, expect sadness, anger, and difficult accounts.

Get legal advice if uncertain about the legal status of any information you handle, if you encounter evidence of a crime, or if someone you want to interview is already under investigation or surveillance.

## Conducting the Interview

Face-to-face is the preferred channel. Body language can reveal as much as words. Calls are acceptable. Use encrypted, video-capable tools (Jitsi, Wire) and get permission to record before starting. Email is the least preferred option because it limits follow-up, slows the conversation, and cannot confirm the identity of the respondent.

## Key Terms

Explain these concepts to sources in plain language rather than relying on jargon.

- On the record: information can be published and attributed to the named source.
- Off the record: information cannot be published or attributed, but it can guide the investigator to other sources who can go on the record. If the same information is independently verified elsewhere, it can then be published.
- Non-attributable: information can be published but the source cannot be named. For example, "a member of the Canadian Embassy" without naming the individual.
- Anonymous: information can be published, but nothing can be published that could identify the source, not even their role or employer, if that detail could narrow identification. Example: a Syrian woman in a small Norwegian town. If few Syrian women live there, naming the town effectively names her.
- Release form (also called informed consent form or consent form): a signed document in which the source agrees that their words and/or image may be used under specified conditions. For minors, a responsible adult must sign.

Always double-check information from any source and fact-check against other sources throughout the investigation.

## Recording the Interview

Always obtain consent before recording. For audio recording, take notes simultaneously as a backup. For video calls, use a platform that allows recording (Jitsi, Wire) and be aware that some platforms notify participants automatically when recording begins (Skype does this). Have written or recorded verbal permission before the recording starts.

When taking photographs or video, do not put the interviewee's comfort aside in pursuit of a better angle or lighting. Ask rather than instruct. Never photograph ill or elderly people outdoors in cold conditions simply for better light.

When publishing, choose images carefully. Do not use images of survivors of sexual violence that make them appear powerless or weak. Images of minors require particular legal care. The minor's rights take priority in all circumstances.

Check local law on recording. Unlawfully recorded material may be inadmissible in court and could create legal liability.

## Interviews in Foreign Countries

Cultural preparation is essential. Study the place, language basics, customs, and religious practices.

If you stand out physically, you are more visible to security forces and informants. Maintain a low profile (described as being the "grey man"). Do not reveal your identity, purpose, or sources in casual conversation. Avoid telling hotel staff or bartenders where you are going, as these are positions that security forces sometimes use for information gathering. Vary your routes and routines to avoid creating a detectable pattern. The chapter suggests flipping a coin when making mundane decisions such as which route to take or which day to travel.

Communications: obtain a local SIM card for convenience but understand that SIM cards purchased with ID link your identity to the ICCID (Integrated Circuit Card ID) and IMSI (International Mobile Subscriber Identity), which are broadcast across the cellular network. A VoIP number avoids this linkage. Consider whether to bring a phone at all. If you do, keep it offline or in a Faraday bag or aluminum foil wrap (minimum two layers) to block cellular tracking. Test the foil wrap before relying on it: call the wrapped phone to confirm it does not ring.

Language: work with a translator only if you trust their reliability. Contact local journalists or activists for referrals. Learn basic greetings and polite phrases.

Cultural and religious norms: match behavior to local expectations. Do not eat, drink, or smoke in front of someone who is fasting, but do not outright refuse food or drink if offered. Claim illness or fasting yourself rather than refuse outright. Ask where the toilet is rather than the bathroom, to avoid embarrassing hosts who may not have indoor plumbing. Accept dress code requirements in religious or conservative settings as a professional choice made for the investigation's benefit.

Gender abroad: men investigating topics such as female genital mutilation in conservative communities face significant access barriers with women. Women investigators may be treated as a "third gender" in some places, gaining access to spaces unavailable to local women or to foreign men.

Accommodation: hotels are generally safer than staying with local hosts, which can imply taking sides or put hosts at risk. However, exceptions exist. Choosing a neighborhood can itself signal affiliation with a political faction, which may affect both your safety and whether sources will speak to you. Vary your accommodation over a long stay.

Moving around: use inconspicuous transport. Local drivers are better at navigation and language. Park away from interview locations. Have an exit plan before beginning any journey, including alternative routes if the primary route becomes unsafe.

Hostile environments are not limited to active conflict zones. Countries like Colombia, Mexico, Israel, or Egypt can constitute hostile environments. The Institute for War and Peace Reporting (IWPR) publishes relevant guides, including "Conflict Sensitive Journalism in Syria" and "Reporting for Change: A Handbook for Local Journalists in Crisis Areas."

Know entry and exit routes before committing to a location. At protests, research how security forces behave and have a backup plan. The embassy is a resource but not guaranteed protection.

## Interviewing Vulnerable Sources

Vulnerable sources may not fully understand the consequences of having their story, name, or image published. They may have unrealistic expectations about your ability to help them. They may be at physical risk. The responsibility to ensure true, informed consent lies entirely with the investigator.

Connect with vulnerable sources through an organization they already know, a family member, or a trusted friend who can vouch for your credentials and be present during the interview if needed.

Survivors of trauma: prioritize making the person feel heard. Do not force recall of a painful incident unnecessarily. Focus on contextual facts rather than the emotional experience. Listen more than you talk. Use open-ended questions. Ask "what else happened?" instead of "what happened next?" to accommodate non-linear memory. Gaps, changed details, or contradictions across interviews do not necessarily indicate dishonesty. They may reflect trauma. Offer regular breaks. A glass of water or a moment of privacy can matter.

Verify trauma survivors' accounts through other means, but maintain empathy regardless of internal skepticism.

Survivors of sexual violence: all trauma interview guidelines apply. Also consider whether the investigator's gender is relevant. A woman who survived assault by men may prefer to be interviewed by a woman. Do not name victims unless they explicitly request it and demonstrate understanding of the risks. People under 18 cannot consent to being publicly named as survivors of sexual violence. Confirm that local law permits naming adult survivors before doing so.

Coerced victims: some victims may have been instructed not to share certain details, or may be afraid of their own community's reaction. Ask general questions such as "Do you prefer that we talk privately?" to gauge whether the person is speaking freely.

Victims of trafficking: they may fear that details of their own actions during their trafficking could be used against them legally. Be sensitive to this. Make clear that their safety is your primary concern.

Minors: more legal risk is involved. A responsible adult the child trusts should be present. Consent from that adult may be legally required. Use age-appropriate approaches. For younger children, bring paper, crayons, or games. Do not discuss the child in the third person in front of them. Ask open-ended questions ("tell me more about..." or "then what happened?"). Avoid questions that imply blame. Mirror the child's own words in follow-up questions. Move from a broad initial description of events to more focused questions for detail.

LGBTQI+ sources: in many countries this group faces legal and social discrimination. Increase all security measures. Avoid judgmental language. Use neutral vocabulary. Research relevant terms (binary, non-binary, intersex) before the interview. If unsure how to address someone, ask directly: "How do you identify?"

## Adversarial Interviewees

Approach with empathy even when you disagree. If time allows, start with open questions and allow the interviewee to explain their viewpoint. Some investigators present themselves as knowing less than they do, which can make interviewees more relaxed and forthcoming. Gradually introduce tougher questions.

If time is limited, move to critical questions sooner. Do so neutrally and professionally. Do not make unsubstantiated accusations. Do not get emotional. Ask the same tough question in several different ways. The source may contradict themselves or reveal more than they intended. A source is more likely to open up if they can see the questions are based on evidence, not prior conclusions.

Hostile sources may try to intimidate your other contacts into retracting their stories, shut down research resources, alert authorities, or pressure your employer. There is no way to eliminate these risks entirely. Be transparent with colleagues about the possibility of blow-back. Tell someone you trust the time and location of the interview and what to do if you do not check in. Store evidence of other sources and interviews somewhere secure before meeting a hostile source.

Perpetrators: do not make accusations. Allow them to explain their version before introducing contradictory information. Some perpetrators are also trauma survivors. Start with basic biographical questions (name, age, place of birth) before moving to open questions about events. Keep a neutral expression. Some perpetrators are narcissistic and may volunteer information if they feel they are impressing the investigator.

If a source tries to start an argument, do not engage with it. Nod politely and return to your questions.

## After the Interview

Fact-check all claims against other sources, data, and further research. Treat information from any single interview as unverified until confirmed elsewhere. An interviewee may offer certain details as bait. Keep critical judgment active.

Review the balance of your investigation. Counter statements from one side with perspectives from the other. Consider how much weight you are giving each interview relative to the overall evidence.

Reassess once all material is assembled. Return to sources for clarification if needed. Speak to additional interviewees to fill gaps. Let the material rest before a final review. Distance can reveal details previously overlooked.

Right of reply: if your investigation makes allegations against a person or organization, they have a right to respond before publication. In some countries this is a legal requirement under libel law. In all cases it is the ethical standard. Contact the subject late in the investigation, after most of the work is done, and present a written list of questions covering the allegations. Be aware that this step may expose your sources or the investigation itself to interference. Consider timing and method carefully.

Acknowledge the effort of sources and interviewees. Send them copies of or links to the published investigation. Reconnecting when you return to their area builds a network of contacts for future work.

Maintain safe communication practices with ongoing sources. Make clear why encrypted messaging is in their interest as well as yours. Only share sensitive information through secure channels.

Know when to abandon an investigation. The evidence may not support the case, or the risks may outweigh what can be achieved. Recognizing this is part of the process.

## Digital Traces Throughout the Interview Process

The chapter includes a detailed timeline of how digital traces accumulate at each stage and how to reduce them.

Before contact: use VoIP or virtual numbers (Google Voice for US users, Skype, Burner App, Coverme, or Hushed for international options) rather than a number linked to your identity. When researching potential interviewees on low-traffic websites, you may be the only visitor that day. The site's webmaster may be able to identify you. Use a VPN and a dedicated browser (Tor Browser preferred, Firefox second) for all research. Use incognito or private mode. Public internet networks are better than your home or office router. Create dedicated email accounts for investigation work at Tutanota.de or Protonmail.com. Use a password manager such as KeePassXC and set passwords of at least 15 characters.

During the interview: if meeting a source for the first time, use a verification system agreed in advance. This can be visual (wearing a specific color), physical (bringing an agreed object such as a Rubik's cube), or a challenge-and-response phrase. Encrypt all notes and recorded material. For handwritten notes, the chapter describes a method of videotaping pages sequentially with a smartphone or camera, then encrypting the video. For electronic files, use VeraCrypt (more features) or Cryptomator (easier for most users). Hardware-encrypted drives with a keypad provide another layer of protection.

After the interview, when sharing large files: OnionShare creates a Tor-accessible download link for peer-to-peer transfer. SEND by Tresorit allows uploading up to 5 GB to a cloud server with automatic deletion after 48 hours, password-protected download links, and email notification on download. The chapter strongly recommends encrypting files with VeraCrypt or Cryptomator before uploading to any cloud service.

## Verification Tools for Background Checks

Phone numbers can be verified by calling from a disconnected number, using reverse lookup services (Comfi, Whitepages, Spokeo, Pipl), or using carrier and caller lookup tools. Twilio performs carrier lookups (approximately $0.005 per request as of mid-2019) and caller lookups ($0.01 per request). Truecaller identifies numbers through crowdsourced caller ID data. Numerify covers global numbers.

For companies, non-profits, and government institutions: the Investigative Dashboard (maintained by the Organized Crime and Corruption Reporting Project, OCCRP) provides a global index of databases. For the US: Little Sis, ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer, Charity Navigator, state business entity search tools, and the SEC.

For online presence: NameChk and Instant Username search a username across platforms. Spokeo and Pipl search for personal information. Family Tree Now and Thats Them are people-finder services. Contact Out pulls contact data from LinkedIn. Transparency Toolkit covers additional data.

OSINT tools for investigators with technical skills: Maltego CE visualizes relationships between datasets. Spiderfoot and Spiderfoot HX look up IP addresses, domain names, subdomains, ASNs, email addresses, and names. The Harvester extracts information from email addresses, names, subdomains, IPs, and URLs.

## Glossary

- Anonymous source: information is published without any attribution that could link it to the source.
- Background check: pre-interview research on a person, group, organization, or topic to verify information and assess reliability.
- Double check: confirming information from a source by obtaining the same data from multiple other sources.
- Encrypted communication: messaging or email using encoding that can only be decoded by someone with the appropriate password or key.
- Fact-checking: the ongoing process of verifying information across multiple sources at multiple stages of an investigation.
- Interviewee or interview subject: the person being interviewed.
- Non-attributable: information that can be published without naming the source.
- Off the record: information that cannot be published or attributed to its source, used to guide the investigator toward on-the-record material.
- On the record: information that can be published and attributed to the named source.
- Proof of life: a document with personal details and answers only you know, left with a trusted contact, to be acted upon if you are detained or disappear.
- Release form (consent form, informed consent form): a signed document in which the source agrees to use of their words and/or image under specified terms.
- Risk assessment: a structured evaluation of the threats that may arise during contact with sources and interviewees, along with how to prevent or address them.
- Source: someone who shares information and is maintained as a contact across multiple investigations over time.
