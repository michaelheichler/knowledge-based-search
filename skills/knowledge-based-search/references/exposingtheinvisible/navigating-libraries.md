## Overview

Written by Ricardo Gines, this chapter explains how investigative journalists and other researchers can use libraries and archives more effectively. It covers the difference between libraries and archives, how to work with reference services, and where to find newspaper archives, thematic archives, and academic journals. It closes with an index of specific resources organized by type and language.

## Libraries and Archives: the Distinction

Libraries hold published, typically mass-produced materials organized for broad access. Archives preserve unique or rare documents, both published and unpublished, and apply stricter access guidelines because their holdings are treated as irreplaceable. In practice the line blurs: archives are often housed within libraries, and a library can itself function as a combination of archives. The Society of American Archivists is cited for the formal definition.

## Reference Services and the Reference Interview

The professionals who run these institutions, librarians and archivists, offer what the chapter calls "reference services": any personal assistance in locating materials relevant to a question or investigation. The most common form is the reference interview, which can happen in person, by email, by chat, or by phone. Many institutions now run a Virtual Reference Service (VRS) staffed by trained information specialists.

The chapter uses Pavla Holcova, founder of Prague-based investigace.cz and regional editor for Central Europe at OCCRP (Organized Crime and Corruption Reporting Project), to illustrate the value of these relationships. She describes two types of archivists: those who respond only to precise wording, and those who proactively volunteer documents the researcher did not know existed. Her examples include an archivist offering a copy of a full contract and then a prior conviction on the same subject.

For investigators tackling sensitive topics, the chapter advises a risk assessment before submitting any request to a librarian or VRS, to avoid disclosing information that could be unsafe to share with a third party.

Before sending a research request, the chapter recommends answering five self-assessment questions drawn from the scholarly article "Virtual Reference Interviewing and Neutral Questioning":

1. In brief, what is my topic about?
2. What have I already found?
3. What additional information do I need?
4. What type of information do I need (books, articles, audio, etc.)?
5. Do I need current or historical information?

The chapter also warns to account for possible costs (photocopies, travel to a physical location) and notes that much material is not digitized and requires an in-person visit.

Two UNESCO-published guides are cited as evidence that reference services are underused: "Story-based Inquiry: a manual for investigative journalists" (first written by Mark Lee Hunter) and "The Global Investigative Journalism Casebook" (edited by Hunter). Both describe reference services as "under-appreciated" and "the most underappreciated assets" in investigative journalism. The story of journalist Anne-Marie Casteret's book "L'affaire du sang" (Blood Scandal), about the French government knowingly selling HIV-contaminated blood products, is cited as a case where a hospital librarian compiled all the relevant scientific literature for Mark Lee Hunter in a single afternoon.

## Newspaper Archives

Nicolas Cheviron, a French foreign correspondent based in Turkey who writes for Mediapart, describes working before the internet with microfilm on machines resembling arcade cabinets. For his biography of Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan, Cheviron used the Ataturk Library in Istanbul and found old news about Erdogan's youth, his father's first wife, half-brothers in the Black Sea, his career as a football player, and his time as a public bus company employee.

Beyond historical biography, newspaper archives let investigators survey how a topic has been covered across different countries. The chapter recommends this as one of the first steps in any investigation, citing Paul Radu's guide "Follow the Money." Newspaper archives also supply possible sources, expert contacts, images, headlines, and timeline information. Mark Lee Hunter notes in "Story-based Inquiry" that news clips can serve as ice-breakers in interviews: the reporter can ask a source to confirm whether information in old stories is accurate and proceed from there.

## Thematic Archives

The chapter argues against the view that offline archives are obsolete now that so much is digitized. Alexenia Dimitrova, a Bulgarian investigative journalist specializing in Cold War-era secret archives (author of "The Iron Fist," "The King's Secret Files," "Murder Bureau," and "The Secret Files of Sol Polansky"), explains her core archival research techniques:

- Read inventories yourself. Do not rely on other researchers or archival staff.
- Read all files that seem close to your subject, even ones without obvious direct relevance.
- Look inside documents for human sources, places, and new leads to pursue deeper.
- Do not preset limits on what you will do with the documents. Initial plans for newspaper articles often evolve into documentary books or films.

She also notes that writing FOIA (Freedom of Information Act) requests and reading inventories can be tedious but is essential.

The chapter makes the point that modern digital archives grew from pre-digital traditions. OCCRP's ALEPH database is offered as an example. Its name comes from a short story by Argentine author Jorge Luis Borges, "The Aleph," which depicts a magical place that encapsulates all other places. Paul Otlet's 1935 book "Monde: Essai d'universalisme" is cited as a text that predicted the World Wide Web, including search engines and hyperlinks. Otlet's Universal Decimal Classification, a system for organizing all branches of human knowledge with interlinked themes, is still used by many libraries and bibliographic services outside the English-speaking world.

### Examples of Thematic Archives Listed in the Chapter

- **Interference Archive** (interferencearchive.org): open-access archive of social movement ephemera including pamphlets, posters, buttons, and t-shirts.
- **The Civic View From Above** (cargocollective.com/hagitkeysar): image archive by researcher Hagit Keysar, covering DIY aerial photography in spaces of political conflict.
- **Egyptian Revolution Archive / 858** (858.ma): a collection documenting events of the Egyptian revolution.
- **Syrian Archive** (syrianarchive.org): Syrian-led project preserving documentation of human rights violations in the Syrian conflict.
- **Yemeni Archive** (yemeniarchive.org): parallel project for Yemen.
- **Sudanese Archive** (sudanesearchive.org): preserves documentation of human rights violations, conflict, and resistance in Sudan.
- **Digital Library of the Middle East / DLME** (dlme.clir.org): federated digital library of cultural material from the Middle East, created in response to displacement and destruction.
- **Archiv3** (archiv3.org): network of eleven German Third World archives formed in autumn 1998.
- **ISIS Files** (isisfiles.gwu.edu): over 15,000 pages of internal ISIS documents obtained by New York Times journalist Rukmini Callimachi, digitized and published in partnership with George Washington University starting in 2018.
- **Statewatch** (statewatch.org): NGO monitoring civil liberties and the state in Europe, with a network of contributors from 18 countries, founded in 1991.
- **National Security Archive** (nsarchive.gwu.edu): nongovernmental collection of declassified U.S. documents obtained via FOIA, not connected to the U.S. government, founded in 1985 by journalists and scholars.
- **Sites of Memory / Memorias Situadas** (cipdh.gob.ar): interactive map of sites related to serious human rights violations worldwide, maintained by CIPDH-UNESCO.
- **Snowden Archive / SIDtoday** (theintercept.com/snowden-sidtoday): four years of internal NSA Signals Intelligence Directorate newsletters released by The Intercept.

## Academic Journals

Academic journals provide rigorously researched, empirically verified information and specialized knowledge. The chapter uses an investigation by the author into looting of underwater archaeological heritage in Spain and the Caribbean to show how scholarly literature served the investigation. Key uses included identifying country-specific threats to submerged cultural heritage, finding patterns, identifying experts to interview, and understanding technological advances in underwater detection instruments. UNESCO's Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage is mentioned in this context.

The chapter describes the journalist-academic relationship as symbiotic: scholars often feel their work lacks dissemination, while journalists need empirically sound sources but dislike academic jargon. Science journalism (exemplified by outlets like The Conversation, Geo, Science et Vie, and Spain's Tendencias21) is described as the genre that bridges the two. Data journalism is also noted as a field that has drawn heavily on academic research.

### Four Tips for Accessing Academic Literature

1. **Go to the library for closed-access journals.** Many research journals are behind paywalls. A library card is the best way around them. University libraries often allow public visitors. Librarians can recommend subscription journal databases. Large libraries and universities frequently subscribe to Mint Global, Orbis (Bureau van Dijk, covering more than 300 million companies), and LexisNexis, which index company records, court records, and media resources.

2. **Use Google Scholar and Internet Archive Scholar.** Google Scholar searches across scholarly disciplines. Internet Archive Scholar is part of the Internet Archive (archive.org), home of the Wayback Machine. Both tools may return full text or only metadata (author, year, table of contents) depending on the article.

3. **Check Open Access resources.** Open access means research distributed online free of cost or other barriers. The Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ, doaj.org) is the recommended starting point.

4. **Consult country-specialized archives.** Useful resources include Scielo.org, the Endangered Archives Programme (eap.bl.uk), and WorldWideScience.org.

## Other Library Resources

The chapter advises investigators to treat a local library as a living organism that is constantly changing, and to scroll through its full catalogue rather than assuming they know what it contains. The New York Public Library maintains a list of over 841 article and database resources including archive finders. The National Library of Spain offers a free-to-use deposit of 30 million digital historical images. The chapter recommends using advanced-search mechanisms in any database to narrow findings efficiently. Finding something unexpected is described using the librarian's term "serendipity."

## Index of Libraries and Archives (summarized)

The chapter closes with a structured index. Key entries:

**Newspaper archives:**
- Newspaper Map (newspapermap.com): shows what media exists in a given country.
- Internet Archive newspapers (archive.org/details/newspapers).
- Google News Archive (news.google.com/newspapers): digitization discontinued but historical papers remain.
- World Digital Library (wdl.org): U.S. Library of Congress project with UNESCO support, 100+ languages, launched 2009.
- ICON / International Coalition on Newspapers (icon.crl.edu): links to digitization projects by country.
- Elephind (elephind.com): free simultaneous search across multiple historical newspaper sites.
- Europeana (europeana.eu): European newspapers from 20 countries, 1618 to 1996.
- Readex World Newspaper Archive: historical newspapers from Africa, Latin America, and South Asia (access through libraries).
- Eastview (eastview.com): titles from over 80 countries in diverse languages (library access preferred).

**Language-specific:**
- Gallica (gallica.bnf.fr): Bibliotheque nationale de France digital library, nearly 3 million documents, 1.3 million newspaper pages including Le Figaro and L'Humanite.
- British Newspaper Archive: England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales.
- Zefys (zefys.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de): German-language newspapers including the DDR / former East Germany.
- BDPI (iberoamericadigital.net): Ibero-American digital heritage, single search point across national libraries of Latin America and Spain.
- Hispana: digital collections of Spanish archives, libraries, and museums.
- Hispanic Digital Library / Biblioteca Digital Hispanica (bne.es): National Library of Spain, free access, books from the 15th to 20th century, historical press, sound recordings.
- Prensa Historica (prensahistorica.mcu.es): approximately 8.5 million digitized pages of Spanish-language historical newspapers and cultural magazines, free.
- Hemeroteca Digital (hemerotecadigital.bne.es): over 2,371 titles, more than 67 million pages of historical Spanish press, created March 2007.
- El Pais archive (elpais.com/archivo): printed edition from May 4, 1976 to February 7, 2012 (subscription required, 10 free articles per month).
- La Vanguardia archive (lavanguardia.com/hemeroteca): free access from 1881 up to 30 days before current date, with full issue PDF download available.
- ABC archive (abc.es/archivo): founded 1903, paid content.
- Chronicling America (chroniclingamerica.loc.gov): sponsored by National Endowment for the Humanities and Library of Congress, covers U.S. newspapers 1860 to 1922, approximately 7.2 million pages from 1,270 titles.
- Imperial Russian newspapers archive (gpa.eastview.com/crl/irn) and Duke University Russian and East European Historical Newspapers guide.
- Wikipedia List of Online Newspaper Archives.

**International archives:**
- Internet Archive (archive.org): described by the chapter as the world's largest collection of digitalised materials, and home of the Wayback Machine.
- Archives Portal Europe (archivesportaleurope.net): archival information from across European countries.
- UNESCO Digital Archives (digital.archives.unesco.org): 70+ years of UNESCO institutional records and audiovisual collections.
- Wikileaks Archive (archive-it.org/collections/2017): websites and news surrounding Wikileaks releases including Afghan war diaries, Iraq war logs, and U.S. State Department diplomatic cables.
- Unified Court Records Database / UCR (ucr.irmct.org): public court records of the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (ICTR), the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY), and the International Residual Mechanism for Criminal Tribunals (IRMCT).
- Open Society Archives (osaarchivum.org): post-war history of Communist Europe, including Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty research from the early 1950s through the mid-1990s.
- National Security Archive, Washington (nsarchive.gwu.edu): founded 1985, described by the Los Angeles Times as "the world's largest nongovernmental collection" of declassified U.S. documents.
- MERCOSUR IPPDH Archives Guide (atom.ippdh.mercosur.int): archives related to human rights violations in Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Paraguay, and Uruguay.
- IMF Archives (imf.org/en/About/Archives): primary resources on IMF history, policies, and member country relations.
- ECB Archives: institutional memory of the European Central Bank including audiovisual material and artefacts.
