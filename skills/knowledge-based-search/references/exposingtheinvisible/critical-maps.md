title: Thinking Critically About Maps: Researching, Resisting and Re-imagining the World

Written by Yung Au and published October 2021, this chapter argues that thinking critically about maps is not only a way of avoiding bias but is itself a method of investigation. When investigators question the dominant perspectives encoded in maps, they gain new insights into the cases they study.

## Maps are not objective

Maps attempt to summarize an infinitely complex world and are necessarily reductive. Flat maps are always "projected," meaning a three-dimensional surface must be transformed into two dimensions, and every projection makes choices about what to center and how to distort.

The Mercator projection, one of the most widely used projections today, centers Europe and makes Africa appear smaller than Greenland, even though Africa is 14.5 times larger. The website thetruesize.com illustrates this distortion interactively.

Even the concept of "north" is not fixed. Several distinct norths exist:

- **True North**: the northernmost point on the spherical Earth, which flat maps distort.
- **Grid North**: the north running upward along the grid lines of a flat map.
- **Magnetic North**: the north indicated by a compass, shaped by solar winds and the Earth's rotation. This point shifts regularly, sometimes closer to Canada, sometimes toward Russia.
- **Google Maps' North**: an adjusted north specific to Google's own projection.

These choices mean that maps reflect the mapmaker's truths, not an objective reality. Several scholarly fields are built on this premise. Decolonial and Postcolonial Mapping seeks to resist map-making legacies that stem from settler colonialism and imperial projects. Indigenous Mapping seeks to recenter Indigenous cartographies as practices of world-making with a long history. Radical Cartography is map-making aimed at subverting the status quo and promoting social change. Critical Cartography and Critical Geography is a broad field grounded in critical theory, starting from the position that maps reflect and perpetuate relations of power.

Despite these shortcomings, maps remain central to investigations, storytelling, resistance, and governance. They can enforce territorial claims, empower communities, and erase realities.

## The critical map: four investigative angles

### 1. The vertical and beyond

Most maps are flat and presented from a bird's-eye view, compressing a multi-tiered world into a single layer. The vertical and volumetric dimensions of space are often lost. "Vertical" refers to upward and downward directions. "Volumetric" refers to thinking in terms of the volume or density of a space rather than just its area.

Hong Kong is offered as an example: it contains some of the most vertical localities in the world, with winding underpasses that turn into overpasses, miles of elevated footpaths and outdoor escalators, and life extending from underground layers to rooftops of skyscrapers. This complexity is not captured in a flat map.

The chapter discusses the Kowloon Walled City, a settlement in Hong Kong demolished in 1993. By 1990 it housed 50,000 residents within 2.7 hectares. It consisted of 500 interconnected high-rise buildings, living quarters, shops, classrooms, and other structures, stacked haphazardly on top of each other and under the control of various triads and families. Investigations of the settlement required attention to its stacked, vertical nature. Japanese anthropologist Kani Hioraki and a team of researchers published hyper-detailed maps of it in 1997 (in the publication "Daizukai Kyuryujo"). Notable details: all buildings were arranged to remain just under 14 floors to avoid low-flying planes landing at the nearby airport, and an extensive water system of 77 wells, rooftop tanks, and electric pumps was organized to carry water down to inhabitants. Flattening the map would have omitted this structure entirely.

Investigators are advised to ask:

- What dimensions and details might be missing in a flat, bird's-eye-view map?
- Are uneven topographies, elevation, and city structures important in this investigation?
- Are there elements of conflicts over airspace or sea-space?

The chapter cites further examples: the Cu Chi tunnels used during the 1955-1975 US-Vietnam War, the multi-level occupation of the West Bank as examined by Eyal Weizman in "The Politics of Verticality" and Helga Tawil-Souri in "Digital Occupation: Gaza's High-Tech Enclosure," and the 2019-2020 city-wide protests in Hong Kong that adapted to all levels of the city. Stuart Elden's "Secure the Volume: Vertical Geopolitics and the Depth of Power" (Political Geography, vol. 34, pp. 35-51, 2013) is recommended reading.

Advanced tools for three-dimensional investigation include remote sensing, 3D modelling, photogrammetry, Lidar scanners (being integrated with iPhone cameras), Virtual Reality (VR), and Augmented Reality (AR). The research agency Forensic Architecture is cited as an organization that uses these methods. Three examples are given:

- "Environmental Racism in Death Alley, Louisiana" (1718-Ongoing): Forensic Architecture, in partnership with RISE St. James, reconstructed an interactive 3D representation of a typical sugarcane plantation in the lower Mississippi River, drawing on historical archives, property surveys, interview transcripts, and cartographic evidence. The 3D model helped communicate that the plantation "was at once industrial facility, farm, prison, death camp, and luxury estate."
- "The Killing of Mark Duggan": As part of a civil claim against the UK's Metropolitan Police, Forensic Architecture built a VR environment emulating the event to illustrate inconsistencies in police testimonies.
- "Destruction and Return in Al-Araqib": Forensic Architecture collaborated with local families, Zochrot, and PublicLab to collect evidence on the village of al-Araqib in the northern Naqab desert, synthesizing aerial footage, archaeological surveys, images from cameras on kites, and photogrammetry into a navigable digital platform supporting a legal petition by the al-Turi family.

Investigators are advised to be familiar with the locality they are studying, or to collaborate with local individuals and organizations, since armchair research may miss crucial details.

### 2. Missing data

Much of the world remains unmapped or obscured. Much of the ocean floor is unexplored, and Google Maps uses infrared modelling to infer contour lines as an approximation. Nigerian-American artist and researcher Mimi Onuoha has written that blank spots "exist in spaces that are otherwise data-saturated," and that a lack of data "typically correlates with issues affecting those who are most vulnerable in that context" (see "Missing Datasets").

The late British geographer J.B. Harley wrote about what he called "cartographic silence," where blank spots are not mistakes or ignorance but are part of the map itself, where "silence can reveal as much as it conceals." Colonial maps of the Americas often erased Indigenous locations and names to represent those lands as available for settlement (see Anita Lucchesi, "'Indians Don't Make Maps': Indigenous Cartographic Traditions and Innovations," American Indian Culture and Research Journal, 42(3), 2018, and J.B. Harley, "Silences and Secrecy: The Hidden Agenda of Cartography in Early Modern Europe," Imago Mundi, vol. 40, pp. 57-76, 1988). In 2014, Brazilian geography scholar Andre Reyes Novaes argued that Rio de Janeiro's official maps mostly ignored the city's informal favela neighborhoods until the 1990s, with that silencing indicative of political elites' indifferent attitude toward favelas (Social and Cultural Geography, vol. 15, no. 2, pp. 201-225, 2014).

Mapping the unmapped is a form of investigation, activism, and resistance. One example is Unnayan, a collective formed in Kolkata in the mid-1970s, which mapped unauthorized settlements invisible on government maps, making residents' housing claims visible.

Sterling Quinn's research on "Geographies of Empty Spaces" illustrates how different maps have different information voids, comparing digital and paper maps of the same areas.

Investigators should triangulate data by consulting varied sources, including paper maps and locally created maps, rather than relying on any single map as definitive.

Questions to ask:

- Whose maps does the investigation rely on, and what missing data might exist?
- Are certain parts of the map blurred, pixelated, or available only in low resolution? Satellite maps commonly blur strategic locations such as military airbases, refugee camps, power plants, and important infrastructure. The Federation of American Scientists has investigated how selective blurring can reveal the location of secret facilities.
- Are the maps intentionally outdated? Some maps intentionally depict an older time period. The Associated Press investigated post-9/11 obfuscation in US maps.
- Are there vague or misleading labels? Military and intelligence assets are often omitted from UK maps or labeled as "farms," "depot," "works," or "disused airfield" (C. Perkins and M. Dodge, "Satellite Imagery and the Spectacle of Secret Spaces," Geoforum, vol. 40, no. 4, pp. 546-560, 2009).

Some omissions arise from technical and design decisions rather than intent. "Cartographic generalization" in Geographic Information Systems (GIS) controls how much detail appears in a map, with trade-offs shaped by human and automated decisions. This can produce the "Baltimore phenomenon": the city of Baltimore is often omitted from maps at certain scales because larger neighboring cities crowd it out, even though smaller cities appear at those same scales.

The chapter also notes that not everything can or should be captured. Communities and actors may wish to remain off the map for safety reasons. Investigators must acknowledge "data refusal" and genuinely obtain consent. The chapter cites research by E. Tuck and K.W. Yang ("Unbecoming Claims: Pedagogies of Refusal in Qualitative Research") and A. Simpson ("On Ethnographic Refusal: Indigeneity, 'Voice' and Colonial Citizenship"). Location data is particularly revealing: controversies from the Muslim Pro App and phone location data mapping are cited as examples of the risks.

### 3. Mapping communities with careful crowdsourcing

Investigators must consider whether they are mapping their own community or one they are not part of. Questions to ask: whose maps are you relying on? Whose knowledge and interpretation are you using? Who are you including and excluding?

If working outside your own community, consider partnering with community members from the research design stage onward. The chapter recommends Annita Lucchesi's work on Indigenous Mapping principles in "Spatial Data and (De)Colonization: Incorporating Indigenous Data Sovereignty Principles into Cartographic Research," and ideas on co-produced ethnography in "Decolonizing Ethnography: Undocumented Immigrants and New Directions in Social Science" by Carolina Alonso Bejarano, Lucia Lopez Juarez, Mirian A. Mijangos Garcia, and Daniel M. Goldstein (Duke University Press, 2019).

Critical thinking should extend to open-source and crowdsourced projects. Questions include: who defines the questions and parameters? On whose terms is information collected and analyzed? What input is actually allowed from participants? Who decides what gets mapped? Who owns the data and knowledge produced? Is the crowdsourced data from a representative sample? What languages are the maps available in?

The chapter notes that even apparently open platforms face challenges: Google Maps hosts thousands of fake company listings each month and carries various biases despite attempts at openness. The authors of "Indigenous Data Sovereignty" caution that data sovereignty principles must be actively incorporated into cartographic research.

Crowdsourcing is not inherently good. Critical reflection about its limitations, combined with collaboration with experts, is essential (see A. Basiri, M. Haklay, G. Foody, and P. Mooney, "Crowdsourced Geospatial Data Quality: Challenges and Future Directions," International Journal of Geographical Information Science, vol. 33, no. 8, pp. 1588-1593, 2019).

## Maps and UnMaps: five types of investigative maps

The chapter identifies five non-exhaustive, overlapping uses for maps in investigative projects.

### The Patterned Map (concerned with analysis)

Some maps place pattern detection at the forefront rather than navigation. Maps can be compared over time, examined across space, and overlaid with data to reveal trends, relationships, and patterns.

Example: The Detroit Geographic Expedition's 1968 map "Where Commuters Run Over Black Children on the Pointes-Downtown Track" plotted traffic deaths of children in Detroit, making a pattern of racial geography visible.

Example: In the 1850s, physician John Snow and collaborator Charles Cheffins mapped cholera cases in a particularly bad outbreak in Soho, London, using mortality report data from the Registrar General Office and interviews with local residents. The maps showed cases clustering around a specific contaminated water pump. This evidence eventually persuaded the local council to act and persuaded the medical profession to take the waterborne theory of cholera seriously.

### The Map of Reimagining (concerned with counter-mapping)

Since maps are constructed, they can be deconstructed and reconstructed to resist the status quo, rectify historical omissions, or imagine an alternative future.

The Detroit Geographic Expedition emphasized "oughtness maps," maps showing how things are and how things ought to be. Their first publication, "Field Notes I," states that "after all, it is not the function of geographers to merely map the earth, but to change it."

The Native Land Digital project is a living map of Indigenous territories, languages, and treaties in North America.

The "Secret Countries" section of the cartographic issue of the Chimurenga Chronic envisions an alternative mapping of Africa, moving beyond nation-state representations and instead offering arrangements of territories tied together through shared history, trade, and other flows, inspired by Achille Mbembe's "At the Edge of World: Boundaries, Territoriality, and Sovereignty in Africa."

### The Power Map (concerned with mapping relationships)

Power mapping, also called relational mapping, identifies who holds what type of power in a community, locating relevant parties including allies and decision-makers.

Example: The Anti-Eviction Mapping Project uses power and relational mapping in its work on displacement and eviction.

### The Storytelling Map (concerned with communicating)

Some maps have storytelling as their primary focus, humanizing data and conveying the scale or severity of issues.

Example: The Views from the North Atlas, produced by Nunavut Sivuniksavut students and Carleton University in Ottawa, situates interviews between students and elders alongside photographs, archival materials, and cartographic features to create a living map of Inuit culture.

Example: The Anti-Eviction Mapping Project's "Narratives of Displacement and Resistance" places incidents of eviction alongside oral histories in several US cities. Each red dot represents a case of eviction. Each turquoise dot links to an interview.

Example: Geographer and artist Levi Westerveld's "Those Who Did Not Cross" maps individuals who died attempting to cross borders, covering the period 2005-2015.

Example: VisualizingPalestine's infographic of the Israeli government's ID system, informed by Helga Tawil-Souri's article "Uneven Borders, Coloured (Im)mobilities: ID Cards in Palestine/Israel" (Social Text, 2011, 29(2(107)), pp. 67-97), visualizes how the ID system stratifies and controls various Palestinian communities.

### The Real-Time Map of Protest (concerned with mapping the now)

Protest maps accompany conflicts and protests in real time to share information, document events, and organize logistics as a movement unfolds.

Example: HKmap.live mapped the 2019-2020 city-wide protests in Hong Kong in real time.

Example: ScanMap, built by the activist group Radio12 using Open Source Intelligence (OSINT) techniques, is a free tool that maps hotspots of protest, police locations, and blockades in various cities. It emerged during the Black Lives Matter protests in summer 2020.

These maps are designed to be ephemeral. The chapter cautions that digital maps often extract data from users, and centralized storage of personal data can pose risks from data leaks or government requests for law enforcement access. Designers should consider what data they store and whether it puts anyone in danger.

## Staying critical

The chapter closes by quoting writer Taiyon J. Coleman (2017): "We use maps to find our way in the world, to locate ourselves in relation to others, to measure distance and record change. Maps are inherently contextual, which can make them seem old-fashioned in a culture that values immediacy, one that operates through image and spectacle. The image is a mask, a face, a front, an arrow. The map is its opposite, not an index of the world but a way of relating to it."

Maps can reveal and hide. They can reinforce but also reimagine. Investigators who incorporate maps into their work must keep a critical eye on how those maps summarize space and time, whose perspectives they encode, and what they leave out.
