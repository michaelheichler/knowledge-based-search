## What bio-investigation is

A bio-investigation is the process of locating, collecting, and analyzing samples and measurements from the environment to provide evidence for broader investigations. The chapter frames the living environment as a record of past and present events. Investigators examine polluted soils (soil, groundwater, soil gas), sludge and sediments (marine, estuarine, fluvial), ambient and workplace air, and biological indicators such as plants, animals, and microorganisms. The goal is not only to gather evidence but to document the process so that knowledge can be shared beyond the original investigator.

The chapter uses cooking as a sustained metaphor: just as cooking skill develops through practice, collaboration, and gradually better tools, investigative skill grows the same way. Simple experiments, such as extracting DNA from strawberries or split peas using household materials in about 15 minutes, are offered as entry points that demystify scientific methods.

## Why communities conduct bio-investigations

Environmental crises, industrial accidents, and climate events create urgent questions that communities cannot always rely on official sources to answer. Two real cases frame the chapter's rationale. In June 2019 in Chateaubourg, France, an industrial abattoir discharged blood into a local stream. In September 2019 in Rouen, France, a petrochemical facility fire created information uncertainty while political interests shaped the public narrative. In both situations, pre-established collaborative networks of citizen investigators were positioned to coordinate sampling and provide independent evidence. The chapter argues that communities need to understand environmental information affecting their surroundings and that local investigators should develop both technical methods and regular collaborative relationships before crises occur.

## Safety and preparation

The chapter is explicit that investigators should never work alone and should plan operations thoroughly before entering the field.

Individual precautions include: planning the investigation in advance, examining the location beforehand (including using online resources to identify surveillance cameras), using appropriate protective equipment such as respiratory masks and latex gloves, and maintaining digital security.

Collective precautions include: working in pairs or groups, ensuring all members are equipped and briefed, documenting all work, and developing safe teamwork habits.

Legal awareness requires understanding what sampling authorizations apply, whether the site contains protected biodiversity, and whether local authorities or NGOs have issued relevant health alerts. Investigators should avoid destroying or polluting natural areas.

The chapter notes that investigators are typically observed by humans or devices and recommends researching camera locations in advance. It references publicly available surveillance maps for cities including Brussels (bruxelles.sous-surveillance.net), Seattle (seattle.sous-surveillance.net), and Rennes (rennes.sous-surveillance.net). Smartphones and IoT devices can also reveal location through geolocation and automatic Wi-Fi connections.

A mnemonic organizes pre-field preparation across eight categories: Person(s) involved, Equipment, Workwear, Communication devices, Food and hydration, Driving assignments, Course of action, and Appointments. Group movement in the field is planned around: Direction, Meeting point, Itinerary, and Training. Situational assessment before launching work uses the framework: Situation, Objective, From idea to action, Implementation, and Coordination.

A theoretical example illustrates the full structure: after a factory fire, three teams of three people each collect 12 water samples from three locations, spending one hour per location, wearing respiratory masks and latex gloves, communicating between teams using the Element app (formerly Riot, an encrypted messaging application), and storing samples in coolers.

## Protocols and recipes

The chapter calls a sampling procedure a "recipe" and argues that drafting it before fieldwork minimizes errors and provides context for interpreting results. Recipes should be written under free licenses and shared. The chapter distinguishes four forms of sharing: tutorials (focused on learning), practical guides (step-by-step instructions), explanation forms (background and context), and reference guides (complete and verified projects). Trusted reviewers should test protocols before use.

The mindset of a bio-investigator links a chain of activities: identifying methods and tools, inventorying sampling areas, investigating particular situations, making field records and collecting samples, analyzing samples within defined areas, interpreting analyses, and informing others beyond the immediate initiative. The chapter names this practice metrology through citizen investigation, meaning the application of measurement science to investigative goals.

Every protocol must cover five procedures: geolocate each sample, measure and plot the sampling area, take photos or video of the work, write descriptions and commentary, and make the protocol public when safe to do so.

The chapter introduces the term "routine" for the documented lifecycle of a single sample from collection through analysis. Each routine covers: collection, storage, contextualization, information indexing, and transport. The sequencing of these steps is described as the chain of custody, and maintaining it is the basis for confidence in sample results. The documentation is compared to a certified envelope that validates sample integrity at every stage.

Quality control uses Ishikawa's cause-and-effect analysis across five dimensions: Material, Equipment, Method, Manpower (personnel), and Environment.

## Choosing sampling areas

Overpass-turbo is the primary recommended tool for identifying sampling locations. It is a data-filtering interface for OpenStreetMap (OSM) that allows investigators to search for specific features, such as schools, drinking water points, or factories, build and run queries, and produce standardized maps with associated source data. The chapter describes a scenario of preparing for field sampling after a factory fire in which Overpass-turbo is used to identify critical intervention points.

OpenInfraMap provides accessible maps based on OpenStreetMap data showing energy infrastructure, specifically oil, gas, and electricity networks. The chapter identifies energy infrastructure as offering triple investigative interest: understanding the landscape's energy networks, locating information sources relevant to an investigation, and recognizing ecological influence on local environments.

## Community engagement

Before entering a site, investigators should meet with local residents, obtain consent, and conduct interviews in written, audio, or video format. The chapter emphasizes building relationships with indigenous communities where relevant. During environmental crises, misinformation is common, and the chapter calls for vigilance about potentially manipulated information. The value of the exchange is presented as mutual: investigators share findings, and communities contribute local knowledge and historical context.

## What is sampled and how

The chapter identifies several categories of environmental sample.

Soil: Sampling involves creating diagrams of test areas, using plants as markers for locating sampling points, measuring soil pH (acidity or alkalinity), and collecting samples for DNA analysis with specific handling and storage protocols.

Water: Sampling covers rivers, aquatic plants used as indicators, water reserves and ponds, and ocean or coastal environments. The chapter mentions plankton collection and documenting plastic waste on coastlines. A coastal pollution scenario in Brittany involves oil spills and blooms of chlorophytes (green algae) whose decomposition releases a toxic gas.

Air: Methods include DIY monitoring tools paired with citizen cartography efforts and collecting atmospheric samples for external analysis.

Biological markers: Lichens are named as pollution indicators. Flowers and yeasts are mentioned as biological markers. Products that people consume are also examined as part of the investigative process.

## Analysis and defined terms

The chapter includes a glossary of technical vocabulary. Defined terms include: bioindication, bioaccumulation, biocommunity, biodiversity, DNA, DNA sequencing, genome, metrology, mnemonic, morphoimprint, oligoelements, pH measurement, photosynthesis, routine, sample, sampling, and supply chain. Other defined terms include: beaker, barcode, commons, distilled water, grid, grid map, totem, and IoT (Internet of Things).

Analysis of collected samples is described as something investigators can conduct themselves or arrange through qualified external organizations.

## Core principles

Bio-investigation is a collective activity that should not be done alone. Protocols and documentation are not optional. They constitute the integrity of the investigation. The investigative process should be shared under open licenses so others can reproduce and build on it. The living environment, including its non-human members, is an active carrier of evidence that citizen investigators can access with basic tools, careful preparation, and collaborative relationships.
