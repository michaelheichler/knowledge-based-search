This chapter (labeled Chapter 47 in the source text) addresses three interconnected topics: how to structure an organizational policy for online investigations, how to reason through ethical questions that arise during OSINT work, and how to build a career in the field.

## Investigations Policy

Every organization conducting technical investigations needs a written policy. The authors recommend keeping it to one or two pages. Avoid tying the language to specific platforms or tools, because those change quickly and platform-specific rules become obsolete or restrictive almost immediately. Instead, build policy around techniques and principles, drawing language from whatever policies the organization already uses for traditional investigations (training standards, chain of custody, supervision, equipment use).

A sound policy framework includes these sections: Definitions, Appropriate Use, Approval Process, Training, Retention/Audit, Approved Forms, Revision History, and Appendix. Template language is available at https://inteltechniques.com/osintbook11/.

For smaller teams that do not have a formal policy structure, a set of standard operating procedures (SOPs) is an acceptable alternative. The content is essentially the same but formatted less formally, typically as an agency memorandum approved by a manager above the front-line supervisor. The value of having any documented standard becomes clear when a controversial case puts the team under scrutiny.

If a policy must address "interaction" with subjects (friending, messaging), the language should be as non-specific as possible. Restrictions on covert accounts should be resisted through education of management and legal advisors, because decades of case law supports undercover operations in traditional investigations and the same reasoning applies online.

## Child Sexual Abuse Material (CSAM) Policy

Every OSINT team needs a specific procedure for the possibility of encountering CSAM. Relevant U.S. law appears in Title 18, Part 1, Chapter 110, Section 2258A. The chapter gives seven steps for investigators who encounter CSAM without prior authorization:

1. **Do not capture it.** Copying CSAM can harm the victims and may constitute a crime regardless of intent unless the investigator operates under special legal authority.
2. **Notify a supervisor.** Start a paper trail immediately to document that the incident was handled properly.
3. **Report to law enforcement.** Reporting requirements vary by jurisdiction, but the ethical obligation is universal.
4. **Report to NCMEC.** Submit a report to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children at missingkids.org/gethelpnow/cybertipline, and save a screenshot of the submission.
5. **Report to the platform.** Time this carefully. If the platform may be involved in the criminal activity, or if law enforcement has not yet disclosed the case, platform notification should wait.
6. **Document the incident.** Record date, time, investigator, supervisor, URLs, and a description of the content. Do not include images or videos in the documentation.
7. **Address wellness.** Exposure to CSAM can cause lasting emotional harm. Consulting a professional counselor after such exposure is appropriate and encouraged.

Non-law-enforcement investigators who participate in crowd-sourced missing-persons or child exploitation investigations should ask organizers directly about CSAM procedures before participating.

## Ethics

The ethical core of OSINT work is privacy. The chapter frames the central question as: given the seriousness of the matter under investigation, what is the appropriate scope of intrusion into a subject's public data?

Two scenarios illustrate the range. In the first, a report of online threats against a public official turns out on review to involve mean-spirited comments that do not articulate a true threat. The appropriate response is to report that finding and stop. Digging further into that person's life is not justified. In the second, an IP address is linked to a large volume of child pornography flowing through a Tor exit node. Even though the person at that address may be unaware, the seriousness of the potential harm justifies exhausting all reasonable public data sources before drawing conclusions.

The chapter also addresses data that is publicly accessible but not intentionally shared, such as an improperly configured Elasticsearch database reachable without credentials. The legal standing may be acceptable, but whether to use such data should be governed by the investigator's intent and the purpose of the investigation.

Receiving the initial assignment in writing is recommended in all cases, whether in the public or private sector. Written assignments establish a record of the request and its justification, which protects both the investigator and the organization.

### Deception

Deception in OSINT takes two main forms:

**Covert accounts.** Using covert accounts violates the terms of service of most social media platforms, but those accounts are necessary for effective platform queries. The deception involved is relatively low in invasiveness because the investigator is one of many anonymous accounts and is not directly interacting with other users.

**Infiltration.** Joining groups, friending subjects, or otherwise gaining a position inside a target community is more invasive because it changes the dynamics of the group. It is ethically justified when the value to the investigation clearly outweighs the intrusion (for example, joining a criminal forum to deanonymize child predators). The test is whether the investigator can articulate the justification before initiating online contact.

The chapter closes the ethics section by stating that ongoing training in both OSINT techniques and privacy law is itself an ethical obligation. Sloppy casework and ignorance of legal requirements harm the field and the people it serves.

## Professional Development

### Formal Education

No specific degree is required for OSINT work. Practitioners come from widely varied educational backgrounds, including degrees entirely unrelated to intelligence or investigations, and many received training in the military or through employer programs. Some employers set degree requirements, but these are often arbitrary and may be waived in favor of work experience.

The skills that matter most are often domain-general: the ability to organize information and communicate clearly, both verbally and in writing. Investigators who cannot present findings in a professional written report will struggle to advance regardless of their research ability.

### Experience

Experience is the most valuable qualification and the hardest to obtain early in a career. The chapter identifies five paths for building it:

- **Military service.** Provides initial intelligence training and may include language study and cross-cultural exposure.
- **Law enforcement or government.** Many agencies have intelligence units, though assignment to them typically requires years of general service first.
- **Private sector entry points.** Departments like loss prevention have lower requirements but still involve investigative work.
- **Creating an internal program.** Writing a proposal and demonstrating OSINT capability to management can create a new role within an existing organization.
- **Volunteering.** Unpaid internships and volunteer roles with teams that do this work can lead to full-time employment.

Early-career investigators should expect assignments outside their area of interest. Entry-level and unrelated roles provide experience that compounds over time.

### OSINT-Specific Training

A motivated person can build a solid skillset at low or no cost through self-directed research, since the research skills being developed are also the tools for finding new training material. Autodidactic learning (figuring out problems independently) builds investigative problem-solving capacity in ways that receiving pre-packaged answers does not.

Premium options (live courses, bootcamps, online video training) accelerate learning when time is short or budget is available. Live instruction with hands-on labs is typically the fastest path. Online video training sits between live instruction and self-study: it allows techniques to be demonstrated but usually lacks hands-on lab components.

IntelTechniques offers this book, online video training at IntelTechniques.net, and live sessions. Before purchasing any training program, the chapter advises using OSINT techniques themselves to evaluate instructors and course content, including searching communities where people discuss courses.
