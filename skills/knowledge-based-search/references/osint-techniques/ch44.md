## Overview

This chapter defines four categories of exposed data, explains why investigators benefit from holding their own offline copies, and lists specific offensive and defensive use cases for each category. The chapter appears as "Chapter Thirty-Six: Investigative Benefits" in the ebook rendering.

## Four Data Categories

**Leaks** are data that entered public reach accidentally, without a criminal act. Examples include legally acquired voter rolls that were shared online, marketing databases archived by third parties, and misconfigured databases that expose their contents to anyone who knows the URL. Because no server was illegally compromised, this category is the most likely to be legally accessible.

**Breaches** result from criminal intrusion: an attacker compromises a data source, copies its contents, and publishes the result. The Twitter and LinkedIn breaches are cited as examples, where email addresses and passwords were stolen and released. Tens of thousands of such breaches now exist, introducing billions of plain-text passwords into public circulation. Downloading publicly available breach data may not create legal liability in many jurisdictions, but republishing it might. An organization's internal policies may also prohibit accessing it. The chapter notes that possession and research of this data is common even among law-abiding investigators.

**Logs** (also called "Stealer Logs" or "Password Logs") are data extracted from victim machines by malware. A criminal tricks a user into installing a virus that harvests stored passwords, session cookies, documents, photos, and other sensitive content, then transmits that data to a server the criminal controls. Hundreds of thousands of victims' logs are uploaded daily. Because logs come directly from infected machines rather than from a compromised database, they often expose passwords for people who do not appear in any known breach, and the content tends to be more current and accurate.

**Ransomware** data results from criminals who infiltrate a company network, copy all data, encrypt the original files, and demand payment for decryption while threatening to publish the stolen copy if the ransom is not paid. New stolen company data, including passwords, sensitive documents, and passport scans, is published daily.

## Offensive Use Cases

The chapter frames "offensive" use as active investigation of a target.

**Home addresses.** Subjects who scrub their names from people-search websites still leave traces when ordering products or registering to vote. Breach and leak data frequently discloses true home addresses that no other online resource reveals.

**Alias account identification.** A subject using a disposable "burner" email account may reuse passwords across that account and a real one. If a breach exposes the burner address with password "badguy432!@" and another breach exposes a different email with "badguy432%$", that similarity is treated as an investigative lead. Repeated password reuse across multiple breaches can allow an investigator to link alias accounts to a real identity.

**Social network account attribution.** When a service suffers a breach, hidden email addresses and phone numbers associated with usernames are often exposed, immediately revealing the true identity behind an otherwise anonymous account.

**Telephone number owner identification.** Prepaid cellular plans and VOIP numbers resist standard OSINT lookup. Breach data regularly surfaces ownership details for these numbers that no people-search site would return.

**Machine identifiers.** Stealer logs can contain a unique hardware identifier and a screenshot of the victim's desktop, information that no public website provides. The chapter describes stealer logs as the most beneficial data type acquired over the past year.

**Additional email addresses.** Searching a target's known username in breach data often surfaces other email addresses constructed from the same username pattern, opening new investigative pivot points.

**Business intelligence.** For investigations into a company that has suffered a breach, the stolen data itself can reveal customer counts, encryption practices, and internal details that no public business listing would contain.

## Defensive Use Cases

The chapter frames "defensive" use as protecting clients or organizations.

**Identifying stolen assets.** A company that has suffered a breach should locate and obtain the publicly circulating data to understand the true scope of what was taken, independent of criminal claims or demands. This is described as one of the most requested client services.

**Disclosure notification support.** Data breach notification laws require companies to inform affected individuals. Knowing exactly what data is circulating in criminal channels helps determine which victims must be notified and under what regulatory requirements.

**Intercepting data before abuse.** Daily monitoring of new leaks, breaches, logs, and ransomware allows an investigator to alert a client about newly exposed information before criminals begin exploiting it, enabling the client to block access proactively.

## Rationale for an Offline Copy

The chapter argues strongly for maintaining a personal offline database rather than relying on third-party search services. Online breach query services often start free and shift to paid models, may be prohibited by organizational policy, require account creation that exposes the investigator's query patterns to the service operator, and can shut down without warning. An offline copy eliminates all of these dependencies.
