## What Stealer Logs Are

Stealer logs are data packages created when a malicious virus infects a Windows computer. The victim typically installs the virus by visiting a malicious website or running pirated software. Once active, the virus extracts valuable data from the machine and transfers it to criminal networks, where it is traded as a commodity. The most historically common families were Raccoon Stealer, Redline Stealer, and Vidar Stealer. Criminal groups constantly rebrand stolen data from one another, and new groups emerge every week. The data is used to hijack online accounts, steal cryptocurrency, and make unauthorized purchases. The author's organization ingests over one million logs per day.

## Structure of a Single Stealer Log

A complete log for one victim is a folder whose name encodes the victim's country (two-letter code), a Hardware Identifier (HWID), and a timestamp. The HWID is the unique identifier Microsoft assigns when Windows is first installed.

Inside the folder:

- `-----000.txt`: Contact and pricing information for the criminal group distributing the log.
- `00000000000.jpg`: The branding logo of the stealer product.
- `Autofills/` (e.g., `Google_[Chrome]_Default.txt`): All data the victim allowed their browser to save from web forms, including name, address, email, phone, date of birth, vehicle VIN, and partial credit card details.
- `Cookies/`: Session cookies from browsers such as Chrome and Edge. These can be used to hijack authenticated sessions even without knowing the account password.
- `DomainDetects.txt`: A quick-reference list of high-value domains present in the log (e.g., PayPal, Amazon, Steam), categorized by priority. Criminals use this to quickly identify logs worth targeting.
- `ImportantAutofills.txt`: Parsed subset of autofill data most useful for fraud, often including date of birth and Social Security number.
- `InstalledBrowsers.txt`: All browsers and version numbers on the machine.
- `InstalledSoftware.txt`: All installed applications with version numbers. For investigators this reveals the VPN service used, hardware model, and software preferences, which can support further profiling and social engineering analysis.
- `Passwords.txt`: All passwords stored in all browsers, displayed in plain text, with associated URL and username. The author warns this is exactly why users should not use native browser password storage.
- `Screenshot.jpg`: A screen capture taken at the moment of infection, showing the victim's open windows, browser tabs, toolbar shortcuts, and avatar.
- `UserInformation.txt`: IP address, username, country, zip code, city and state, HWID, screen resolution, time zone, operating system version, CPU, GPU, and RAM.

Some logs also contain a `FileGrabber/` folder with files taken from the victim's Desktop and Documents directories, typically Word documents and PDFs.

## Why HWID Matters

The HWID is a pivot point. If an investigator obtains the HWID from one log, it can be searched across an entire log collection to find additional records from the same machine, potentially linking multiple accounts and identities to a single person.

## Investigative Value

A suspect who was also a stealer log victim exposes all email addresses, usernames, passwords, and device information in one package. The author offers two case examples. In the first, a harasser using a throwaway email address was identified because that address appeared in stealer logs alongside a device name (Desktop-u3ty6). Querying the device name returned dozens of email addresses and passwords from the same machine, revealing the suspect as a 15-year-old whose computer was infected after downloading a pirated anti-virus. In the second, a financial fraud suspect using a burner email address was himself a stealer log victim. Querying that email returned his full log, including a FileGrabber folder containing a recently modified resume. The investigation concluded in under five minutes.

The author's firm treats this as a defensive tool as well, calling their log monitoring function a "Pre-Crime Unit." They have identified client machines infected with stealer viruses within days of infection and notified affected organizations.

## Acquiring Stealer Logs via Telegram

Telegram is the primary distribution platform. Dozens of channels offer free logs to promote paid services. The browser-based preview URL format is `https://t.me/s/[channelname]`. The direct link format (which prompts the Telegram app) is `https://t.me/[channelname]`. Within the Telegram app, channels are found by searching their name, which begins with `@`. Downloads come directly from Telegram servers at full connection speed, avoiding the throttling of third-party hosts.

The channel "Moon Cloud | Free Logs" (username: Moon_Log) aggregates logs from DaisyCloud, LulzsecCloudLogs, Tiny Cloud, and dozens of others, with over a terabyte of compressed logs in that channel alone. CRYPTON 2.0 is another example of a channel offering free promotional samples.

Additional known channels at the time of writing: Base Cloud, Boosty, BradMax, Bugatti Cloud, CBank, Cloud Logs, DB_Cloud, Eternal Logs, Expert Logs, Hub Head, HUBLOGS, Keeper Cloud, Link Cloud, Logs Arthouse, Logs Cloud, Luffich Cloud, Luxury Logs, NinjaByte, Observer Cloud, OneLogs, Redlogs Cloud, Ruban Cloud, Snatch Logs, Syxap's Cloud, Tor Logs, Wild Logs, and X-Cloud. Channels appear and disappear daily. The author recommends starting with Google.

Searching Telegram directly by the name of a breached service can also surface fresh downloads.

BreachForums is a secondary source. Useful queries:

```
site:breachforums.is "Url-Log-Pass"
site:breachforums.is "mega.nz" "stealer log"
site:anonfiles.com "logs"
site:mediafire.com "logs"
```

Google queries such as `"stealer logs" "download"` and `"stealer logs" "Redline"` may surface results but most lead to marketplaces requiring accounts.

## Two Approaches: Full Logs vs. Summaries

### Full Stealer Logs

Full logs contain screenshots, cookies, FileGrabber documents, and folder structures that associate multiple accounts with one victim. They are large. A single compressed sample may be 677 MB and contain 1,101 victims. The author estimates his organization holds over 40 TB of stealer log data, ingesting over a terabyte some days.

To extract only the password data from a large collection of decompressed full logs, the following Terminal command navigates to each file matching the pattern and combines them (case-insensitive, catching both `passwords.txt` and `Passwords.txt`):

```
find . -iname '*pass*.txt' -exec cat {} \; > ~/Downloads/Passwords.txt
```

For ImportantAutofills:

```
find . -iname 'importantautofills.txt' -exec cat {} \; > ~/Downloads/ImportantAutofills.txt
```

To append a datestamp to the output filename:

```
timestamp=$(date +%Y-%m-%d) && find . -iname '*pass*.txt' -exec cat {} \; > ~/Downloads/Passwords.$timestamp.txt
```

Applied to a 30 GB test dataset (over 37,000 victim logs), these commands produced a 200 MB Passwords.txt and a 20 MB ImportantAutofills.txt.

### Querying Merged Password Files with Ripgrep

A standard Ripgrep search returns only the matching line. Because stealer log credentials span multiple lines (URL on one line, Username on the next, Password on the third), the `-A2 -B2` context flags are required:

```
rg -aFiN -A2 -B2 test123@gmail.com
```

This returns two lines before and after each match, producing the full URL, username, and password together.

### Stealer Log Summaries

Summary files strip out screenshots, cookies, and documents, retaining only credentials, one per line. Format:

```
https://www.amazon.com/ap/signin:test123@gmail.com:pass456
```

Compared with the full-log format:

```
URL: https://www.amazon.com/ap/signin
Username: test123@gmail.com
Password: pass456
```

A 1 GB compressed full log becomes a 10 MB summary. Summaries cannot link multiple accounts to one victim (since all credentials from all victims are combined into one file), and they lack screenshots and documents. However, they have already been cleaned and are far faster to acquire and store.

The channel "Moon Cloud | Combo/txtbase" aggregates summaries from many sources. The channel "Link Cloud" (username: @linkpass) periodically posts large batches. At the time of writing, one such batch was 6.7 GB compressed, decompressing to 71.1 GB of text. Combined with other sources that day, the author held 76.5 GB of text data from a single session.

## Daily Workflow for Summary Files

After downloading all summary text files from the Telegram Desktop downloads folder:

```bash
timestamp=$(date +%Y-%m-%d-%H-%M)
cd ~/Downloads/Telegram\ Desktop
cat *.txt > Logs.csv
rm *.txt && mv Logs.csv Logs.txt
LC_ALL=C sort -u -b -i -f -S 80% --parallel=8 Logs.txt > Logs-$timestamp.txt
rm Logs.txt && mv Logs-$timestamp.txt ~/Downloads/
```

This merges all files, removes duplicates using case-insensitive sort, and saves the result as a timestamped file. The author reduced 80 GB of raw downloads to 57 GB after deduplication in one demonstration run. The result is named in the pattern `Logs-2023-11-28-09-00.txt`.

## Weekly Merge Workflow

When daily Logs files accumulate, merge them into a single deduplicated file:

```bash
cd ~/Downloads && timestamp=$(date +%Y-%m-%d-%H-%M)
LC_ALL=C sort -u -b -i -f -m Logs* > Merged-$timestamp.txt
rm Logs*
```

Move the merged file to a dedicated external SSD (volume named DATA, folder named Logs):

```bash
mv Merged* /Volumes/DATA/Logs      # macOS
mv Merged* /media/$USER/DATA/Logs  # Linux
```

The `-m` flag tells sort to merge already-sorted files rather than re-sort them, which is faster.

## Merging New Data with the Primary Collection

To combine the current week's new sorted Logs files with the existing primary collection on the SSD, producing a single deduplicated file and discarding everything else:

macOS:
```bash
cd ~/Downloads
LC_ALL=C sort -u -b -i -f -m Logs* /Volumes/DATA/Logs/* > Current.txt
rm Logs* /Volumes/DATA/Logs/*
mv Current.txt /Volumes/DATA/Logs/Current.txt
```

Linux:
```bash
cd ~/Downloads
LC_ALL=C sort -u -b -i -f -m Logs* /media/$USER/DATA/Logs/* > Current.txt
rm Logs* /media/$USER/DATA/Logs/*
mv Current.txt /media/$USER/DATA/Logs/Current.txt
```

The result is a single growing `Current.txt` on the SSD with no duplicate entries. The author's collection at the time of writing was 300 GB of unique credentials, containing billions of entries. He notes that Moon Cloud and Link Cloud held many terabytes of data dating to August 2023 (with logs going back to 2015), and that replicating his full collection in one day is feasible. He also notes that this data is equivalent to what commercial services sell, making paid access unnecessary.

## Combo Files

Combo files are credential-only extracts (typically email:password format, no URL) that circulate alongside full summaries, usually under 20 MB. Most of the time their contents overlap with the summary files, but occasionally they contain credentials not found in any log. They are processed with the same commands but kept separate from logs to avoid format mixing. Replace "Logs" with "Combos" in the variable names and filenames:

```bash
timestamp=$(date +%Y-%m-%d-%H-%M)
cd ~/Downloads/Telegram\ Desktop
cat *.txt > Combos.csv
rm *.txt && mv Combos.csv Combos.txt
LC_ALL=C sort -u -b -i -f -S 80% --parallel=8 Combos.txt > Combos-$timestamp.txt
rm Combos.txt && mv Combos-$timestamp.txt ~/Downloads/
```

If forced to choose one type, the author recommends logs over combos because logs carry more data per credential entry.

## Downloading from Mega.nz with Megatools

Mega (mega.nz) provides 50 GB of free storage and is frequently used to host large log dumps, but free accounts face bandwidth caps. VPN users often share throttled IP addresses with others abusing the free tier. The command-line tool Megatools bypasses some of these restrictions by tracking position separately from browser session cookies and retrying automatically after throttle events.

Install:

```bash
# macOS
brew install megatools

# Linux: download from https://megatools.megous.com/builds/builds/

# Windows
choco install megatools
```

Usage from Terminal:

```bash
cd ~/Downloads
megatools dl MEGA_URL
```

When bandwidth is restricted, the command retries automatically every couple of minutes. Switching VPN servers during a throttle event can restore the connection. This method also avoids browser memory problems that can cause corrupted or incomplete downloads of large files.

## Operational Cautions

The author states that Telegram-based stealer log acquisition carries the highest malware infection risk of any technique in the book. He recommends running Telegram only on a dedicated machine used exclusively for leaks, breaches, logs, and ransomware work. Investigators should confirm that organizational policy permits this activity, comply with applicable laws, never attempt to log in to any victim account, and never publish or disseminate the data.

## The Author's Argument for This Work

The author acknowledges that publishing these methods might enable misuse, but argues that criminals already possess and use this data without tutorials and could teach more sophisticated methods. He frames the defensive case as follows: investigators who collect and monitor stealer logs can detect client infections quickly, notify affected organizations, and disable compromised credentials before criminals act. He suggests that a company such as PayPal could dedicate staff to identifying its own credentials in stealer logs and disabling them proactively. He argues that broad adoption of this defensive posture would reduce the usefulness of the data to criminals over time.
