## Overview

This chapter covers OSINT investigation on Twitter (rebranded as X in 2024, but referred to throughout as Twitter). The core premise is that a logged-in Twitter account is required for full access. Without one, posts are hidden and chronological ordering is unreliable. Investigators should maintain at least one prepared Twitter account at all times. Twitter has over 415 million users total and over 335 million active monthly users. Most posts are limited to 280 characters, with longer content available to paid subscribers.

## Official Search Interface

The standard search bar at x.com/explore is the entry point for all queries. The advanced search page (x.com/search-advanced) adds fields for exact phrases, hashtags, language filters, date ranges, and directional filters (from, to, mentioning). However, the author does not recommend relying on the advanced search page. All of its capabilities can be replicated with manual search operators typed directly into the search bar, which is preferable for documentation purposes (especially when findings may be used in court) and for enabling live monitoring.

Topic searches through advanced search are limited to roughly the previous seven to ten days. Individual profiles display posts further back, as far as you are willing to scroll.

The five tabs returned by any Twitter search are: Top (popular results, least useful for investigation), Latest (reverse chronological, useful for topics), People (best tab for locating a specific user by name), Media (photos and videos matching the query), and Lists (curated account collections).

## Search Operators

Twitter supports a set of search operators that produce precise results.

**Directional operators.** `from:username` returns only outgoing posts from that account. `to:username` returns posts publicly sent to that account, including messages the account holder cannot suppress from this view. These can be combined: `to:IntelTechniques from:protonprivacy` isolates a specific conversation thread.

**Reply filters.** `from:username filter:replies` shows only posts that were replies. Prefixing with a minus sign (`-filter:replies`) shows only posts that were not replies, which tends to be more revealing.

**Media and link filters.** A direct URL such as `https://x.com/IntelTechniques/media/` displays only posts with embedded media. The operator `filter:links` restricts results to posts containing a URL.

**Engagement filters.** `min_faves:150` limits results to posts receiving at least 150 likes. `min_replies:100` limits to posts receiving at least 100 replies.

**Highlights, Lists, Topics.** Specific URL patterns expose additional data: `/highlights/` shows pinned posts curated by the account holder, `/lists/` shows lists the account created, `/lists/memberships` shows lists created by others that include the account (something the target cannot control), and `/topics` shows interest topics the user has added to their feed. List members and followers can be queried using the list's numeric ID: `https://x.com/i/lists/[ID]/members` and `https://x.com/i/lists/[ID]/followers`.

**Followers and following.** `https://x.com/username/followers` and `https://x.com/username/following` display the full connection lists.

**Boolean construction.** The `OR` operator (uppercase required) allows optional terms alongside mandatory ones. Example: `"Michael Parker" kill OR stab OR fight OR beat OR punch OR death OR die`. Only one of the OR terms needs to match.

**URL content search.** `url:mega.nz breach` finds posts linking to a specific domain combined with a keyword. This is useful for locating breach data postings.

## Location-Based Search

The geocode operator finds posts made within a specified radius of GPS coordinates:

`geocode:43.430242,-89.736459,1km`

No spaces are used. The radius can be set to 1, 5, 10, or 25 reliably (other values produce inaccurate results). Units can be km or mi. Keywords can be appended: `geocode:43.430242,-89.736459,2mi "fight"`. The same query works as a URL parameter: `https://x.com/search?q=geocode:43.430242,-89.736459,1km&f=live`.

The author notes that geo-enabled tweets are now rare. Twitter's default has been "location disabled" for years, so most users never re-enabled sharing. This operator is most productive for historical investigations, where users were unknowingly sharing location data before the default changed.

## Date Range Search

The `since:` and `until:` operators (in YYYY-MM-DD format) restrict results to a specific window:

`since:2015-01-01 until:2015-01-05 "bomb threat"`

This is particularly valuable for targets with many thousands of posts. Scrolling through a live profile will eventually hit a limit imposed by Twitter or browser constraints. Splitting the search into yearly (or monthly or daily) batches using `from:username since:YYYY-01-01 until:YYYY-12-31` allows systematic archiving of a complete post history. The same approach applied with `to:username` captures incoming messages. A broader search placing the username in quotes (without from/to) captures both outgoing posts and mentions.

Date ranges can recover information no longer visible on the live profile. For example, `from:humanhacker email since:2006-01-01 until:2009-12-31` can surface an email address posted in an old tweet that the user has long since stopped including in their content.

## Searching by Email Address

Twitter does not expose an email-based account search through its website. A workaround requires a burner Twitter account, the Twitter mobile app running inside a virtual Android environment, and the target's email address entered into the Android Contacts app. Enabling Twitter's "Discoverability and contacts" sync then generates a "Find your friends on Twitter" notification with suggested followers. The investigator must not click to follow, as this sends a notification to the target. The author notes this technique was observed failing in newer accounts as of 2022 and expects it to continue degrading.

## Deleted, Suspended, and Missing Tweets

When a user deletes posts or an account, several recovery paths exist.

**Search engine caches.** Searching Google for the profile and using the cached view can surface deleted content. The URL `https://webcache.googleusercontent.com/search?q=cache:https://twitter.com/username` attempts to load a cached version directly. Google, Bing, and Yandex each maintain independent caches taken on different dates, so checking all three yields more content. The Bing cache may be older than Google's, and Yandex older still, each containing unique posts.

**Site-specific Google queries.** `site:twitter.com/username/status` forces Google to return direct links to individual posts. Even if Twitter now reports those posts as deleted, the Google cached view of each link may display the content.

**Wayback Machine.** `http://web.archive.org/web/*/twitter.com/username` retrieves archived snapshots of the profile. The same pattern with `/likes` appended can sometimes surface posts the user had liked before wiping their account.

## Twitter Post Image Details

For an individual tweet image, removing `&name=small` from the image URL (which ends in a format like `pbs.twimg.com/media/[ID]?format=jpg&name=small`) returns a larger version. Appending `&name=large` or opening the image in a new tab gives the highest available resolution.

## TweetDeck

TweetDeck (tweetdeck.x.com) provides access to Twitter's full public firehose of data, enabling real-time monitoring through persistent column views. As of 2023, TweetDeck requires an X Premium subscription, which involves associating payment information with an account (a risk to operational security). The open-source browser extension OldTweetDeck (available at github.com/dimdenGD/OldTweetDeck) restores most of TweetDeck's original functionality on Firefox or Chromium browsers without a premium subscription, though some features (such as filtering by likes) no longer work due to Twitter API changes.

TweetDeck columns can monitor search queries or individual user accounts. Multiple columns run simultaneously, so investigators can watch several subjects at once. Alerts (audible sounds on new matches) can be configured per column. The geocode operator works inside TweetDeck columns, providing a live stream of geo-tagged posts from a location. A practical example from the book: a real-time monitoring setup of `to:myclient kill OR die OR shoot OR death` identified a threatening sender, and a separate column of `from:suspect to:myclient kill OR die OR shoot OR death` with an alert tracked that suspect's further messages until an arrest occurred.

Firefox installation steps for OldTweetDeck: download OldTweetDeckFirefox.zip from the Releases page, go to about:config and set `xpinstall.signatures.required` to false, go to about:addons and press "Install Add-on From File...", select the zip, then navigate to `https://x.com/i/tweetdeck`.

## Third-Party Resources

Twitter closed its free API tier in 2023, which eliminated most free third-party tools. Any remaining free services return limited results. Services that survived are largely premium. A small set still provides value.

**Omnisci** (heavy.ai/demos/tweetmap): A database platform developed through MIT and Harvard collaboration. Searches historical geotagged tweets by topic, username, or location, displayed as dots on a map. Best for events from several years prior.

**One Million Tweet Map** (onemilliontweetmap.com): Displays the most recent one million geo-tagged tweets on an international map, updating continuously. Useful for live event monitoring, not for complete location coverage.

**Tweet Mapper** (keitharm.me/projects/tweet): An abandoned project that occasionally returns results for targets whose posts carry location data.

**Twitter Audit** (twitteraudit.com): Identifies the proportion of an account's followers that appear fake. Requires Twitter authentication. Limited to one free query per user account. Accessed via `https://www.twitteraudit.com/username`.

**TweetBinder** (dash.tweetbinder.com/report/free): Analytics-focused, useful for identifying patterns in recent account activity.

**Memory.lol** (github.com/travisbrown/memory.lol): A web service holding 542 million historical screen names for 443 million Twitter accounts, scraped from the Twitter Stream Grab and Wayback Machine. Querying `https://api.memory.lol/v1/tw/username` returns the account's numeric ID and the date range during which that screen name was observed. The reverse query `https://api.memory.lol/v1/tw/id/[numeric_ID]` resolves a numeric ID back to a username and history. This is useful for tracking name changes and linking numeric IDs to screen names.

**Threadreader** (threadreaderapp.com): Makes long Twitter threads readable on a standard web page. Also provides a secondary means of viewing Twitter content without directly visiting the platform. Queries can be run by keyword (`https://threadreaderapp.com/search?q=keyword`) or by username (`https://threadreaderapp.com/user/username`), though user-based queries only work for accounts that have used Threadreader themselves. No Twitter credentials are required.

## Pivoting to Alternative Platforms

Twitter's API restrictions and policy changes have pushed many users to competitor platforms. Taking a target's username, display name, or distinctive bio text and searching it across alternative microblogs often locates new accounts, sometimes with the same username. Platforms to check include Instagram (instagram.com), Threads (threads.net, linked to Instagram accounts), Bluesky (bsky.app, popular among technical users), and Mastodon (mastodon.social). A Google query combining multiple site operators works efficiently: `site:threads.net OR site:bsky.app OR site:mastodon.social OR site:instagram.com "username"`.
