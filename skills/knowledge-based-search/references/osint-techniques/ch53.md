This unit is the opening introduction to Section IV, which covers OSINT methodology: workflow, documentation, ethics, and reporting. It does not present techniques but frames why the section exists and who wrote it.

## The core argument

Documentation and methodology are no longer optional. The authors open with a case from the late 1990s in which the investigator interviewed a registered sex offender, observed illegal activity on his computer, and later testified about it with no digital evidence (only verbal description). That was acceptable then. It is not acceptable now. Current prosecution requires forensic acquisition, detailed logs, and pictorial proof at every step.

Three consequences follow from skipping documentation:

1. Without digital evidence, the crime or online incident being investigated effectively never happened.
2. Without proper training and policies, the evidence may be rejected.
3. Without confidence in the work, the investigator may not be taken seriously.

## Scope of Section IV

The section addresses workflow, documentation, formalities, and the ethics of online investigations. The authors acknowledge these are not easy conversations and that they do not claim to have all the answers. The content draws on their own experiences and lessons learned from mistakes. Document templates and workflow diagrams covered in the section are available for download at `https://inteltechniques.com/osintbook11` (username: `osint11`, password: `bk3t7`).

## Authorship and voice

The primary author is Michael Bazzell. Jason Edison, a 25-year veteran of a major U.S. police department and investigative lead for its Digital Crimes Unit, co-authored this section. Edison has trained thousands of students in open source intelligence and cyber-crime investigations in both public and private sectors. He is an official IntelTechniques OSINT instructor and maintains the IntelTechniques online video training courses at IntelTechniques.net. Most of the content in Section IV comes directly from Edison. The first-person "I" throughout the section is a collective voice from both authors.

## Deliberate disagreements and tool diversity

Bazzell and Edison do not agree on every tool or method, and the section intentionally presents alternatives that may contradict each other. The authors see this as a strength. Bazzell avoids Microsoft and Google products when possible, prefers Linux, and avoids closed-source tools that "call home." Edison prefers Microsoft OneNote for note-taking and uses Chrome. Neither position is presented as correct. The goal is to offer enough options that readers can choose what fits their own workload and investigative context.
