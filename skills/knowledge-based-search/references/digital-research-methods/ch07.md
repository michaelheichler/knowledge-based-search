## Overview

Chapter 7, written by Tanja Schneider, surveys the subfield of digital food studies, maps its methodological repertoire, and uses the FoodCoach research project as an extended illustrative case. The chapter addresses two guiding questions: what are the methodological implications of the digital turn in studies of food and eating, and how have digital and computational methods been taken up in social science and humanities (SSH) disciplines?

## The Emergence of Digital Food Studies

Food and eating practices are increasingly entangled with what researchers call "digital food environments" (Granheim et al., 2022) or "digital foodscapes" (Goodman and Jaworska, 2020). These environments include dietary tracking apps, social media influencers on YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok, online grocery shopping, meal kit services, and food delivery platforms. The boundary between offline and online engagement with food has become blurred.

Early scholarship, exemplified by de Solier (2013), showed how food blogs enabled active sharing of culinary knowledge rather than passive consumption of expert content. This gave rise to digital communities of "food amateurs" (Rousseau, 2015) on Twitter, Facebook, and YouTube. Researchers in media and cultural studies used these platforms to study identity formation and community development.

A parallel stream examined food activism. Mann's (2014) study of La Via Campesina showed how the peasant movement used alternative digital media to challenge corporate narratives around food security. Subsequent work explored digitally enabled food activism more broadly, including its tensions with individualising tendencies in ethical consumption politics (Eli et al., 2016; Humphery and Jordan, 2018).

Sociologist Deborah Lupton coined the term "digital food cultures" (2018a) to capture how digital technologies shape popular food trends and communities. She argued that algorithmic engagement and personalised advertising do not merely reflect expressed preferences but actively shape future food practices. She also noted that Big Data on digital food can influence industry, research, and health policy. Anders Munk's research applied digital methods, specifically visual network analysis, to map the emergence of the New Nordic Food movement across Scandinavia using Facebook data, tracing how specific ingredients spread among chefs.

The field now spans sociology, anthropology, human geography, science and technology studies (STS), and media studies. Tania Lewis identified four key topics: the rise of food photography on social media, the growing role of ordinary people in online food culture, the impact of digital media on food politics, and the expanding role of large food companies in social media. She called for critical media literacy.

## Human-Computer Interaction and Human-Food Interaction

Researchers in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) contributed a distinct strand. Human-Food Interaction (HFI) emerged as a research area focused on designing and testing novel technologies that mediate relations between humans and food. One example is the HAPIfork, a smart fork that vibrates and blinks when the user eats too quickly. Critics noted that such devices risk turning eating into a technology-mediated intervention that disturbs the enjoyment of shared meals.

A systematic review of 270 HFI publications (Bertran et al., 2019) found radical growth, with 6 percent of papers published before 2009, 51 percent between 2010 and 2015, and 43 percent between 2016 and mid-2018. The review identified three focal areas: (1) the functionality or experience of human-food interactions, (2) agency in the interplay between humans and technology when dealing with food, and (3) food from paddock to plate, covering sourcing, storing, producing, tracking, and eating. The review also identified two key challenges: a dominance of techno-solutionist approaches and a dominance of technology-centric framings that risk marginalising user agency. A further emerging area, Human Food Practices (HFP), developed by Khot and Mueller (2019), attends to how food practices are shaped by individual and societal expectations in wider context.

The principal divergence between SSH and HCI researchers lies in their orientation toward technology. STS researchers tend to study how the digital turn changes practices and social relations, using digital methods to map food controversies and platform affordances. HCI researchers generally conduct experiments to test digital devices and optimise food consumption against pre-defined nutritional norms.

## Research Methods in Digital Food Studies

Krogager and Leer (2020) identified a considerable variety of methods in the field, from classic qualitative approaches to computational methods. Some well-established methods, including interviews, observation, and textual analysis, remain relevant but require updating for digital contexts.

Asdal and Reinertsen (2022), drawing on Hagen and Skorpen (2016), describe four modes of fieldwork for studying digital practices:

1. Analogue fieldwork using digital tools: using email and online meetings as routine research infrastructure.
2. Fieldwork stretching into the digital: observing how actors engage in web-based activities, such as co-writing documents online.
3. Fieldwork exploring the digital itself: investigating how actors engage with digital spaces like Facebook and the blogosphere, relying on co-presence rather than co-location.
4. Fieldwork within the digital: full immersion in the digital environment through participant observation.

The edited volume by Leer and Krogager (2021) illustrates these approaches through textual analysis and ethnography but largely stops short of engaging with Big Data or computational methods. Richard Rogers (2015) observed a broader tendency in SSH to digitise existing methods rather than develop genuinely new digital methods that repurpose the logic of digital platforms themselves.

A team of HCI researchers (Dolejšová et al., 2020b) used experimental food design co-creation methods across workshops to anticipate food-tech issues. They tested six approaches: crafting, situated food-play design, speculating, boundary objects, foraging, and mapping. Their recommendations emphasised creativity, sustainability, and social and ecological fairness.

Another group studied how computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) tools could "open up" food infrastructures and foster food citizenship (Prost et al., 2021). They collaborated with a primary school and four food organisations in the United Kingdom, designing "contact zones" where pupils aged 7 to 8 encountered food practices at a mushroom grower, a vegetable farm, a bread-baking community centre, and a food bank. The study found that physical encounters enabled young people to develop caring relations with more-than-human food worlds.

Venturini and Munk (2022) called for social science researchers to experiment with new strategies that go beyond the quantitative-qualitative divide, noting that digital technologies produce inscriptions whose granularity, dispersion, and rhythm are unprecedented in the social sciences.

## The FoodCoach Case

FoodCoach is an interdisciplinary research project at the University of St. Gallen, Switzerland, funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation (SNSF, project number IZKSZ1_188402, 2020-2024). Its goal is to develop an automated approach for analysing individuals' digital food shopping data and to make this analysis accessible via a mobile app, specifically a progressive web app. The project aims to increase the nutritional balance of diets and prevent diet-related health issues. The project began in the HCI tradition but shifted to include SSH perspectives on digital foodscapes when sociologists, anthropologists, and STS researchers joined the team.

### Obtaining and Analysing Food Shopping Data

Direct access to anonymised loyalty card data from Swiss retailers was not available. The project used the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) instead: volunteers request their own shopping data and donate it to the research project. The intermediary facilitating this process is BitsAboutMe (BAM), a startup that requests data from organisations on consumers' behalf and lets consumers choose with whom to share it and for what purposes.

The two retailers involved are Migros and Coop, which jointly hold around 70 percent of the Swiss grocery market (GfK, 2021). Consumers request their digital receipts from these retailers and then link their email accounts to the BAM web interface. The process proved non-trivial for many participants, and a considerable number abandoned data donation partway through.

Once data is received, the team cleans it, matches food items to the Eatfit database (which holds nutrient information for products sold by both retailers), and calculates a Nutri-Score for each shopping basket. Nutri-Score is a front-of-pack label using a colour scale from dark green to red and letters from A to E. The app then presents this information visually and suggests tailored dietary interventions. Recommendations were developed in collaboration with the University Clinic for Diabetology, Endocrinology, Nutritional Medicine and Metabolism (UDEM) at the University Hospital of Bern. Dieticians at UDEM helped design the recommendation algorithm, specifying when to intervene, what to suggest, and why.

Ongoing challenges include keeping the Eatfit database current, choosing the right nutritional quality score, deciding on the best visual representation, and determining on what basis to suggest alternative products.

### Contextualising Shopping Data with Additional Data

To avoid reducing food behaviour to individual consumer choice, the team developed a separate work package studying the social context of eating. Methods included a survey of 441 people aged 16 and older living in Switzerland, 21 semi-structured interviews lasting between 26 and 63 minutes, 15 ethnographic go-alongs with early prototype users, and four focus groups with 26 participants to discuss app mock-ups. Survey, interview, and focus group samples all included both users and non-users of digital platforms.

Survey analysis identified six distinct clusters of how participants think about "eating well" (Stehrenberger et al., 2024). Only two of the six clusters appeared likely to be interested in an app like FoodCoach based on prior engagement with dieting and dietary tracking. This raised questions about how to reach the other clusters, whether the most health-aware cluster actually needed the app, and how to sustain long-term engagement, since research showed users of dietary tracking apps often stop after a limited period.

Focus group participants raised many questions about who is behind the app, how data is analysed, why Nutri-Score was chosen, and what its scientific basis is. Some expressed difficulty trusting the app despite its non-commercial, research-oriented background. Participants indicated they would want a seal of approval from a recognised, independent nutritional association or research group. This led the team to discuss whether they needed a "human in the loop" in the form of a collaborating nutrition body.

### App Design and Interdisciplinary Collaboration

The collaboration with UDEM deepened over time. A side project produced DietCoach, a related prototype app introduced to UDEM clinic clients. DietCoach will be reviewed during regular consultation visits, allowing dieticians to discuss its use, answer questions, and adjust settings. It will also generate training data for the recommendation algorithm.

The author notes that dietary tracking apps represent more than a simple digital replacement for analogue tools such as food diaries, food labels, or plate-proportion charts. New data and analysis methods change how both nutrition professionals and everyday eaters know and engage with food, shifting the dietician-client relationship and raising the question of what forms of knowing food, including tasting, preparing, and growing, may be marginalised by a turn to digital monitoring.

The team identified two main lessons from the project. First, a co-design approach involving citizens from the outset would have been ideal to continuously incorporate diverse user perspectives and build trust in a non-commercial app. Second, dependence on the BAM intermediary for data donation proved complicated. The team investigated building its own data infrastructure or finding alternative partners. Both lessons point to the need for research projects in this space to build in flexibility and to secure additional funding, since changing the research design requires renegotiation with funders.

## Conclusion

Digital food studies has developed a broad methodological repertoire encompassing qualitative fieldwork, textual analysis, digital ethnography, computational methods, and experimental design approaches from HCI. The dominant tendency in SSH remains adapting existing methods to digital field sites, with limited uptake of new computational datasets or genuinely novel methods. HCI researchers have been more open to combining computational and qualitative approaches, likely due to the field's applied and interdisciplinary orientation.

Interdisciplinary collaboration is both a characteristic and a requirement of the field. The FoodCoach case shows that combining technical-computational and socio-technological expertise produces novel questions but also requires flexibility that funding structures often do not accommodate.

Schneider speculates that digital food studies as a named subfield may eventually be absorbed into mainstream food studies. As digital media and platforms become seamless infrastructure for food and eating, the digital dimension will lose its novelty while retaining its analytical importance. Training the next generation of researchers in digital and computational methods, through PhD courses, summer schools, or online training, remains an open challenge.
