## Overview

Chapter 8, written by Jens Koed Madsen, argues that behavioural psychology must combine qualitative and quantitative methods, including agent-based modelling (ABM), to study and intervene in wicked, complex societal problems. The chapter makes the case that neither machine learning alone nor qualitative research alone is adequate for these problems, and that a cyclical, multi-method approach is necessary.

## Behavioural Psychology as a Discipline

Behavioural psychology studies the antecedent causes of how people make choices, respond to incentives, and behave in social contexts. It draws on behaviourism (inference from observed behaviour), economic theory (rational utility maximisation as a normative benchmark), and the study of heuristics and biases. Herbert Simon's concept of bounded rationality is cited: people generally have reasons for what they do, even when they deviate from economic norms. The discipline overlaps with sociology, anthropology, and behavioural economics. Its practical aim is to design and test interventions, with nudging (changing the decision environment to alter behaviour without changing economic incentives, Thaler and Sunstein) as a prominent application.

Methods in the field are diverse: experiments, surveys, interviews, Bayesian computational models, large-scale datasets such as the World Values Survey, and machine learning. Vaccine acceptance during COVID-19 is given as an example where social norms, Bayesian belief networks, and machine learning models have all been applied to the same problem.

## Wicked and Complex Problems

Most problems behavioural psychology addresses are described as wicked (a term from Rittel and Webber, 1973): no single or clear solution, interdependencies among system elements, no natural stopping point, and adaptive resistance to interventions. They are also complex: actors interact in ways that generate feedback loops, adaptive behaviour, and strong heterogeneity, and the system itself evolves over time.

The chapter identifies four structural features of these problems, illustrated throughout with fishing behaviour.

**Interaction and feedback loops.** Two-person problems have closed-form analytical solutions, but systems with three or more interacting people require iterative or numerical methods. Interactions generate feedback loops that change systems qualitatively and quantitatively. The fishing example illustrates tragedy-of-the-commons dynamics (Ostrom) when fishers observe and copy each other. Behaviour cannot be predicted by scaling up individual models.

**Adaptive behaviour.** People change behaviour as they learn. A fisher may copy a more successful neighbour, potentially causing the whole fleet to target the same area and deplete the stock. Adaptive change can be quantitative (how long to fish) or qualitative (leaving the profession).

**Heterogeneity.** People differ in goals, beliefs, knowledge, and methods. Risk-averse fishers behave differently from risk-seeking fishers. Understanding individual differences matters for anticipating how a system evolves and how quickly it adapts to changed conditions.

**Time.** Systems unfold over time. Feedback loops and adaptive changes do not happen instantaneously. The speed of connectivity matters (stock market crashes, Tulip Mania of 1637 is cited). Interventions may be reversible early and irreversible later, as with fisheries overexploitation.

Because complex problems have no closed-form solutions, numerical models are needed.

## Agent-Based Models

Agent-based models (ABMs) are computer-simulated multi-agent systems that describe individual agents, the environment they act within, and the interactions among them. Axtell and Epstein (1996) coined the term "artificial societies" and argue for "generative social science" as a contribution to understanding human behaviour at scale. ABMs simulate how local-level behavioural rules produce societal-level patterns.

**Agents** represent any acting entity (individuals, firms, teams). They are endowed with psychological and behavioural rules that must be expressible as computable functions. Agents in a fishing model may follow an explore-exploit-imitate strategy (explore a new area with some probability, imitate a successful peer with some probability, or exploit the historically best area) or may be modelled as perfectly informed profit-maximisers. Agents can have demographic attributes (boat size, starting capital) and psychometric attributes (risk aversion, drawn from a Gaussian distribution to introduce heterogeneity). Rules enable adaptive behaviour: a change in fuel prices causes profit-maximising agents to fish closer to shore, while explore-exploit-imitate agents adapt through their social networks.

**Environment** is the simulated world: economic features (monetary incentives), political features (Marine Protected Areas), and physical features (port locations, biomass distribution). Environmental elements must be algorithmically expressible. Biomass can include spawn rates, growth rates, and migratory patterns. The environment can change dynamically and can be used to test system fragilities by running simulations with different biological estimates.

**Interactions** can be agent-agent or agent-environment, and direct or indirect. A fisher telling another where fish are found is a direct agent-agent interaction. A fisher inferring that a peer with the same boat type is doing better and deciding to explore is an indirect agent-agent interaction. Extracting biomass is a direct agent-environment interaction. Avoiding coastal lines due to currents is an indirect one. Any interaction that can be expressed as an algorithm can be implemented.

ABMs support self-organisation and emergent behaviour. Arton et al. (2024) show that a communal fishing quota can trigger a tragedy of the commons if fishers are socially motivated to fish more when peers are outperforming them. Schelling's (2006) segregation model is cited to illustrate how simple rules (an agent moves if more than X percent of its neighbours differ from it) produce non-trivial emergent patterns (around 75% segregation even when agents are content to be a minority at 30%).

## Limitations of ABMs

Every component must be expressible as a computable function. Belief revision can be approximated with Bayesian models, but phenomena such as joy or aesthetic appreciation are harder to formalise. Even tractable functions are reductionist simplifications. Researchers can also reasonably disagree about which behavioural rules belong in a model, requiring explicit theoretical justification. Critics can ask whether behavioural rules are designed to produce the expected outputs (baking in the result). The chapter counters that baking in is most common early in development when the goal is to test feasibility, and that even baked-in models can yield non-trivial sensitivity insights. The broader point is that all theories limit the scope of investigation to what falls within their own framework.

## Why Machine Learning Falls Short

The chapter devotes attention to why back-propagating data approaches such as machine learning are insufficient on their own for complex human-environment problems.

Machine learning may overfit past observations and fail to predict how people will behave when conditions change. COVID-19 mobility patterns are cited as a case where historical training data would have misled transport models. Machine learning also provides a conceptual black box: the nodes and weights of the model do not translate into interpretable theories of behaviour. Stakeholders, politicians, and citizens need to understand the rationale for interventions. If the human component of the model cannot be explained, public adherence to policy recommendations is at risk. This point is tied explicitly to the COVID-19 pandemic, where perceived trust in scientific advice was key to compliance.

## Case Study: The POSEIDON Model

POSEIDON is a computational ABM of fisheries (Bailey et al. 2019, Carrella et al. 2019 and 2020a, Madsen et al. 2020) that integrates market conditions, ocean ecology, fishing fleet characteristics, and fisher behaviour. It serves as the chapter's running case study for how qualitative and quantitative methods can be combined in iterative ABM development.

Standard bio-economic models assume fishers are perfectly informed rational profit-maximisers. POSEIDON questions this. Carrella et al. (2020a) show that agents with perfect information behave in ways that diverge from observable data. Research cited in the chapter shows fishers forgo profit for in-group fairness (Polania-Reyes and Echeverry Perez, 2015), conservation concerns (Holland, 2008), and social norm compliance (Cardenas et al. 2000, Hatcher et al. 2000). Klein et al. (2017) find that consistency, sustainability, and neighbourliness matter as much as income. A survey of 93 Indonesian fishers across 13 harbours (Madsen et al., 2023) found that fishers weigh income, social norms, and personal reputation together, and that a fisher may accept economic sub-optimality to avoid social exclusion.

**Pattern-oriented testing** (Grimm et al. 2005, Jakoby et al. 2014) is used as a sanity check. Known real-world patterns are used to evaluate whether the model produces sensible outputs without hardwiring them. Fishers are known to cluster near the edges of Marine Protected Areas and to fish closer to port when fuel prices rise. An explore-exploit-imitate model in POSEIDON replicates both patterns without being explicitly programmed to do so.

**Calibration** means model fitting: adjusting parameters to minimise the distance between simulated outputs and observed data. The method used is indirect inference (Gourieroux et al., 1993), with a Bayesian optimiser (Shahriari et al. 2016, Snoek et al. 2012) minimising error across sequential runs. This resembles BACCO (Bayesian analysis of computer code output) techniques.

**Validation** means model prediction: parameters fixed after calibration are used to predict a held-out portion of data (typically 20 to 30 percent withheld from calibration). Carrella et al. (2020a) compare twelve behavioural models against data from 2011 to 2015 (US West Coast groundfish fishery), including four adaptive models (such as heatmap agents that build a statistical map of profitable areas), five statistical models (including one similar to machine learning trained on historical area distributions), and three standard assumptions (random agents and two variants of Homo economicus). Adaptive heuristic models match statistical models for calibration. For validation against 2015 data, adaptive models outperform statistical models, even though 2015 did not differ from the training years. The chapter argues the gap would be larger under conditions of significant change.

**Qualitative work** was used to enrich the behavioural component for Indonesian fisheries. Following a literature review, conversations with local partners, and collaboration with YKAN (Yayasan Konservasi Alam Nusantara, an affiliate of The Nature Conservancy), the team conducted 93 semi-structured interviews across 13 Indonesian harbours chosen to cover a range from commercial ports (Galesong) to village ports (Kasuari). The interviews revealed that fishers weight recent experiences more than past ones when choosing where to fish. They evaluate success through both absolute terms (is gross profit above a threshold?) and relative terms (am I doing better than peers?). Social port structures shape information sharing. Personal reputation constrains behaviour, for example refraining from targeting areas informally belonging to another fisher, and refraining from fishing during Ramadan.

However, qualitative data cannot stand alone. The chapter cites Stephens-Davidowitz (2017) on self-representation bias: most interviewed fishers claimed strong adherence to legal frameworks, yet observable behaviour showed some targeting East Timor waters (sparse enforcement) while avoiding Australian waters (active surveillance). The qualitative findings were translated into a new computational framework (Madsen et al., 2024) in which fishers update beliefs probabilistically about biomass location (with a memory function weighting recent observations more heavily than distant ones) and weigh competing considerations including economic gain, legal compliance, and reputation damage.

## Methodological Conclusion

The chapter argues for a cyclical process: model development involves continuous movement among the ABM, collected data, stakeholder and expert interviews, pattern-oriented sanity checks, and calibration and validation exercises. No single pass suffices. The work requires interdisciplinary teams that include people with cultural insight, people who can build and test psychological components, and data scientists who can test the models. The final recommendation is that researchers adopt pluralistic, collaborative, and iterative approaches, and engage directly with the people who will be the targets of proposed interventions.
