## Overview

Martin Grandjean argues that digital history is best understood as a craft practice rather than a revolution or a new disciplinary age. The chapter develops this argument by reading two classic texts against each other: Marc Bloch's "The Historian's Craft" (1953) and Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's 1968 column in "Le Nouvel Observateur," which ended with the phrase "the historian of tomorrow will be a programmer or he will cease to exist." Grandjean uses these texts to place current enthusiasm about digital methods in a longer historical frame, and he closes with a case study on network analysis of League of Nations archives.

## Le Roy Ladurie's 1968 Column and What It Reveals

Le Roy Ladurie published his column on May 8, 1968, a moment in France remembered for student revolts, not computing. He described computers as enabling historians to work on "vast corpora of documents, whose data were vital, but whose dimensions had hitherto defied the efforts of researchers." He predicted that archiving data on punched cards would produce a new kind of "historical engineer," and he observed that young American graduates were already comfortable with computing.

Grandjean reads this text as a cautionary tale. The prediction that historians would program turned out to be mostly wrong. The widespread availability of graphical user interfaces (GUIs) meant most historians only needed to push buttons. Specialized conferences and journals on computing and history appeared in the 1960s and 1970s but remained niche. Quantitative and computer-aided methods were later overshadowed by cultural and micro-historical approaches. The generation Le Roy Ladurie praised as digitally fluent has since retired without being recognized as especially digitally literate.

The recurring pattern, Grandjean argues, is that each generation believes it stands at a tipping point and can predict the digital future of the discipline. Adam Hodgkin's 1987 forecast that computing would be so integrated into historical scholarship by 1997 that specialized conferences would become unnecessary illustrates the same error. This pattern counsels modesty about current claims regarding artificial intelligence, augmented reality, or the metaverse in historical research.

Where Le Roy Ladurie and his contemporaries were right was on acceleration and massification. Computers do enable historians to process larger corpora faster. What began as the prerogative of historical demography and economic and social history has now spread across the discipline as "datafication" (Clavert, 2016). Grandjean also credits Le Roy Ladurie with anticipating what is now called open science: datasets created for one researcher could be archived and reused by future researchers.

The "Programming Historian" (programminghistorian.org) illustrates how complicated the relationship between historians and programming actually is. Originally created as a collection of Python lessons for historians by Turkel and MacEachern in 2008, the platform now contains many lessons that require no programming at all. What distinguishes digital historians is not programming per se but a practical orientation toward tools, as opposed to purely encyclopedic scholarly knowledge.

## The Historian's Toolbox: A Three-Layer Definition

Because digital history has no universal method and no global program, Grandjean proposes defining it through three layers: the toolbox, the practices, and the institutional settings.

### Tools

The toolbox metaphor captures how digital historians actually work. Researchers often do not know which tool they need, try ill-suited ones, adapt them for purposes they were not designed for, and arrive at results that are imperfect but analytically useful. Marc Bloch is quoted to the same effect: historians need at least a working knowledge of their principal techniques, "if only to learn the strength of his tools and the difficulties of handling them."

Grandjean deliberately excludes artificial intelligence from a specific toolbox slot. He draws a distinction between a tool (defined by its function) and the technology that implements it. A data visualization produced in the late eighteenth century and one produced by a modern web browser serve the same analytical function even though the technologies differ. What matters is the effect that a family of methods has on the production of historical knowledge, not the specific technology used to apply it.

### Practices

At the level of practices, Grandjean identifies several clusters.

Data analysis methods include named entity recognition and other text mining approaches, prosopography, network analysis, and spatial analysis. These methods have longer learning curves and are most consistently associated with "digital history."

Communication and public history practices use the same tools to present research rather than produce it. This includes data visualization, interfaces, augmented reality applications, online publications, and social media strategies. This overlaps with the growing field of digital public history.

Reflexive and meta-practices include the history of computing, digital historiography, and the study of how history is represented in digital media such as video games.

Archive processing is a large and foundational cluster. It covers digitization of paper sources, metadata enrichment, interoperability, linked data, and the handling of natively digital archives (administrative records, social media, archived web content). The distinction between digitized and natively digital sources is a challenge for source criticism, since the digital medium can give the false impression that all digital data is equivalent.

### Institutional Settings

How digital history gets implemented depends on local, budgetary, and symbolic contingencies as much as on any ideological vision. Grandjean maps four institutional postures.

As a discipline: some universities create a dedicated chair in digital history, which implicitly denies the "digital" label to other historical fields.

As interdisciplinary: a center or laboratory positioned at the overlap of history, art history, computer science, and information science, where computing and humanities professions collide.

As transdisciplinary: digital history as a platform common to all departments of an arts faculty, not rooted in any single discipline.

As a global evolution: digital history as the direction all historical disciplines are heading, making the label eventually unnecessary. This view sometimes carries ideological weight, treating digital history as a political movement. Its critics frame it as an emanation of digital industry lobbies or a deviation from humanistic ideals.

As communities of practice: networks of researchers who gather around methodological or technical questions rather than shared research subjects, typically through conferences and journals. Grandjean views this as the definition that follows most naturally from his argument, since it describes what actually happens when practitioners share craft knowledge.

## Case Study: From Archives to Networks and Back Again

To show what happens when historians use tools designed for other purposes, Grandjean presents a study of the correspondence archives of the International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation (ICIC), set up by the League of Nations in the interwar period to coordinate scientific and cultural affairs. The committee's members included Albert Einstein, Marie Sklodowska Curie, Henri Bergson, Kristine Bonnevie, and Hendrik Lorentz, among others.

The dataset covers 1919 to 1927 and includes more than 30,000 letters exchanged among roughly 3,000 people. The method is co-occurrence network modeling: two individuals are connected in the network if they both appear as sender or receiver in the same document. Three time slices of the network correspond to distinct institutional phases: the preparatory phase managed by the League Secretariat in Geneva, the arrival of the ICIC scientific experts and growth of external exchanges, and the rise of the International Institute of Intellectual Cooperation (IIIC) in Paris.

The network visualizations show the ICIC committee members becoming visually central, sitting between the Geneva and Paris institutions. But betweenness centrality tells a different story. This metric counts how often each node lies on the shortest path between all other node pairs. It is not a measure of how many letters someone sent or received. It identifies structural bridges, individuals whose removal would disconnect parts of the network. When this metric is calculated, League Secretariat staff and Paris Institute staff score far higher than the expert committee members, by an order of magnitude.

The historical point is that almost all prior scholarship on the ICIC focused on the scientific experts. The network analysis throws light on the bureaucratic apparatus that actually held the network together. But Grandjean stresses that reporting a betweenness score is not the end of the process. The historian must return to the archives to understand what the score means. When the actual files associated with the high-betweenness individuals are examined, they cover far more varied subject matter than the files linked to the expert members. This allows the ratio between betweenness and weighted degree to be reinterpreted as a specialization index: a high ratio indicates a generalist position (someone who bridges many diverse connections), a low ratio indicates monothematic expertise (someone who exchanges many documents but within a narrow domain). A neutral mathematical formula was translated, through two interpretive steps, into a custom analytical tool for this specific archive.

The word "translation" (developed in Grandjean and Jacomy, 2019) names this process of making computational outputs intelligible in the language of historical narration. Good translation requires understanding the nature of the historical source, the choices made in modeling it as data, and the assumptions built into the algorithm, before any historical claim can be made from the output.

## Conclusion

Grandjean closes by returning to Bloch's image of the historian as a wandering craftsman who must find a path without mastering every detail of the terrain underfoot. The "digital age" label is practically useful for communicating with a general audience but is misleading as a historical periodization. The actual writing of history has changed less than the tools surrounding it.

The positivist temptation to feed all available data into a machine and automatically reconstruct the past is real, and Grandjean warns against writing history without historians. What counts, as Le Roy Ladurie himself wrote in 1968, "is not the machine, but the problem." The historian of tomorrow, Grandjean concludes, will either be a historian, or will not be one at all.
