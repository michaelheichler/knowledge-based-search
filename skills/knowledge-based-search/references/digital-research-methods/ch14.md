## Overview

Chapter 14, written by David Moats, argues that the split between quantitative and qualitative methods in the Social Sciences and Humanities is not a permanent feature of intellectual life but a historically specific and recent formation. Moats traces how the divide emerged, why it hardened into a near-ideological conflict in the late 20th century, and how the rise of digital and computational data both reproduces old tensions under new names and opens possibilities for new method combinations. The chapter is itself a demonstration of the argument: Moats uses Google N-Grams, citation network analysis with VOSviewer, and qualitative documentary reading in combination, narrating the methodological choices as he goes.

## Two Initial Clarifications

Moats makes two foundational points before the historical account.

First, the quant/qual distinction is about how researchers treat data, not about what kind of data they have. Drawing on Harry Collins's argument about money, Moats notes that units become interchangeable when a community agrees to treat their qualities as irrelevant. Data becomes quantitative when researchers treat it as countable units rather than as traces of interaction. Facebook likes can be aggregated into an engagement metric or interrogated as acts of meaning between users. Images and text can be processed as numbers (pixels with hex codes, topic models from Blei, Ng, and Jordan's Latent Dirichlet Allocation) or read interpretively. Nothing in the nature of the data forces a particular treatment.

Second, the apparent philosophical gap between the paradigms is overstated. Stephen Jay Gould argued that the split between humanistic and scientific mindsets is exaggerated and that researchers in both camps move through similar stages. Martin Hammersley positions quant and qual as a spectrum on many dimensions, not a binary. Alain Desrosières notes that statisticians behave constructively (adjusting models, cleaning data) during research and write up as if they were discovering facts, while ethnographers make tacitly realist claims about what they observed. The philosophical performances of objectivity or subjectivity happen mainly at the write-up stage, not throughout the research process.

## Historical Origins of the Terms

To locate when "quantitative" and "qualitative" acquired their current methodological meaning, Moats uses Google N-Grams (Franz and Brants, 2006), which tracks relative word frequency across the Google Books corpus from 1800 to 2019. The graph shows both terms rising in the mid-19th century, a modest bump around 1870, a larger one around World War II, a steep rise for "quantitative" peaking around 1980, and a secondary spike in both terms around 2012 and 2014. Before the mid-19th century, both words referred mainly to chemistry: qualitative described properties such as color and texture, quantitative described amounts. Social-statistical usage of "quantitative" appears at the end of the 1800s.

To go beyond what N-Grams can show, Moats draws on Theodor Porter's Trust in Numbers (1995, 1996). Porter argues that numbers were not inherently authoritative in the Renaissance. They became central to science through the 17th-century practice of "virtual witnessing" (Shapin and Schaffer, 1985): precise measurements allowed scientists in different locations to reproduce experiments and check results without being present. Newtonian mechanics then made mathematics a model for other disciplines. Condillac saw arithmetic as the purest expression of rational thought. Karl Pearson saw quantification as a means of settling disagreements. Porter cautions that this drive to quantify did not necessarily follow from a positivist belief in uncovering natural laws. Pearson wanted useful models for social decisions, not discovery of underlying reality. Quantification arose as a practical tool for managing large populations (also Hacking, 1990) and monitoring workers, colonial subjects, and enslaved people (Whittaker, 2023). Porter traces how quantitative methods moved from science into industry and state bureaucracies as a trust strategy, then returned to the academy in the post-war years, driving the spike visible in the N-Grams graph. He associates the embrace of numbers especially with disciplines like psychology, where theoretical disagreements made external demonstrations of rigor important.

For the qualitative side, Lyman and Vidich (1994) trace qualitative methods, especially interviews, to colonial encounters. Missionaries recorded dialogues with other cultures that did not fit biblical knowledge, producing proto-interviews. These became part of formal colonial enterprises framed as "civilising." Nick Seaver (2015) adds that from the time of Malinowski, quantitative techniques were sidelined within Anthropology as "bastard algebra," contrasted with the flesh-and-blood reality that only ethnographic writing could capture. Munk and Jensen (2015) identify 19th- and 20th-century ethnological mapping as a moment before numbers and anthropology became opposed: maps displayed regularities that required explanation by other means rather than settling questions. W. E. B. Du Bois developed both qualitative chronicles of Black American communities and innovative quantitative visualizations. The early Chicago School of Sociology, as described by Andrew Abbott (1999), ran urban ethnographies alongside maps and surveys without conflict, until departmental splits divided the department along interpersonal, not philosophical, lines.

Lyman and Vidich's main caution is that qualitative methods still bear the imprint of the problems for which they were invented, including a hierarchy between researcher and subject rooted in a progress narrative about civilization.

Jovanović (2011) pushes the philosophical genealogy back to Pythagoras on one side and hermeneutic reading of sacred texts on the other. He asks when methods themselves became a necessary part of thinking and acknowledges that practical and material pressures (war, confrontation with other cultures) drive historical swings between numbers and interpretation as primary. Philosophical orientations are co-produced with cultural change, not prior to it.

## The Paradigm Wars

To investigate the 1970s-to-1990s intensification visible in the N-Grams graph, Moats uses VOSviewer (van Eck and Waltman, 2009) to perform a citation network analysis. He queried the CiteAlex database for articles containing both "quantitative" and "qualitative" in the title, 1900-2020, yielding 8,621 documents. He visualized the top 100 most connected documents in a citation network. The clusters include general mixed-methods guides (Teddlie and Tashakkori's 2009 manual, Bryman, Amaratunga, Smith), discipline-specific debates in medicine (Duffy, Mahoney and Goertz), and education (Creswell, Johnson).

The term "paradigm wars" was coined by Gage (1989), writing about education research. Gage described constructivist and critical researchers attacking "scientific" (quantitative) approaches to studying education for failing to produce useful generalizations and for ignoring teachers' perspectives. This was a backlash against the mid-century dominance of quantitative methods. Over time, the backlash effectively marginalized quantitative methods in education and other social science disciplines.

For Gage, the paradigm wars were about philosophical outlook, not methods as such. Somewhere in this period, method and philosophy became inextricably linked. The debates also became political: because statistics and positivism were associated with the state, leftist critiques of authority attached to constructivism and qualitative methods. Hammersley (1992b) noted that different accounts of the wars described either two paradigms (post-positivists and constructivists) or three (post-positivists, critical theorists, and relativists), and that the positions were identities associated with researchers and disciplines, not just methods. Academic identities are harder to shift than methods, which helps explain why the divisions became entrenched.

Denzin (2010) traces two waves of interest in mixed methods. The first, in the 1960s and 1970s (Campbell and Fiske, 1959), combined methods without treating this as philosophically problematic. Webb and co-authors (1966) coined "triangulation" for using multiple methods to gain a more complete picture of the same phenomenon. Denzin argues that mixed methods were sidelined during the paradigm wars, with triangulation repositioned as tacitly positivist (Silverman, 1985). A second wave revived mixed methods in the 1990s and 2000s after Howe (1988) proposed pragmatic solutions not requiring strong realist commitments. One effect of the paradigm wars was to solidify the expectation that researchers were either quantitative or qualitative, leaving little methodological "bilingualism" (Teddlie and Tashakkori, 2009). Evidence-based medicine's promotion of randomized control trials as the gold standard added a further pressure, attempting to subsume all other approaches under that banner.

Schwemmer and Wieczorek (2020) applied a political-orientation scaling technique to sociology journal abstracts from two decades. They show that even though explicit use of the words "quantitative" and "qualitative" has declined, a methodological divide in language persists. Terms cluster toward two poles in a unidimensional spectrum corresponding to quantitative and qualitative communities. The authors attribute persistence partly to how methods education is structured early in training and to journals that reinforce particular conventions. Moats notes the method presupposes a binary split rather than, say, multiple dimensions.

## New Divisions in the Big Data Era

The rise of big data around 2010-2011 did not dissolve the old divide but relabeled it. Moats identifies several new tensions.

Competition between social science and computer science for funding reproduces the old battle lines, as some computer scientists sideline SSH work as unscientific while SSH scholars point out limits of big-data approaches (boyd and Crawford, 2012). Savage and Burrows (2007, 2014) argued that methodological innovation moved to the private sector, which had easier access to large datasets, creating a crisis for empirical sociology. Within humanities departments, a softer divide exists between humanistic and computational researchers, which rarely reaches the intensity of paradigm wars because humanistic disciplines do not claim to produce definitive causal findings. Computational approaches such as distant reading (Moretti, 2013) and cultural analytics (Manovich, 2020) are generally seen as complementary to interpretive work rather than a threat.

boyd and Crawford (2012) warned that big data hype risked re-inscribing established quant/qual divisions and the associated philosophical identities.

## Proposed New Relationships

Moats surveys several proposals for productive method combinations, drawing mainly on Munk (2019) and Jensen (2022).

Munk (2019) distinguishes four styles of quali-quantitative analysis. "Complementarity" (Smith, 2014; Blok and Pedersen, 2014) uses ethnographic fieldwork to contextualize decontextualized data and data science to find larger patterns in which to situate ethnographic observations. "Single-level" zooming, from Venturini and Latour (2010), allows fluid movement between aggregate views and individual cases in a single dataset without constructing an explicit bridge between levels. "Curation," drawing on Rogers (2013), involves using qualitative insight to create alternative rankings or partitions of digital data rather than accepting the categorizations given by platforms. "Algorithmic sensemaking" is Munk's more speculative category, proposing full alignment of computational and ethnographic methods in exploratory pattern detection.

Jensen (2022) describes a "slalom" approach in which a researcher moves back and forth between digital and non-digital methods as doubts raised by one method suggest questions for another. In his project following psychologists, observations about their knowledge practices suggested a scientometric verification step, which in turn raised new questions for interview material. Jensen developed a counting technique that tracks new terms in scientific papers across several dimensions (concepts, theories, problem settings) and displays results as bar charts showing new and old growth, giving a different role to both interpretation and counting.

Venturini and Latour (2010) and Latour et al. (2012) argued that the only historical reason for keeping quantitative and qualitative methods separate was a lack of data. Population-level samples yielded thin numerical data, and it was hard to bridge aggregate trends with small-scale case studies. Fine-grained transactional internet data makes it possible to have full-population datasets made of text, images, and numbers, which can be analyzed by zooming in and out of large visualizations such as network graphs. They call this approach "quali-quantitative."

Moats identifies limits in all these proposals. Complementarity tends to assume that what each method does separately is already fixed, leaving little room for methods to become something new. Suturing quant and qual together at different stages of a project (as in most mixed methods designs) can suppress tensions rather than use them productively.

## Toward Problem-Driven Methods

Moats's concluding argument is that methods should be oriented to problems, not to disciplinary identities. He draws on C. Wright Mills (1959), who warned that sociology was becoming dominated by "abstracted empiricism" and "grand theory": two camps that endlessly refined their procedures and concepts while divorcing method from the private troubles and public issues that should animate social research. When methods become attached to academic identities with elaborate epistemological justifications, it becomes harder for empirical reality to adjudicate between them.

Moats argues that real-world problems should determine the balance of quantitative, computational, or qualitative approaches used, and that the computational turn makes this easier because more varied approaches are available at lower switching cost. He also argues that productive tensions between methods, rather than managed separation, may be what generates new research problems and new methods. He cites Maniglier (2021) on how confrontations between ways of knowing are essential to thinking. In his own chapter, the disjunctures between N-Grams and citation network analysis and qualitative reading raised new questions (what is the divide composed of? where and when should it be sought?) rather than confirming a settled answer.

He cautions against subsuming one set of methods entirely into another, which produces parasitic or tokenistic relationships (Barry et al., 2008). Jensen's injunction that "no method should adjudicate for another" captures the needed stance. Flexibility and methodological humility, along with more "bilingual" researchers and teams, are preconditions for new combinations to emerge. The question of which method is appropriate should remain open throughout the research process.

## Methods Used in the Chapter

Moats demonstrates his argument by combining three approaches in a self-reflective way.

Google N-Grams tracks frequency shifts in "quantitative" and "qualitative" across Google Books, 1800-2019, with zooming into individual documents in Google Books search to interpret peaks. Citation network analysis with VOSviewer queries CiteAlex for articles with both terms in the title (8,621 documents), visualizing the top 100 most connected in a citation network (resolution set to 0.5 for fewer clusters), then reading individual papers to interpret clusters. Qualitative historical and documentary analysis reads Porter (1995), Lyman and Vidich (1994), Jovanović (2011), Abbott (1999), Gage (1989), Hammersley (1992a, 1992b), Denzin (2010), and Schwemmer and Wieczorek (2020), among others.

He is explicit that each method raised problems that prompted recourse to another, that arbitrary cuts in network analysis are comparable to a qualitative researcher's corpus delimitation (Strathern, 1996; Amit, 2000), and that the interpretive work required by graphs is usually done but not reported in standard write-ups.

## Key Terms and Names

- Quali-quantitative: Venturini and Latour's term for methods that move fluidly between aggregate and individual cases in a single dataset.
- Paradigm wars: term coined by Gage (1989) for the 1980s conflict in education research, subsequently used broadly for the quant/qual divide.
- Triangulation: Webb et al. (1966), using multiple methods to converge on a phenomenon (later criticized as tacitly positivist by Silverman, 1985).
- Mixed methods: research designs combining quantitative and qualitative elements, reviewed by Teddlie and Tashakkori (2009) and Denzin (2010).
- Methodological bilingualism: Teddlie and Tashakkori's term for fluency in both quantitative and qualitative methods, which the paradigm wars suppressed.
- Virtual witnessing: Shapin and Schaffer (1985), the 17th-century practice of enabling remote replication of experiments through precise measurement, which made quantification central to science.
- VOSviewer: van Eck and Waltman (2009), software for bibliometric mapping used here for citation analysis via CiteAlex.
- Google N-Grams: Franz and Brants (2006), tool for tracking word frequency in the Google Books corpus over time.
- Slalom method: Jensen (2022), alternating between digital and non-digital methods as each raises doubts that the other can address.
- Abstracted empiricism and grand theory: Mills (1959), two pathologies in sociology that sever method and theory from real-world problems.
- Bastard algebra: Seaver (2015), term used within Anthropology to dismiss quantitative techniques from the time of Malinowski.
