## Overview

Chapter 18, by Sabine Niederer and Gabriele Colombo, argues that computational research methods carry inherent risks of bias, exclusion, and power imbalance, and that feminist research traditions offer principled ways to counter those risks. The chapter traces a line from technofeminism and cyberfeminism through the concept of matters of care, into the applied framework of data feminism, and then grounds all of it in three participatory visual research projects. A recurring structural finding is that even projects relying on digital data collection and computational visualization tend to revert to analog, in-person formats at the participatory stage, raising questions about where feminist methods have most influenced digital research.

## Feminism and Technology

Technofeminism (associated especially with Judy Wajcman, 1991, 2004, 2010) investigates how technological objects and systems embody gender biases. Technologies are understood as sociotechnical artifacts rather than neutral tools: when men dominate design, technology becomes "a key source of men's power and a defining feature of masculinity." A related concept is the genderscript, which refers to how a technology is designed, intentionally or not, for particular users while excluding others. Home appliances are a common example: cooking appliances are scripted for women, lawnmowers for men. The repair movement is offered as a technofeminist counter-practice: it encourages users to repair and reuse objects rather than accept their scripted lifespan, thereby subverting built-in assumptions and returning agency to marginalized users.

Cyberfeminism is an umbrella term for critical engagement with digital spaces and internet technologies, examining how online cultures reproduce or resist gender and identity norms through hacking, new media art, and net activism. Mindy Seu's Cyberfeminism Index (2022) is cited as a contemporary resource in this tradition. Scholars in adjacent fields have probed and audited algorithms, machine learning datasets, and AI systems for embedded bias, with the goal of making technology more equitable. These efforts are described as rooted in an ethics of care.

## Matters of Care

The chapter draws on Bruno Latour's distinction between matters of fact and matters of concern (Latour, 2004, 2005). Latour argues that shifting attention to matters of concern reveals that constructed things are fragile and require care and caution. Maria Puig de la Bellacasa extends this into a concept of matters of care (2011), insisting that research must go beyond the individual researcher's reflexivity to actively protect and attend to neglected things and oppressed subjects. Care is framed as something researchers and knowledge creators can practice.

Calvillo Gonzalez and Mesa del Castillo (2018) operationalize matters of care into design and architecture education through five pointers for "designing with care." They draw on Noortje Marres's work on material participation to argue that the policy of technologies resides not in the objects themselves but in how they are used across situations and contexts, and in their capacity to redistribute agencies. Their educational approach asks students to identify who is at the center of a design program, to work with neglected materials, and to place underrepresented agents (environment, climate, plants, animals) at the core of architectural design. Locality and situatedness are emphasized: a design approach must take seriously the local stakeholders affected by the issue under study.

## Data Feminism

Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren Klein's book Data Feminism (2020) is the central reference for the chapter's applied framework. Data feminism is defined as a critical and intersectional approach to understanding and working with data. It challenges biased data collection, analysis, and interpretation while advocating for equity and inclusion in data science. Its key moves include challenging binary classification systems, elevating lived experience and emotion, and promoting pluralism.

The framework builds on three foundational concepts from feminist theory. First, Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination (1990) captures interlocking systems of oppression based on race, class, and gender. Second, Donna Haraway's situated knowledges (1988) counters the scientific quest for universal objectivity with a situated, localized, and community-connected objectivity. Third, Arlie Hochschild's concept of emotional labor (1983) acknowledges that research work, for both researchers and participants, can involve emotional effort, particularly on trauma-informed topics.

The data feminism framework translates these into research principles: examine and challenge power (drawing on Collins), elevate emotion and embodiment (drawing on Haraway), and make labor visible (drawing on Hochschild). D'Ignazio and Klein also propose feminist data visualization as a practice that rethinks binaries, considers context, and makes visible the labor embedded in data work (2016). The chapter notes that data feminist concerns apply to both analog and digital data practices, and that new computational methods risk exacerbating existing power imbalances if not coupled with sensitivity to marginalized voices.

## Participatory Visual Methods

The chapter argues that participatory visual methods, which involve the subjects of study in the analytical process, offer a practical way to operationalize data feminist principles in digital research. Three projects are presented on a gradient of participation, moving from responsive interpretation, through collective enrichment, to fully inclusive co-ownership.

All three projects share a structural pattern: digital methods are used for data collection and visualization, but the participatory moments are conducted in analog, in-person settings using printed materials. This pattern is treated as a finding in itself: it shows that feminist-informed practice in digital research tends to affect the computational phases less than the collaborative analysis and annotation phases.

**Responsive Participation: NATURPRADI (Paris).** This project studied the issue of urban nature in Paris using images collected from Twitter around keywords such as "nature," "agriculture," and "biodiversity." Computer vision techniques were used to cluster and display similar images as composite images, large synthetic images made by combining social media visuals. In participatory workshops, local stakeholders, experts, and decision-makers were presented with printed composite images and asked to place a transparent sheet over the printout and draw their interpretation: labeling clusters, highlighting individual images, or adding elements they saw as absent. This process is called graphical interpretation (drawing on Johanna Drucker, 2020). The gap between what actors read into a map and what they think is missing generates collaborative speculation and, in some cases, agenda-setting. Multiple interpretive diagrams from different participants are compared to spark discussion. The method is described as a form of knowledge co-production and as a means of constructing publics around the issue under study (drawing on Carl DiSalvo, 2009, and Ezio Manzini's notion of conversation prompts, 2015).

**Collective Participation: DEPT. (Rijeka, Porto, Paris).** This project studied urban politics and aesthetics using images collected from platforms including Google Street View, Airbnb listings, and Twitter. The method is named "developing," referring to a series of visual transformations that move online images toward artifacts for public participation (Ricci, Calibro, Evennou, and Verjat, 2021). The process has three steps. First, researchers produce catalogs of images scraped from the platforms. Second, these catalogs are used in one-on-one dialogues with urban actors, who select individual images and add observations. Third, the selected images and annotations are rearranged into tableaux, defined as structured compilations of scraped and cropped images indexed by algorithms, annotated by hand, and layered with each participant's personal narrative. In a performative final step, the tableaux are hung on a wall and read aloud, producing collective descriptions that foreground tensions and partial connections among participants. The project is described as a response to the critique that digital research often ends with an epiphanic media reveal (Allen, 2020) rather than enabling action. Participants' situated knowledges become a central element in designing new collective representations.

**Inclusive Participation: Urban Belonging (Copenhagen).** This project, conducted by researchers from the Techno Anthropology Lab at Aalborg University Copenhagen and the Visual Methodologies Collective of the Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences, developed a photovoice application in collaboration with urban planning professionals and marginalized communities. Participants included people who self-identified as deaf, LGBTQ+, homeless, ethnic minorities, people with physical disabilities, and people with mental vulnerabilities. Each participant used a custom app to photograph places in Copenhagen and rate each photo and place by their feeling of belonging, then respond to other participants' photos within the app.

Workshops were organized around analog representations of each participant's digital data. Each participant received a deck of cards containing their own photos with annotations, selected two images, placed them on a printed map of Copenhagen, and added personal stories. Other workshop activities used printed maps of individual routes through the city as conversation prompts for discussing how belonging is experienced in movement. A third map visualized participants' reactions to each other's photos alongside a card deck of the most contested photos (those that generated the most varied reactions), to open discussion about aligned or conflicting feelings about the city. The output is a collective catalog of photos, data visualizations, and maps linking personal experience to geospatial data.

The project draws explicitly on Sasha Costanza-Chock's Design Justice principles of community-led design (2020) and on data feminist principles of embracing pluralism. An additional concern, drawn from Christopher Kelty's The Participant (2019), is making participation visible: Kelty argues that participation requires that participants can see themselves participating. Giving participants back their own data in visual form, and allowing them to choose what to filter out and what to add, is presented as a form of recognition that makes visible the labor of those who contributed to the research.

## Conclusions

The chapter concludes that computational methods have greatly expanded the possibilities for data collection, analysis, and visualization, but these advances have not automatically produced more inclusive participatory practices. The participatory step, in all three projects examined, depends on analog materials: printed maps, card decks, tableaux, and printed composites. Feminist principles are thus more fully operationalized at the point of in-person collaborative annotation and interpretation than at the computational stage.

The three projects are arranged on an explicit gradient: responsive participation (reacting to existing visualizations), collective participation (enriching outputs through multiple ways of knowing), and inclusive participation (co-owning every step from data collection onward). Each addresses power imbalances in a different register, consistent with data feminist principles of challenging power, elevating situated knowledge, and making labor visible. The chapter's core claim is that data feminist principles can guide the design of more thoughtful and equitable data-driven research, but achieving that requires deliberate integration of participatory, often analog, practices at the moments where diverse communities engage with the data.
