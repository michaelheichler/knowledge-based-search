## Overview

This chapter, authored by Iza Romanowska, surveys how digital and computational techniques have transformed archaeology, from field data collection through analysis and public engagement to the discipline's broader theoretical identity.

## What Archaeology Studies and Why It Differs

Archaeology studies past peoples through their material remains and the contexts in which those remains are found. Unlike historians, archaeologists do not rely on written sources as their primary evidence. Unlike social scientists, they cannot observe or interact with their subjects. Excavation is the main mode of data collection, and excavation is destructive: once a site is dug, it cannot be dug again. This irreversibility, combined with the high cost of fieldwork and the legal obligation to preserve records for others, creates strong pressure to document completely and to share data widely.

The discipline's scope is broad, touching on economy, urban studies, gender studies, network science, physical anthropology, and evolutionary biology, among others. This inherent interdisciplinarity has made archaeology receptive to importing methods and frameworks from many other fields. The chapter argues that all contemporary archaeological projects are effectively digital projects, because virtually all archaeological data passes through a digital filter at some point, creating what Roosevelt et al. (2015) call a "digital heritage ecosystem."

## Terminology

Several overlapping terms describe computational work in the field. Digital Archaeology covers all uses of digital technology in archaeology, including storage and virtual museum experiences. Computational Archaeology is narrower and denotes analytical or generative computing aimed at learning about the past. Related terms include Archäoinformatik (German), Archaeological Computer Sciences, Analytical Archaeology, Virtual Archaeology, and Cyber Archaeology. Computational Archaeology overlaps with Archaeological Science (formerly Archaeometry), the branch concerned with applying scientific techniques to archaeological questions.

## Historical Development

Computational methods entered archaeology in the 1950s, initially applied to seriation, statistics, and data cataloguing using tapes and punch cards. Wider adoption accelerated in the 1970s. The typical pattern has been: a method originates in another discipline (engineering, geography, life sciences), a small group of specialists with non-archaeological backgrounds brings it in, and the community then adapts and integrates it.

The chapter traces four waves of technological advance:
1. Data analysis and management (statistics, scientific data, databases), dominant in the 1970s and 1980s.
2. Remote sensing and Geographic Information Science, dominant in the 1990s.
3. Image processing and 3D modelling, taking off in the mid-2000s, alongside renewed interest in simulation and network science.
4. The current decade, marked by growing interest in digital tools for heritage and public engagement (including video games), and the rapid rise of AI methods, widely predicted to define the next decade.

With new tools came not only technical capabilities but also theories and debates from the source disciplines. GIS, for instance, brought with it geographical discussions of landscape perception and phenomenology that enriched archaeological theory.

## Data Acquisition

Because excavation is destructive and expensive, accurate and complete documentation is critical. Over recent decades, field teams have adopted Total Stations, iPads, drones, photogrammetry, and 3D scanning. Some projects aim to go entirely "paperless." These tools speed recording and improve precision. However, the chapter notes a tension: hand-drawing an artefact forces close inspection that aids understanding, while 3D scanning enables access for other researchers and the public. The emerging consensus is that digital recording is the future, but it is unlikely to replace methods that require direct engagement with the record and professional judgement.

Remote sensing, particularly LiDAR and satellite imagery, has enabled discovery and mapping of previously unknown sites in dense vegetation and remote areas, including urban outlines invisible from ground level. Coupled with AI algorithms, these techniques have detected thousands of new sites even in regions already well documented archaeologically.

## Data Management

A multi-year excavation can produce millions of artefacts and environmental samples, all of which must be recorded, processed, and archived. The legal obligation to enable access by other archaeologists, heritage bodies, and the public means that standards, common ontologies, and interoperability tools are essential.

Key infrastructure repositories include the Archaeological Data Service (ADS), Open Context, and the Digital Archaeological Record (tDAR). Coverage of the Global South remains limited.

For data integration, the field is moving toward FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Tools under adoption include Linked Open Data with ontologies such as CIDOC-CRM, ISO-standard-based ontologies, and OWL (Web Ontology Language). The most advanced synthesis is the Ariadne Project (currently Ariadne+), which provides both a research data infrastructure and a CIDOC-CRM-based integration framework called the Ariadne Reference Model.

Standardisation debates are active. The chapter identifies real trade-offs: standardisation reduces flexibility and expressiveness (structured vocabularies constrain natural language), and governance of data resources tends to be dominated by specific groups, which can marginalise alternative epistemologies.

## Data Analysis and Modelling

The open science movement in archaeology (Marwick et al., 2017) has driven a shift from point-and-click statistical software to scripting languages, especially R. Scripting brings three benefits: it documents analytical decisions for transparency and replication, it handles large datasets without breaking them into smaller chunks, and it frees time previously spent on repetitive operations.

Four primary computational modelling tools are described.

**Statistics.** Used across all branches, with a discipline-specific application in chronological modelling via radiocarbon (14C) dating. Radiocarbon dates require calibration because atmospheric ratios of 14C to 12C vary over time. Calibration curves were first constructed from tree rings, then supplemented by multi-proxy curves (IntCal). Bayesian methods now allow contextual information to constrain date estimates. Aoristic modelling (Cumulative Probability Distributions) treats 14C dates as data points, combining hundreds or thousands into density kernels to detect population pulses, assess group resilience, and correlate population dynamics with climate change at continental scales.

**GIS.** A long-standing strength of the discipline. A distinctively archaeological application is archaeological predictive modelling, which estimates the probability of finding remains by correlating known site locations with spatial and environmental variables such as slope, soil type, and distance to ancient roads. These models inform present-day planning processes, helping assess development impacts before excavation. GIS has been applied from site-level artefact mapping to landscape-wide settlement modelling, fuelling the rise of landscape archaeology.

**Agent-Based Modelling (ABM) and Network Science.** Methods from Complexity Science that have grown rapidly in use over the last decade. ABM connects individual-scale decision-making to population-level patterns that match the scale of the archaeological record. For example, a model of urban evolution may be built from algorithms governing household demographics and economic interactions. Its output (an urban plan) can then be compared directly with LiDAR mapping of an ancient city. Network science enables analysis of settlement systems, revealing the structural importance of individual nodes within larger transport or exchange networks. A particular strength of both methods is that they generate formally stated, replicable, and transferable theory.

**AI methods.** Still in early stages but gaining pace, particularly for finds categorisation and for processing remote sensing data to identify new sites.

The concept of "Archaeological Synthesis" refers to using the long-term archaeological record as comparative material to address questions with present-day relevance, including sustainability, urban resilience, and societal dynamics.

## Dissemination, Outreach, and Public Engagement

The chapter describes a reevaluation of archaeology's relationship with the public. Outreach efforts range from site information boards to dedicated online resources, and have extended into social media platforms such as Twitter, TikTok, and Facebook. Three modes are identified: broadcasting (sharing knowledge), participatory (collaborative projects with the public), and media-as-data (studying what the public knows or believes, drawing on Bonacchi, 2017).

Digital tools allow narrative-driven, immersive heritage experiences that can inspire "enchantment" (Perry, 2019) and civic activism, going beyond simple information boards. Interactive 3D models hosted on web platforms combine GIS and 3D renderings, reconstruction of structures, and textual information in integrated, story-driven formats.

The chapter also addresses the use and misuse of the deep past in political and social discourse. The field of archaeogaming studies the depiction of the past in video games and the archaeology of games themselves. Research in public archaeology and digital methods has examined how archaeological narratives are enlisted in political discourse around nationhood and shared ancestry, often through artificially constructed versions of the past.

## Case Studies

**Catalhoyuk.** The Neolithic site of Catalhoyuk, considered one of the earliest urban-like settlements in the world, is described as an exemplar of digital approaches. The excavation team pioneered terrestrial laser scanning and digital photogrammetry, producing detailed 3D models curated in a GIS database. These formed the basis for a fully immersive simulation of the site accessible after excavation was complete, allowing specialists to browse archaeological contexts, cross-reference documentation types, and access database files. The same digital record fed an outreach and education programme including a "Virtual Museum" experience using fictional characters to engage visitors with the site's hypothesised egalitarianism, designed to prompt reflection on modern social structures (Perry et al., 2019).

**Jerash ceramics.** In the Roman city of Jerash, Jordan, an assemblage of close to 800,000 ceramic pieces required data handling tools beyond spreadsheet software. Python-based pipelines (Romanowska et al., 2021) analysed counts of pottery types and attributes across the full dataset, revealing an unusually high dominance of local pottery over 800 years of the city's history. Possible explanations, including utility, price, availability, customer preferences, and social influence on buying behaviour, were formalised as hypotheses and tested using an agent-based simulation model. This is presented as an example of archaeological synthesis: computational methods opened the analysis to the full ceramic assemblage rather than the small subset of distinctive fine wares, producing a more representative view of ancient society, and the formal ABM made the theory-building replicable and transferable.

## Challenges and Future Directions

Achieving the discipline's synthetic ambitions requires two advances: increased interoperability of archaeological data, and continuing development of computational analysis and modelling.

On interoperability, the legal framework matters. The Valletta Convention provides the legal basis for archaeological data collection and management in the European Union and requires signatories to maintain inventories of their archaeological heritage. But meeting this requirement demands greater technical and archival capacity in national and local heritage institutions, integration of datasets from academic research and legacy museum collections, and uniform application of standards across heritage management bodies.

The chapter identifies data ownership, long-term management, and open science publication norms as actively debated questions. The rapid expansion of open-access archaeological datasets has been called a "data deluge." The process is uneven across regions, data types, and scholarly communities. A poorly managed transition risks a "digital Dark Age" in which data becomes inaccessible or unusable. Active efforts are underway to develop standards for long-term data handling, sustainability of 3D and spatial data, data analysis script reviews, data citation, and teaching.

## Conclusions

The chapter argues that digital and computational methods did not simply make standard archaeological practice faster or larger in scale. They enabled a fundamental epistemological and theoretical shift: a slow but steady move away from humanities toward social sciences, from studying individual artefacts and sites toward society-wide phenomena, cross-cultural comparisons, and large-scale spatio-temporal patterns. Modern archaeological research proposals routinely reference UN sustainable development goals and contemporary societal challenges such as climate change, food security, and migration. This represents a departure from an earlier view of archaeology as the study of the distant past for its own sake.
