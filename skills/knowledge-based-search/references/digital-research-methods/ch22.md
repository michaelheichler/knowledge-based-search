---
title: Meet the DCSSH Toolmakers: Institutional Contexts and Challenges of Digital Research Tool Development
source: Handbook of Digital and Computational Research Methods (Madsen, Munk)
---

This chapter examines who makes tools for Digital and Computational Social Sciences and Humanities (DCSSH), the institutional tensions they face, and what scholars need to understand about this community to work with it effectively.

## Who Makes DCSSH Tools

Free research tools in DCSSH do not come from professional software companies. The people who build them occupy three broad positions: research engineers who maintain technical infrastructure but whose job does not formally include tool design, academics who create tools as a byproduct of their research even though toolmaking falls outside their official mission, and many intermediate figures with unusual job titles or informal arrangements with management. The term "scientist-coder" (Perkel, 2021) describes this last group.

Universities enforce a strict division between support staff (IT, research engineers, administrators) and academic staff (researchers, professors). This division has genuine justifications: it protects researchers' ability to set their own scientific agenda while keeping technical skills in-house. But toolmaking does not fit cleanly on either side. Scientific instrument design holds that technology and methodology are inseparable, which conflicts directly with the university's division of labor.

Toolmaking profiles exist on a spectrum. At the infrastructure end is the Research Software Engineer (RSE), a term coined by Baxter et al. (2012) to name someone who relishes writing code well and wants recognition for producing tools others rely on for research. At the research end is the Researcher-Developer, who "wants to be judged on their scientific output" and "develops a lot of code due to the nature of their research" (Baxter et al., 2012). Researcher-Developers valorize their tools academically through proxy papers (papers meant to be cited in place of the tool itself), books, praxes (reflective activities integrating theory and practice), or by making the tool citable with a DOI. Some DCSSH tools also originate in the private sector or civil society and are repurposed by scholars.

## The Absence of a Career Path

Academic toolmaking lacks a recognized career path because it conflicts with the support-versus-science divide. The Science Code Manifesto (Barnes et al., 2013), whose signatories included Peter Norvig (then Director of Research at Google), Joseph Jackson (Open Science Alliance), and Victoria Stodden (statistician known for work on reproducibility), stated: "Software is an essential research product, and the effort to produce, maintain, adapt, and curate code must be recognized." Baxter et al. raised the same concern in 2012. The problem persists.

The threat to toolmakers differs by position but follows the same pattern. An RSE is typically pressured to stop maintaining their own tool and to support accredited researchers instead. A researcher-developer is pressured to stop coding and documenting and to teach and publish peer-reviewed articles instead, with their technological output treated as worthless. Either way, the toolmaker faces institutional pressure to abandon the activity that makes them a toolmaker.

RSE positions have become more common since 2012, but normalization has shifted rather than solved the problem. In practice, RSEs are often paid through structural funding, which introduces bureaucracy around who may use their help, for what purpose, and for how long. This can decouple software development from actual research. The chapter identifies three underlying causes: the rigid silo between support and academic positions, the invisibility of code maintenance, and the failure of SSH institutions to recognize technology as academic production.

## Tensions Inherent to Toolmaking

Toolmakers face two forms of academic gatekeeping. The first challenges the scholarly legitimacy of software production itself. The second criticizes the methodological assumptions embedded in a tool. Geoffrey Rockwell recounts that when he presented TACTWeb at ALLC/ACH Paris in 1994, a prominent community member dismissed it as "just a pretty visualization" with no argument behind it.

The methodological critique takes two forms. One directly opposes the algorithm or method (for example, framing an algorithm implemented in Gephi as "harmful," as in Peixoto, 2023). The other blames ease of use for undermining good practice. Tenen (2016) argues that simplifying a tool adds a layer of obfuscation and that "out-of-the-box tool use is best treated with a measure of suspicion." This framing casts the toolmaker as a "foolmaker" who enables unqualified users to do things they should not.

The chapter treats these criticisms as legitimate and healthy while pointing to a separate tension: a tool inevitably escapes its maker. It allows unintended uses, gets repurposed in unexpected ways, and has a life of its own. Because the digital literacy of users varies widely, a tool is always too easy for some and too hard for others. The method it embeds is always objectionable to someone. Toolmakers try to nudge users toward open science or privacy-by-design (Peeters and Hagen, 2022), but there is no guarantee users will follow design intents. Accountability is therefore distributed among the user, the tool itself, and its maker, and that distribution can never be taken for granted (Latour, 1994).

Beyond methodological accountability, toolmakers face legal risk. Meta (Facebook, Instagram) and X have filed lawsuits against web scraping (Hutchinson, 2024; ACLU, 2024), exposing researchers and toolmakers who relied on scrapers. The chapter raises this as an open question: is a DC tool more like a knife (where agency lies in the hand that holds it) or more like a highly toxic chemical (harmful by its existence)? The example of DMI's Censorship Explorer illustrates how a tool can put its maker in tension with platform terms of service or national law. Johanna Drucker once told Jacomy, "What a plague you unleashed upon us!" for co-creating Gephi. Toolmakers respond to legal and ethical risk in different ways, from ignoring it entirely to keeping a tool relatively confidential to following FAIR principles (Lamprecht et al., 2020) or abstaining from implementing certain features.

A further constraint is material. The data and algorithms themselves resist the toolmaker. An algorithm may be too slow or too memory-intensive, a data structure may not support all necessary cases, two seemingly complementary techniques may turn out to be irreconcilable, a visualization may create anti-patterns that confuse half of its users, or a social media platform may close its API. Toolmakers often do what they can rather than what they wish, and some criticism they receive targets things outside their control or that they cannot afford given their time and resources.

## Collaborative Networks and How to Engage

Most toolmakers risk isolation because caring for technology shapes their professional life in ways that differ from standard academic or support roles. Collaboration with other toolmakers and technologists is both a practical necessity and a source of satisfaction. No single person can cover algorithm development, technical maintenance, system administration, UX design, community management, fundraising, and academic valorization.

Toolmakers build companionship networks to access competencies they lack, find mentorship, and develop their craft. These networks are often invisible to surrounding researchers because they operate through infrastructure specific to toolmaker culture, notably GitHub and IRC channels. Code for most DCSSH tool projects is hosted on GitHub.

This matters for scholars who want support or new features. Demands from within the companionship network are addressed quickly because of mutual dependency. Demands from outside are more likely to succeed if the scholar adopts community norms: filing an issue on GitHub rather than sending an email is the expected approach. If the scholar can reach a trusted toolmaker who acts as an intermediary, that also helps. Geoffrey Rockwell describes his long-running partnership with a collaborator he calls Stéfan as built "paper by paper, tool by tool, interface fiddle by interface fiddle" over years, and he stresses that the human elements of care and sustained collaboration were what made the work possible.

## Implications for Scholars

Knowing this landscape helps scholars understand why certain tools exist while others do not, why some tools are maintained and others are abandoned, and how to communicate effectively with toolmakers. The chapter's broader argument is that the precariousness of DCSSH tools is rooted in institutional structures that do not recognize toolmaking as a legitimate career. The solution it proposes is creating an identified career path for DCSSH toolmakers.
