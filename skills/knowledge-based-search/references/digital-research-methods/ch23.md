---
title: Why DCSSH Toolmakers Make Tools
source: Handbook of Digital and Computational Research Methods (Madsen, Munk)
---

Chapter 22 (labeled ch23 in this volume) is written by Mathieu Jacomy, Bernhard Rieder, Erik Borra, Guillaume Plique, Kenneth Christian Enevoldsen, and Nees Jan van Eck. The authors are themselves toolmakers in the digital and computational social sciences and humanities (DCSSH), and they draw on that experience throughout.

## The central argument

DCSSH toolmakers build tools to express and disseminate their own scientific agenda. Because a tool functions as a product with a public identity, the toolmaker is held accountable for what it does. That accountability pushes toolmakers to keep the tool aligned with their own methodological views, which reinforces the identification of the tool with its maker. The chapter traces several consequences of this situation: particular design priorities, a specific economy of motivation and pride, and a relationship with users that can reshape the tool over time.

## Tools as products and scientific instruments

When an infrastructure gains an identity and a purpose it becomes a product. The authors borrow this framing from Chapter 20 of the same volume. Being a product means the tool is attributed to an authoritative figure or group, is curated, and is intended for a specific public. Most DCSSH tools are small and have a single toolmaker or a handful of them who absorb all accountability.

Large enough projects, such as OpenRefine (originally Freebase Gridworks, conceived by David Huynh in 2008), can distribute accountability across a committee, an online forum, a lead maintainer, and a scientific advisory group. But this is the exception.

Tools are not simply implementations of methods that exist in the toolmaker's mind beforehand. Many tools are byproducts of scientific exploration in which implementation and method are co-constituted, invented together. The scientific agenda the tool asserts may not preexist its construction. Even so, once the tool exists and circulates, its maker is attributed that agenda and finds it difficult to distance themselves from it. The Gephi toolmakers have criticized certain uses of community detection in their tool, but tutorials and user communities have nonetheless treated it as a standard part of the Gephi method.

## The researcher-toolmaker profile

The chapter distinguishes a researcher-toolmaker from a research software engineer (RSE). An RSE is in principle serviceable: their agenda is set by the researchers they support, and their time is shared across many projects, which generates organizational overhead. A researcher-toolmaker collaborates only when the collaboration is low-friction and mutually beneficial, and approaches it more like a peer relationship between researchers. This distinction matters because the researcher-toolmaker is driven by their own scientific perspective, not a service mandate.

The costs of design, maintenance, and accountability remain hidden from researchers who approach a toolmaker with a tool idea. Those researchers typically underestimate what toolmaking entails and assume that coding a simple tool costs only a moderate amount of time.

## Pride, motivation, and the economy of care

One does not become a toolmaker by writing code but by releasing and sustaining a tool. The economy of toolmaking is dominated by the low cost of withdrawing versus the high cost of keeping the tool alive. Toolmakers accept that burden in part because of recognition.

Geoffrey Rockwell, maker of Voyant Tools, is quoted in an interview by Jacomy (2023): "I do not think anyone will ever read the books and the articles I wrote at some point in the future. But I certainly am known for Voyant." Recognition is a reward but not usually the initial motivation for creating a tool. It becomes a reason to keep sustaining one.

Pride and love for the tool are described as necessary components of the care economy. Without them, the toolmaker abandons the tool. These feelings coexist with frustration: toolmakers can feel enclosed in the role of caretaker when they have broader ambitions.

Several costs attach to the toolmaker role. Fears of exposure (buggy code, poor code quality, etiquette violations in open-source communities). The risk of becoming "that-tool-guy," reduced to the caretaker of one artifact. Hidden labor: maintenance and machine administration are invisible events that require the toolmaker to justify time spent on them, sometimes by doing it outside working hours. Insufficient academic attribution: many researchers have invested heavily in research and coding and received only a footnote acknowledgment.

The Journal of Open Source Software (JOSS) and DOI assignment for software versions offer partial remedies by making tools citable, which can yield academic citations alongside or instead of proxy papers.

## Design intent 1: Ease of use

Toolmakers are the first users of their own tools. They automate and optimize for themselves, then extend that optimization to students and collaborators. Ease of use reflects a belief that accessibility is good, but toolmakers also recognize that simplicity has limits.

The chapter identifies four types of difficulty a tool can present. First, bugs and instability. Second, poor visual or interaction design. Third, feature overload that overwhelms the user. Fourth, decisions the tool does not help the user make but that the user must make correctly. The fourth type is deliberately preserved by some toolmakers because the method requires human judgment, or because the decision is a methodological commitment that users should consciously face.

DC tools for SSH aim to produce interpretive insights, not just output. Automating away too many decisions can undermine that aim by allowing users to obtain results without understanding what those results depend on.

## Design intent 2: Play and learning-by-doing

Some tools are designed so that using them teaches the user about methods, algorithms, and different ways to accomplish tasks. Rockwell contrasts this with a "wizard" model that forces a sequence of decisions and installations before the user sees anything.

The authors invoke Stephen Ramsay's concept of the "hermeneutics of screwing around" (Ramsay, 2010): browsing and stumbling through a tool rather than executing a targeted search is a legitimate research methodology. Exploratory use of tools can reveal things even the toolmaker did not anticipate.

Two reasons drive this design preference. First, toolmakers prefer to convey knowledge through the tool's affordances rather than through documentation. If they did not, they would write text rather than code. Second, toolmaking itself involves play, surprise, and creative problem-solving, and toolmakers extend that sensibility to the tools they build.

## Design intent 3: Ecosystem coexistence

Open-source DCSSH tools are built for coexistence, not market dominance. Toolmakers face an unfunded maintenance burden (the authors use the phrase "Unfunded Maintenance" as a shared boat they are all rowing in), and that burden creates a natural division of labor. If another tool already handles a feature, it is more efficient to send users there and avoid maintaining it yourself.

Practical markers of this orientation: DCSSH tools typically use universal formats (CSV, JSON, XML) rather than proprietary file formats. Toolmakers generally avoid encroaching on the scope of another tool and ask permission before reusing algorithms. Rockwell describes the early TAPoR project (text analysis portal) as an "imperial project" that tried to do everything for everyone, which proved unsustainable. Voyant took the opposite approach.

Toolmakers exist within a large cooperative network of software developers that runs largely parallel to the research community. As a user, you can rarely afford to learn only one tool for a given need, because toolmakers have divided labor deliberately.

## Design intent 4: Resistance to feature creep

Users and toolmakers rarely disagree about how a feature should work. They disagree about scope: whether a feature should exist in the tool at all. Users often underestimate the cost of implementing features because the blackboxing of the tool hides that complexity.

The chapter cites an undo feature in Gephi that users have demanded for more than a decade and that toolmakers have worked on for years without shipping, as an example of how ubiquitous features in commercial software can be unexpectedly difficult to implement.

"Featuritis" (also called feature creep) is described as a pathology of aging tools in which the accumulation of features becomes detrimental to maintenance or usability. Tools that survive long enough inevitably face it. Some toolmakers respond by heavily curating features and keeping the tool minimal. Voyant is cited as a tool that has accumulated substantial functionality over time. The addition of a notebook programming environment is offered as one way to handle user demands without expanding the core tool: users who want custom features can build them in the notebook context, and the best ones may eventually be incorporated into the main tool.

Some tools are opinionated (Gephisto is given as an example), while others are methodologically inclusive (Scikit-learn, Pedregosa et al., 2011, is cited). But all toolmakers agree that feature creep is a risk.

Features demanded against terms of service (as sometimes requested of 4CAT or the YouTube Data Tools) represent a category of request that toolmakers must refuse regardless of how reasonable the feature sounds in isolation.

## How users reshape tools

Tools travel differently from papers. They build communities in unexpected ways. Gephi has been repurposed by search engine optimization (SEO) consultants who developed their own non-scientific practices around it, constituting what the authors call (following Knorr Cetina, 1999) an epistemic subculture.

The Digital Methods Initiative (DMI) in Amsterdam built several tools from the principle that various media require their own methods. Richard Rogers, DMI's director, "positions digital methods as research sensitive to the methods embedded in the medium, as opposed to applying existing methods from social sciences and humanities." Tools from DMI include 4CAT (Peeters and Hagen, 2022), DMI-TCAT (Borra and Rieder, 2014), Netvizz (Rieder, 2013), and the YouTube Data Tools (Rieder, 2015), all built by repurposing platform APIs for research.

Repurposing a tool without touching its code is itself a form of methodological innovation. User communities that publish tutorials, documentation, teaching materials, data sprint reports, and scientific papers about a tool co-design it alongside the toolmaker. DCSSH tools exist not only in their source code but in all the cultural artifacts that contextualize them.

## The apparatus is not shaped by academic need alone

The chapter closes with an explicit caution: no superior authority decides which tools DCSSH should have, and current tools cannot be assumed to cover the needs of SSH researchers. The apparatus is shaped by the contingent motivations, constraints, and perspectives of individual toolmakers.

Researchers have more agency than they may realize. They can repurpose tools and publish that knowledge, budget desired features into grant applications, collaborate directly with toolmakers, or become toolmakers themselves. These paths allow researchers to bring new design intents into the ecosystem and steer tool development in directions the existing toolmakers have not pursued.
