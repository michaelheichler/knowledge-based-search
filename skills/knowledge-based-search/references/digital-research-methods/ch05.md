## Overview

Chapter 5, by Christian Borch and Nicholas Skar-Gislinge, argues that the digitalization of financial markets has outpaced the methodological toolkit of economic sociology, and that agent-based modeling (ABM) offers a productive response to that gap. The chapter moves through three main topics: the diagnosis of a methodological problem, a review of existing ABM work on financial markets, and a worked illustration of a portfolio agent designed by the authors.

## The Methodological Problem

Economic sociology and the sociology of finance have traditionally relied on qualitative methods: interviews with market participants and ethnographic fieldwork on trading floors. This approach has been productive for markets where human beings make consequential decisions, because the people doing the work can explain, at least partially, what they are doing and why.

Financial markets in many countries have become fully digital in their core operation. Orders to buy or sell assets are submitted electronically and are executed by automated algorithms. Some high-speed algorithms respond to market events at the level of nanoseconds. When these systems interact, the dominant actors in the market are algorithms, not humans.

This shift creates a specific problem for qualitative research. Even if sociologists can interview or observe the software developers and traders who build algorithmic systems, those interviews do not necessarily reveal how the algorithms actually behave in live markets. Algorithms sometimes produce outcomes their designers did not intend. This can happen because of bugs or design flaws, but it can also happen because thousands of competing algorithms observe the same electronic order book simultaneously, each acting on "if...then" logic, and the interaction of those deterministic rules produces emergent, unanticipated patterns. Studying the algorithm designers cannot substitute for studying the interaction among algorithms directly.

The authors note this limitation applies to both "first-generation" algorithmic systems (which are fully rule-specified by humans) and "second-generation" machine-learning-based systems that have grown common since the mid-2010s. With machine learning, questions about model explainability add further complication, but the core methodological issue is the difficulty of predicting interaction effects regardless of generation.

## Why Agent-Based Modeling

ABM is a simulation approach in which artificial agents, each endowed with a set of rules or preferences, interact with one another inside a defined environment. The researcher can then observe what macro-level patterns emerge from those micro-level interactions. The classic sociological reference point for this kind of logic is Thomas Schelling's 1971 model of residential segregation, which showed that collective outcomes nobody desires can arise from mild individual preferences. Macy and Flache (2009) summarize the core intellectual commitment as two ideas: macrosocial patterns emerge from microsocial processes, and what aggregates from micro to macro are consequential decisions rather than static attributes.

The authors' case for ABM in this context is pragmatic rather than theoretical. They are not proposing to replace other explanatory frameworks. They argue that, for a specific set of research questions about automated trading, qualitative methods are simply insufficient because the phenomena of interest occur at the level of algorithm-to-algorithm interaction, not at the level of human experience.

ABM does not give direct access to real markets. Simulation is not the real thing. But a well-designed ABM can model the interaction level of automated systems, including how individual algorithmic behaviors can produce collective crashes or other emergent outcomes.

## Existing ABM Work on Financial Markets

ABM of financial markets has been developed mainly by scholars working in econophysics and computer science, not by sociologists. The chapter identifies three major features of this literature.

First, the primary validation criterion for financial ABMs is the reproduction of "stylized facts": statistical properties that have been consistently observed in real asset markets. One important stylized fact is the absence of autocorrelation in asset returns, meaning that past price movements do not reliably predict future ones. A model that cannot reproduce such properties is unlikely to generate useful insights about real market dynamics. Platt and Gebbie (2018) discuss this validation framework.

Second, a major strand of this research uses "zero-intelligence" models, developed principally by Doyne Farmer and colleagues. In Farmer, Patelli, and Zovko (2005), agents place buy and sell orders randomly, without any utility-maximizing logic. The finding is that even these minimally specified agents, interacting in an environment that captures basic features of electronic order books, can reproduce certain real-life market properties. The implication is that market microstructure may matter more than individual agent rationality in producing some market outcomes.

Third, these models do embed realistic features of market environments. The Farmer, Patelli, and Zovko (2005) model includes a double auction mechanism, which is the standard system used by many exchanges: buyers and sellers place orders continuously, and the exchange matches corresponding buy and sell orders.

The chapter identifies a significant limitation in this literature. Many econophysics ABMs are highly parsimonious and abstract, often using only two types of agents, both acting randomly. Such models may reproduce some stylized facts without capturing the actor heterogeneity or temporal structure of actual markets. The authors single out temporal hierarchies as particularly important in contemporary automated trading. Latency, meaning the time it takes for algorithms to receive and respond to order-book updates, creates a structured inequality among participants. Menkveld (2013) and Wah and Wellman (2016) address aspects of this. The question the authors pose is whether the sociological literature's richer picture of market complexity could be incorporated into ABMs to make them more informative.

The evolution of market making is one example of that complexity. Market makers, whose function is to provide liquidity by continuously posting willingness to buy and sell, have moved from being human specialists on physical exchange floors to being automated algorithms operating at speed. Min and Borch (2022) cover this transition. An ABM aspiring to model current markets needs to represent this.

The authors' own work (Borch et al., 2022) develops a large multi-agent ABM that incorporates more agent heterogeneity and a more realistic market environment than prior models and is able to reproduce more stylized facts. This work was developed alongside approximately 200 interviews with professionals in the automated trading industry, primarily people working in London, New York, and Chicago. Interview informants with long experience in automated market making identified features of that activity that were then incorporated into agent design. This is an example of combining qualitative field access with quantitative simulation.

## Illustration: A Portfolio Agent

Because space constraints prevent presenting the full model, the chapter presents one component: a portfolio agent based on the investment strategy associated with Harry Markowitz (1952).

The strategic logic follows Markowitz's mean-variance optimization. The agent holds a portfolio of three risky stocks (labeled ABC, DEF, and GHI in the simulation) and can also hold cash at zero risk-free return. Its goal is to find the portfolio weights that maximize the Sharpe ratio, which measures expected return relative to risk. A higher Sharpe ratio indicates a better risk-adjusted investment. The optimization the agent solves is:

max over w of (rE transposed times w) divided by the square root of (w transposed times Sigma times w)

where rE is the vector of expected returns for the three stocks and Sigma is the covariance matrix of their returns.

The agent estimates expected returns by using earnings announcements and expected earnings growth. Earnings are modeled as a random walk with drift. The agent converts an earnings forecast into an expected future price using a price-to-earnings ratio. Its estimate of future earnings has a standard deviation that is larger than the actual earnings process standard deviation, meaning agents systematically overestimate price variation and trade more than a perfectly informed agent would. This reflects the finding by Odean (1999) that investors tend to trade too much. An element drawn from Black (1986) is incorporated through a 10 percent probability at each portfolio check that the agent will update its expectations even without new earnings, representing idiosyncratic noise trading.

The agent's behavioral cycle is defined procedurally:

On start, the agent sets a rebalance threshold (randomly drawn between 1 and 5 percent) and calculates an initial random portfolio. At market open each day, it checks for new earnings, updates its portfolio horizon if the forecast period has expired, and sets a random timer between 5 and 30 minutes. When the timer fires, the agent recalculates actual portfolio weights, compares them to target weights, and rebalances if the combined deviation across all stocks exceeds the threshold. With 10 percent probability, it also makes a new price forecast regardless of the threshold check. At market close, it cancels pending timers.

The simulation environment contains 100 portfolio agents, 9 algorithmic market makers maintaining near-zero net positions, and 30 liquidity traders who submit random orders. Liquidity traders simulate external demand for immediacy and produce intraday fluctuations in order flow. The exchange runs a continuous double auction with price-time priority: among orders at the same price, the earlier-arriving order is matched first. Limit orders (orders at a specified price) and market orders (orders at the current price) are both available.

The authors run the simulation for a year of trading, repeated eight times with different random seeds. With annual earnings growth set at 5 percent and a 5 percent standard deviation for all three stocks, the simulated daily returns show an average of 0.02 percent and a standard deviation of 0.34 percent, consistent with the specified parameters. The distribution of daily returns shows a sharp peak and broad tails. A QQ plot confirms that the distribution deviates from the normal distribution in a manner consistent with a heavy-tailed distribution. Heavy tails in asset return distributions are a well-documented stylized fact (Cont, 2001). Reproducing this pattern provides a first validation step for the model.

The authors note that the portfolio agent simulation could in principle be used to study whether markets with many portfolio agents are more vulnerable to price cascades across stocks, since each portfolio agent considers multiple stocks simultaneously and could propagate a shock from one to another. They do not pursue that analysis in this chapter. Their goal is to show how a realistic, non-random agent can be designed and how it produces outputs consistent with known empirical regularities.

## Conclusion and Research Program

The chapter ends with the recommendation that future computational economic sociology pursue ABMs in which both agents and market environments are designed with a high degree of realism. The authors see their combination of extensive field interviews with formal simulation modeling as a template: qualitative access to market practitioners informs which agent properties and market features to include, while the ABM allows the exploration of interaction effects that qualitative methods cannot reach.

Two further extensions are flagged as important. First, the rise of machine learning in trading strategies means ABMs will need to include self-learning and self-adapting agents. Second, agents that respond to non-order-book information sources, including social media content, will be needed to reflect current market practice. Both raise questions about agency and explainability (Borch and Min, 2022, 2023) that sit at the intersection of sociology and computer science.
