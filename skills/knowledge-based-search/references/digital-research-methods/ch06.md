## Overview

This chapter by Dario Rodighiero argues that datafication gives organizations an opportunity to redesign their charts so that the charts capture human complexity, represent every member, and reflect democratic rather than hierarchical structures. The argument draws on the author's doctoral and postdoctoral research and is grounded in a case study called the Affinity Map.

## The Problem with Existing Organizational Charts

Organizational charts have been used in management since at least the early twentieth century, when consulting engineer Willard C. Brinton published *Graphic Methods for Presenting Facts* (1919). A more detailed precedent exists in Daniel McCallum's mid-nineteenth-century diagram of the New York and Erie Railroads, which represented every employee from the board of directors to track workers, arranged like a tree with the board as the root and workers as leaves. Since McCallum, charts have been drastically simplified. Contemporary charts place the president at the top of a pyramid, rendering lower-ranked employees invisible and adding what the chapter calls an unhealthy sense of superiority. The chapter reads this simplification as a failure to represent what Norbert Elias called individual diversity and what Bruno Latour (Actor-Network Theory) called the sociotechnical dimensions of human complexity.

The philosophical tension between the individual and the whole runs from Aristotle through Gestalt theory. When Christian von Ehrenfels wrote that "the whole is more than the sum of its parts," he was stating a principle the chapter applies to organigram design: charts must show individual identity and collective dynamics together, just as a typographer balances text, image, and negative space in a layout.

## Datafication and Digital Traces

Datafication is the process of converting human behavior into continuous streams of data through digital infrastructures. Rob Kitchin's *The Data Revolution* (2014) and Jose van Dijck (2014) ground the chapter's use of the concept. Digital traces come from everyday actions: an identity card opening a door, a smartphone accessing email, a laptop connecting to a local network. More invasive methods such as facial recognition exist but are not the chapter's focus.

The chapter describes the data life cycle not as a linear pipeline (as in Nathan Shedroff's 1999 model) but as a circular, iterative process (following Leonelli, 2019). The author extends this into a lemniscate shape, adapting Boris Groys's theory of self-design (2008): one loop represents the organization processing data, the other represents employees who are shaped by the system's recommendations and whose behavior then produces new data. The two loops share a flow but remain distinct.

Kurt Lewin's field theory (1951) adds a further frame: employees act as forces maintaining organizational equilibrium, and real-time data accelerate reorganization when that equilibrium is disturbed.

## Digital Traces and Organizational Decisions

The chapter examines how digital traces might influence high-stakes organizational decisions such as promotions. Authority creates disparities in control and salaries among employees, and promotions are tightly regulated by bureaucratic mechanisms designed to ensure fairness, as described by Max Weber (2019), who argued that bureaucracy favors more impartial management. Should digital traces begin to inform promotion decisions, algorithmic thinking would enter organizational decision-making in new ways. Academic citation analysis offers an illustrative case: citation counts influence recruitment, particularly in technical fields, but they also distort the job market by favoring popular research areas over minor topics.

## Conditions That Make Redesign Possible

The chapter identifies four factors that together make it possible to redesign organizational charts using digital traces.

1. The widespread production of digital traces provides raw material that can restore lost complexity.
2. Advances in digital design allow more sophisticated, interactive visualizations (Lowgren and Stolterman, 2004).
3. Information design has matured as a recognized field, drawing on Meirelles (2013) and Tufte (1997).
4. Improved network algorithms, including web-based tools such as D3 (Bostock, Ogievetsky, and Heer, 2011), allow handling of large datasets and engagement with broad audiences.

## Historical Background: Networks and Sociograms

Network visualization traces back to Leonhard Euler's eighteenth-century graph theory, which emerged from the Konigsberg bridges problem (whether all bridges in the city could be crossed without repeating any). Euler's mathematical abstraction was turned into a visual grammar by psychiatrist Jacob L. Moreno in the twentieth century. Moreno used tabular questionnaires to study friendships among schoolchildren and devised sociograms: nodes represented individuals, directed lines represented relationships (*Who Shall Survive?*, 1934). Computational social science (a term introduced by Lazer et al., 2009) extended Moreno's approach to large social structures. Two research laboratories were central to this development: Albert-Laszlo Barabasi's lab at Northeastern University and Bruno Latour's medialab at Sciences Po Paris.

## The Four Design Principles

These principles were first presented at Universite de Liege in a seminar titled *Images Today: Archives, Identities, and Algorithms*.

**1. Represent Every Employee.** Every member of an organization, from directors to workers, should appear in the chart. McCallum's nineteenth-century diagram achieved this even without full names for each individual. Digital traces, such as office presence inferred from badge scans or Wi-Fi connections, make inclusive representation feasible. The chapter notes that privacy regulations currently prevent use of email communication data, but such data would reveal connectivity patterns that differ from hierarchical relations.

**2. Convey a Sense of Community.** Excluding employees from organizational charts excludes their identity from mutual recognition. An organizational chart functions like a class photograph: it is an instrument for being seen and for seeing others. Belonging cannot develop without representation. Connectivity between peers, not just top-down hierarchy, constitutes a community. Datafication applied uniformly (every employee scanning a badge, every employee on the same network) can make connectivity a shared, democratic feature of the chart.

**3. Avoid Evaluation and Comparison.** Hierarchical tree diagrams create implicit rankings. Network visualizations, following Latour's account of Actor-Network Theory, place all actors on the same plane without predetermined centers or apexes. Representing individuals with symbols of different sizes (scaled by salary or seniority, for example) encodes quantitative differences that reduce individual complexity to a single dimension. Financial data belongs to the private sphere (drawing on Ranciere, 2000). Collaboration data belongs to the public sphere of organizational life and is appropriate to visualize.

**4. Support Transparency and Inclusion.** Designers must disclose the methodology behind an organizational chart: which data were collected, which metrics were used, and how design decisions were made. Agreement with the individuals represented is part of this disclosure. In the Affinity Map project, some employees accepted internal visibility but not public display of their identities, which led to two versions of the map: one for employees showing names, one for the public showing only laboratory names.

## Datafication in Academia: A Critical Example

Academia illustrates both the benefits and distortions of datafication. Citation indexes, originally conceived by Eugene Garfield (1970) to help librarians with journal subscriptions, became tools for evaluating scholars and influencing recruitment. The chapter cites Yves Gingras (2014) on how this distorted the academic job market, for example by making minor research topics less attractive because they receive fewer citations than popular areas like artificial intelligence. A Dutch initiative (VSNU et al., 2019) proposed career diversity metrics to counter this reductiveness.

The chapter treats citation impact as evidence that any single digital trace provides a fragmentary translation of actual practice. Academic work is described as "heterogeneous, multidimensional, and as rich as human nature," and observation of scientists in the style of Latour and Woolgar's *Laboratory Life* (1986) reveals complexity that citation counts cannot capture.

## Case Study: The Affinity Map

The Affinity Map was developed for the ENAC school. It began as a decision-support tool for managers but was unanimously redirected by a public assembly into an instrument for all employees.

The metric underlying the map is collaboration: co-authorship of publications, co-teaching of courses, and shared supervision, all drawn from institutional information systems. This metric was chosen as an alternative to citation indexes and to encourage interdisciplinarity.

The map's structural innovation is a two-level network. Conventional network visualizations connect all nodes on a single surface. The Affinity Map nests networks within nodes: each node in the outer network is a laboratory, and within each laboratory node is a smaller network of laboratory members. This design avoids the visual clutter of thousands of crossing lines and protects individuals who have few or no collaborations, particularly doctoral students just starting. Without this nesting, isolated individuals appear as detached nodes at the edges, conveying exclusion. Laboratory nodes act like cocoons, situating newcomers within a community before they have built individual connections.

A physical version of the map, measuring 15 by 15 meters, was printed on tarpaulin and laid on the floor of a hall in the ENAC school. Employees were invited to walk on it and speak with the design team. This process allowed employees to contribute to the design. One employee's concern about public visibility of identities led to the decision that names would be visible only after logging in through the institution's system, making them inaccessible outside.

The chapter identifies a remaining problem: network visualizations introduce a centrality issue. Individuals at the edges of the network expressed discomfort with that placement. The author's subsequent work explored mapping on continuous spherical surfaces as a way to remove center and periphery altogether.

The most promising trace identified during the project was linguistic distance: the similarity of language used in publications and presentations. Language connects individuals even when they have no direct collaborations, so it offers a metric that can include everyone. Advances in AI and large-scale corpus computation (following Franco Moretti's *Graphs, Maps, Trees*, 2005) made this calculation feasible. Linguistic proximity can also reveal potential collaborations between people in a large organization who have never met.

## Conclusion

The chapter calls on organizational scholars to incorporate digital traces and network design into their methods. Digital traces reveal behavioral patterns that traditional charts do not show. The Affinity Map demonstrates both the possibilities and the unresolved problems: the partiality of datafication, the tension between individual privacy and collective representation, and the unsolved centrality problem in network layouts. The chapter frames datafication as a process still under development, with open questions about how to represent the dynamics of medium and large organizations in ways that are democratic, inclusive, and transparent.
