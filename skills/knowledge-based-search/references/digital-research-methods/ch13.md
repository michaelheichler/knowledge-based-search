## Overview

Chapter 13, written by Hjalmar Bang Carlsen, argues that the digital data revolution has focused disproportionately on data handling at the expense of theory. Programming skills in R and Python are valued, courses are devoted to data management, but the ability to work creatively and critically with theory has been pushed to the background. The chapter's central claim is that digital data adds little value without theoretical engagement: it is through a theoretical lens that found digital traces, data produced by organizations for non-scientific purposes, become valuable knowledge.

The chapter surveys two dominant research strategies for working with digital data, deductive theory testing and inductive theory generation, diagnoses problems with each, and then proposes a three-phase approach (imagining, discovery, grounding) that draws on abductive reasoning and qualitative familiarity with the material.

---

## Features of Digital Data Relevant to Theory

Ruppert et al. (2013) identify several properties of digital data that distinguish it from traditional sources: it captures transactions rather than individual accounts, records continuous rather than bundled time, and allows unprecedented granularity. Salganik (2019) stresses scale, which enables the study of heterogeneity and rare events. Digital data also includes large-scale open-ended text at the level of whole populations. These features are not fixed across platforms or over time, but they consistently differ from what surveys, experiments, or ethnographies provide, and they open possibilities for theoretical innovation.

## Deductive Strategy: Testing Theories with Digital Data

Many social science theories concern social interaction, relations, communication, and behavior, things that surveys capture poorly. John Levi Martin argues that one goal of social science is to eliminate theories that do not explain social life well (Martin, 2018). The problem is that data limitations have left many theories empirically untestable, living as what Carlsen calls "ghosts." When the unmeasurable becomes measurable through digital data, old untested theories can be mobilized (Golder and Macy, 2014; Latour et al., 2012).

Mapping theory to digital data still requires creative theoretical work. Andrew Abbott's Methods of Discovery (2004) catalogs heuristics for this, including shifting levels of analysis and making a strong assumption.

Two examples illustrate these heuristics. First, Carlsen's own research used Facebook groups of social movements to collect within-group interaction data at scale, shifting from organizational actors to patterns of interaction to explain collective action. Second, Golder and Macy (2011) assumed that Twitter posts indicate the mood of individual users. This strong assumption produced a large cross-cultural dataset on sentiment at granular temporal resolution, revealing that individuals express more positive sentiment in the morning than at other times of day, a finding robust across cultures.

The chapter also identifies serious drawbacks of pure theory testing. Digital data is qualitative in the sense that it records what people say or do at specific moments, so the practices generating the data must be examined carefully. For the Facebook movement example, one must verify that rank-and-file members actually interact in those groups rather than having leaders post without response. For the Golder and Macy example, one must ask whether communicative conventions (saying "good morning" more than "good night") rather than actual mood differences drive temporal patterns. More fundamentally, because digitalization alters the social practices it records (Marres, 2017), digital data demands some reconceptualization of existing theories rather than straightforward application.

## Inductive Strategy: Generating Theory from Data, Grounded Theory and Its Computational Variant

The central tradition for data-driven theory generation in social science is grounded theory (GT), developed by Glaser and Strauss. GT holds that theory should be "systematically worked out in relation to the data during the course of research" rather than deduced from prior assumptions (Glaser and Strauss, 2009, p. 6). Its central technique is the constant comparative method: each instance of a category is compared with prior instances to identify defining features, properties, and relations to other categories, working through open coding toward an integrated theory. GT combines systematic coding (shared with quantitative content analysis) with qualitative theory development.

### Computational Grounded Theory

With the emergence of large digital text corpora, Laura Nelson formalized a computational extension called Computational Grounded Theory (CGT) in a 2017 paper. CGT has three steps.

**Pattern discovery.** Computational models, specifically unsupervised methods such as LDA topic models, reduce messy text into simpler representations (lists or networks of words). This is supposed to let categories emerge from the data without researcher bias, and Nelson argues the output is "fully and immediately reproducible" unlike human-coded text (Nelson, 2017, p. 13).

**Pattern refinement (computationally guided deep reading).** The researcher reads documents identified by the algorithm as representative of each topic, confirming plausibility, adding interpretation, and modifying patterns. The algorithm selects documents so that the researcher can "read and interpret any amount of text without the burden of reading the full text" (Nelson, 2017, p. 26). Saturation is guaranteed by the model having processed the entire corpus. The researcher reads paradigmatic examples the model surfaces.

**Pattern confirmation.** The model's measure of a category is correlated with external variables that indicate the same category, providing indirect validation.

## Limitations of Computational Grounded Theory

Carlsen's analysis draws heavily on Carlsen and Ralund (2022), which critically assessed CGT and the wider use of LDA topic models in social science. Four problems are identified.

**Discovery and measurement conflated.** CGT uses the same LDA model for both discovering categories and measuring them. This limits the risks that can be taken in the theory-generation phase, because LDA must produce interpretable results. As Ramage et al. (2009, p. 4) noted, "a topic model's trustworthiness must be determined by informed human judgments" and models must "find what we know is there." This falls far short of the ambition Evans and Aceves (2016, p. 25) describe, where "induced data structures can surprise, challenging presumptions or pre-existing theory." Evans and Foster (2024) argue for inverse supervised models trained to surface the unexpected, in contrast to LDA which is not designed for surprise. Carlsen draws on Peirce's account of abduction: novel theoretical propositions arise from surprising facts, and the method should be designed to surface them rather than confirm the expected.

**Unqualified interpretation.** CGT assumes that minimal immersion in paradigmatic documents is enough to interpret categories. Carlsen and Ralund argue, along with Tavory and Timmermans (2014), that recognizing something as a surprising fact with theoretical consequence requires a qualified interpreter, one who has engaged in intensive and extensive reading and who has become a competent actor in the field site. As Lichterman (2017) observes for ethnography, mastery of the requirements for interaction in the field site must precede analytical claims.

**Model limitations.** LDA is used as if it provides an exhaustive overview of themes in a corpus, but LDA is known to "hide" clusters within other clusters, so any returned topic may contain many distinct sub-topics. Furthermore, LDA assumes that word co-occurrence reflects shared themes, an assumption that fails in news-heavy discourse where co-occurrence is driven by news events rather than stable topics.

**Representative versus paradigmatic documents.** CGT treats the top documents returned by a topic model as representative (standing in for the population of documents) or paradigmatic (being the clearest example of a logic). These are different things. Carlsen and Ralund offer the example that Biggie is a paradigmatic rapper but not representative of the average rapper. Word distributions are highly uncertain indications of meaning. A document with many topic-relevant words is more likely to actually concern the topic, but it does not follow that it represents the remaining documents, nor that it most clearly captures their underlying logic. Reading top documents is a reasonable starting point but cannot support claims about many thousands of other documents.

## A Proposed Solution: Three-Phase Approach with CALM

Carlsen argues that both deductive and inductive strategies have important lessons. The deductive approach stresses creatively imagining how digital data could support testing or developing theories constrained by earlier data limits. The inductive approach focuses on discovering patterns that develop or question established theories. He proposes three phases, following Tavory and Timmermans' Abductive Analysis (2014) as a guide.

### Imagining

At the outset of a project, the researcher thinks through what theoretical moves a digital data source could support, without yet doing extensive data analysis. The goal is a set of theoretical paths that could address questions relevant to a research community.

Carlsen illustrates this with a Facebook dataset of Danish political actors' posts and users' likes and comments. Three possible framings are considered and compared. (1) Political participation: likes and comments resemble letters to politicians or petition signing, providing individual-level continuous observational data. This is challenged by the low cost of the action (clicktivism), described by Lewis et al. (2014) as "Facebook conjured an illusion of activism rather than facilitating the real thing." (2) Public opinion: reacting to a politician's post resembles responding to a survey item as an "opinion statement" (Zaller, 1992). This is challenged by selection problems (social media publics are not representative of national publics, Conrad et al., 2019). (3) Mobilized public opinion: a reconceptualization that treats likes and comments as the public that mobilizes its opinion on social media. This makes the selection problem a theoretical resource rather than a flaw, and opens questions about who mobilizes, how, and with what effects on political elites.

### Discovery

The next phase investigates whether proposed theories apply and how to develop initial ideas. Carlsen and Ralund's CALM (Computer Assisted Learning and Measurement) framework is introduced here. CALM uses unsupervised machine learning (including LDA) to explore and discover topics, but treats the clusters as uncertain indications of categories rather than definitive groupings. Top words from relevant clusters are used to build search queries for document sampling. The query list is expanded using word embedding (specifically word2vec) to find semantically similar words. Documents are sampled for intensive and extensive reading. False positives are acceptable during discovery as long as they do not overwhelm the analysis. Query terms are updated as interpretation develops. Clusters are not assumed to be exhaustive, and their boundaries are not treated as fixed constraints.

### Grounding

In the grounding phase, the list of promising theoretical perspectives is narrowed, and the goal is to anchor interpretation firmly in empirical material and work out the details. For the mobilized public opinion example, this could involve developing a typology of ways citizens mobilize opinions on Facebook and specifying relations among those ways. Word2vec is used to move through semantic space, trying interpretations in different contexts and seeking out boundary cases to specify where a category begins and ends. The output is an empirically grounded conceptualization and a set of theoretical expectations that can then be developed and tested quantitatively using measures prepared during the discovery and grounding phases.

## Broader Applicability and the Role of LLMs

The chapter closes by extending the argument beyond social media text. Online search data captures what people are curious about at population scale, and the theoretical possibilities remain largely unexplored. Mobile sensory data records physical movement and co-presence at unprecedented scale and granularity, enabling questions that bridge social interaction analysis and social network analysis (for example, how interaction group size varies across socio-demographic groups or which social settings foster tie formation). Sensory data differs from text data in that it does not reproduce what the interactants themselves experienced, limiting the kind of qualitative analysis that text supports, but it still supports theory testing and development.

On large language models (LLMs): generative LLMs can assist in the imagining phase by proposing theoretical concepts, borrowing from other literatures, and testing analogies (an important part of theoretical discovery, per Abbott, 2004). The risk is too much and too inadequate theorizing at reduced cost. For discovery, LLMs have already been used to cluster documents by semantic similarity, summarize large corpora, and enable corpus-specific conversation. Both word-based clusters and LLM-based representations should be treated as uncertain indications of meaning, requiring subsequent grounding through intensive qualitative analysis. The chapter closes with the caution that LLMs should assist rather than replace human interpretive effort.
