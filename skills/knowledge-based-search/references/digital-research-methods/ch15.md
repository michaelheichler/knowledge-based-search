## Overview

Tommaso Venturini argues that the sociological distinction between micro-interactions and macro-structures is inaccurate as an ontological claim, outdated as an epistemological one, and misleading as a political orientation. The chapter traces the divide to its roots in the historical separation between the public sphere (governed by the state) and the private sphere (governed by the family). It then shows how "computational interactionism" (social situations in which human interaction is mediated, monitored, or otherwise shaped by advanced digital computation) has made that boundary unworkable. The argument is built around a well-known anecdote and a sequence of critical moves against both surveillance-capitalism alarmism and naive data optimism.

## The Supermarket-Pregnancy Anecdote

The chapter's central illustration is a story first reported by Charles Duhigg in the New York Times (2012). A retailer, by analyzing purchase patterns (sudden increases in unscented lotions and mineral supplements), predicted pregnancy in a customer and mailed her maternity coupons. Her father complained to the store manager, but days later apologized: his daughter was indeed pregnant, and the supermarket had known before he did.

Venturini notes that the story is probably false. No solid evidence has surfaced to confirm that the retailer was using genuine micro-targeting rather than simply sending coupons to all women of childbearing age. The rate of false positives and false negatives is never reported, so a single correct guess says nothing about predictive accuracy. Colin Fraser (2020) examined the claim and found it did not hold up. Yet the story became a go-to example for data-science enthusiasts and surveillance-capitalism critics alike, precisely because it dramatizes the collapse of the wall between intimate family life and mass marketing.

This collapse (the algorithm acting on private knowledge the father lacked) is the "algorithmic gaffe" trope. Venturini connects it to a broader genre of stories (AI assistants spoiling Christmas, ride-sharing apps raising prices for people fleeing a terrorist attack, search engines reproducing racial stereotypes) in which the error comes from applying a macro-level computational rule without sensitivity to the micro-situation.

Axel Meunier's project Algoglitch set out to collect such stories and found them circulating widely, not only among critics but inside computer-science conference proceedings and popular AI books, suggesting the gaffe narrative serves multiple rhetorical purposes.

## The Micro/Macro Divide and Its Origins

The chapter places the sociological micro/macro split in the words of two of its principal architects. Emile Durkheim held that "one cannot deduce society from the individual, the whole from the part, the complex from the simple. Society is a reality sui generis." Erving Goffman replied, symmetrically, that "social situations... constitute a reality sui generis... and therefore need and warrant analysis in their own right." Each positioned his object of study as autonomous and irreducible to the other.

Venturini acknowledges the standard Actor-Network Theory (ANT) account of why the divide arose: the scarcity of records forced researchers to choose between large-but-coarse statistical aggregation or precise-but-narrow ethnography. Bruno Latour and Michel Callon (1981), and Richard Rogers in digital methods work (2013, 2019), suggested that digital data could overcome the quali/quanti split by providing both quantity and quality simultaneously. Venturini says he has made that argument elsewhere and does not repeat it here. Instead he offers a different, complementary origin story.

## The Political Root: Public and Private Spheres

The micro/macro divide, Venturini argues, is better understood as a scholarly metaphor for the political split between the public and the private sphere that accompanied the rise of the modern nation-state and the nuclear family. Statistics, as its name indicates, was developed for the state, not for academia (Alain Desrosieres, 1998). Geography was an instrument of conquest before it became a discipline (David Turnbull, 2000). Economics built markets before it studied them (Callon, Millo, and Muniesa, 2007). Polling was created for political and commercial marketing before scientific investigation (Thomas Osborne and Nikolas Rose, 1999). Quantitative methods are therefore performative as well as descriptive: they do not merely reflect the social world but help enact and enforce simplified versions of it that states and powerful actors can act upon (Theodore Porter, 1995; Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, 1999).

Qualitative methods developed in opposition to that administrative project. Their posture (attending to intimacy, singularity, and the viewpoint of the researched) reflects an effort to resist the generalizing and controlling drive of state-sponsored quantification. Denzin and Lincoln's description of qualitative research emphasizes "the intimate relationship between the researcher and what is studied," and the adjective "intimate" is, Venturini says, crucial: it marks the alignment of qualitative methods with the private sphere.

The public/private distinction is itself contested and legally constructed (Morton Horwitz, 1982; Michelle Perrot, 1990; Susan Gal, 2002). But its influence on sociological imagination is real. Seen in this light, the micro/macro divide and the quali/quanti divide are not merely methodological inconveniences but traces of a deliberate political opposition: the state's project of governing large populations versus the effort to maintain forms of sociality that escape that governance.

## Computational Interactionism

Advanced social computation dissolves this division. Where classical statistical marketing operated at the level of aggregated populations (staying, broadly, within the public sphere), big data and AI techniques promise to follow individuals into their private situations. The "3Vs mantra" (volume, variety, and velocity, cited from Dominique Boullier, 2015) captures the ambition: collect far more granular data and analyze it individually rather than by sampling and extrapolating.

Venturini names this new formation "computational interactionism": social situations in which the interaction between people is mediated or monitored by advanced digital computation. Matching algorithms on dating apps, opponent-matching in video games, and career recommendation systems are examples. In each, computational methods originally designed for governing aggregates are applied inside private, face-to-face-scale exchanges.

Karin Knorr Cetina (2009), speaking to the Society for the Study of Symbolic Interaction, called the result "synthetic situations" (situations in which participants are constantly connected to remote contexts through digital technologies) and argued that this forces sociology to abandon the assumption of a deep divide between the interaction order and macrosocial structure. Venturini distinguishes synthetic situations (which offer connection to remote contexts) from "algorithmic situations" (which impose it), drawing on Mathews, Cardon, and Balague (2022): in algorithmic situations, data extracted from an interaction is automatically compared with data from a multitude of similar situations, and insights are fed back in real time.

## Three Problems with the Surveillance-Capitalism Response

Critics of computational interactionism, including Shoshana Zuboff whose 2019 book coined "surveillance capitalism," call for protecting the private sphere as a sanctuary from algorithmic intrusion. Venturini identifies three problems with this position.

First, the idea of an interaction shielded from external influence was always false. Latour's metaphor is cited: interactions "do not resemble a picnic where all the food is gathered on the spot by the participants, but rather a reception given by some unknown sponsors who have staged everything down to the last detail" (Latour, 2005, p. 166). Material infrastructure (the layout of prisons, Foucault, 1975; mental institutions, Goffman, 1961; classrooms, hospitals, cities) has always imported power relations into local situations. Communication technologies (writing, printing, telephone, radio, television) have always allowed distant actors to intervene in situations without being physically present, contrary to Goffman's definition of a situation as one in which actors are "directly accessible to the naked senses" of each other.

Second, the critique buys too readily into the rhetoric of algorithmic effectiveness. The supermarket story proves one correct prediction, not the accuracy of the system as a whole. Venturini and Richard Rogers (2019) examined the Cambridge Analytica affair and found the data, while massive and immorally acquired through a flaw in Facebook's API, were also patchy, skewed, and hard to use. The secondary data harvested from friends of consenting users did not include the psychological variables that supposedly gave Cambridge Analytica its originality. Respondents were recruited through microwork platforms (including Amazon Mechanical Turk) associated with overseas click farms, raising reliability questions. Aleksandr Kogan, who developed the data-collection protocol, described the data as "noisy" and "worthless" for political advertising. Investigations suggested the firm's actual influence relied more on classic focus groups and disinformation techniques than on computational micro-targeting.

Third, the call to protect the private sphere idealizes it. Feminist scholarship (Elizabeth Schneider, 1991; Ruth Gavison, 1992) has shown that the private sphere (particularly the patriarchal family) is not free of asymmetries. Private subjugation can be as violent as public subjugation. The supermarket-pregnancy story illustrates this double edge: the father's authority is undercut, but the story ends with the apology going to the supermarket manager, not to the daughter, whose opinion no one asks.

## The Consequential Opposition

The real political divide is not between micro and macro, but between technologies that entrench existing power asymmetries and technologies that help challenge them. This opposition cuts across all the classic sociological axes (micro/macro, qualitative/quantitative, local/global, private/public).

Venturini points to fields such as data journalism (Bounegru and Gray, 2021), data activism (Stefania Milan and Lonneke van der Velden, 2016), citizen data (Jennifer Gabrys et al., 2016), data feminism (Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren Klein, 2020), and post-colonial data work (Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejias, 2019) as examples of advanced computational technologies used for empowerment rather than control. Many of these projects deliberately blur micro and macro: they help marginalized individuals pool personal records into shared datasets, turning data technologies into tools for collective organization, and connecting separated private spheres so that members of subjugated groups can weigh in on public debate.

## The Durkheimian Test

Susan Leigh Star (1989) proposed replacing the Turing test with a "Durkheimian test": the intelligence of a computational technology should be measured by its direct usefulness to the work of the community, its ability to change and adapt, and its capacity to encompass multiple points of view while increasing communication across viewpoints (Star, 1989, p. 41). Venturini adopts this as the standard computational interactionism should meet if it is to contribute productively to collective existence.

## Main Claims

1. The micro/macro divide has no sound ontological basis. It is better understood as a scholarly trace of the historical public/private separation of modernity.

2. The quali/quanti methodological split maps onto that same political opposition: quantitative methods were developed as instruments of state administration, qualitative methods developed in resistance to that project.

3. Computational interactionism does not create something entirely new, but it accelerates the dissolution of the public/private boundary by applying population-level analytical tools to intimate, individual situations.

4. The claim that advanced computation is effective at this intervention is largely a myth. The supermarket story is probably fabricated. Cambridge Analytica's data were unreliable and its political effectiveness unproven.

5. Protecting the private sphere as a sanctuary from computation is misguided because (a) interactions were never free of external technological mediation, (b) algorithmic control is not as effective as its proponents or critics claim, and (c) the private sphere was never a haven from power asymmetries.

6. The relevant question is not whether computation bridges micro and macro but whether specific computational technologies entrench or unsettle existing power asymmetries. Progressive data practices show that the latter is possible, even if marginal in current data science.
