## Overview

Chapter 4, written by Samantha Breslin and Kristoffer Albris, introduces computational anthropology as the use of techniques and methods from computer science, data science, and digital methods alongside traditional anthropological and ethnographic methods. The authors situate the field at the intersection of the social sciences and the humanities, and they argue for a pragmatic, open approach that expands rather than replaces the ethnographic tradition.

## Two Distinct Orientations

The chapter draws a central distinction between two versions of computational anthropology.

The first version integrates computational tools (network visualizations, semi-automated content analysis, machine learning) with traditional ethnographic methods such as participant observation, interviews, and archival research. This approach treats ethnography as primary and sees computational methods as adding layers of data and analysis to it.

The second version applies data science and digital methods to large-scale automated analysis of phenomena of anthropological interest (social networks, myths, gift exchange, public opinion) without any ethnographic component. The subject matter and use of anthropological theory, not the methods, define the work as anthropological.

Breslin and Albris focus primarily on the first version but discuss both through case studies.

## Historical and Disciplinary Roots

The authors trace antecedents including quantitative anthropology (Chibnik, 1985), social network analysis (Mitchell, 1974), graph theory in anthropology (Hage, 1979), the search for cultural algorithms (Hymes, 1965), structural anthropology, and systems science. They note that "computation" before digital machines was often treated as clerical work performed by people, frequently women (Hayles, 2005, Light, 1999).

Digital humanities provides a relevant model: it has worked with computational methods longer and has developed substantial debates about their place in the humanities. Anthropologists need not reinvent the wheel but can learn from this broader community.

The chapter emphasizes the particular importance of digital anthropology as a bridge. Tom Boellstorff's (2008) work on virtual worlds established that digital environments are legitimate field sites. Digital ethnography (Pink et al., 2016) extended this to the fluid movement between online and offline worlds. Yarimar Bonilla and Jonathan Rosa's (2015) analysis of #Ferguson illustrates how a hashtag can function as a field site and how computational methods can address questions that traditional ethnography cannot handle at scale (eight million tweets would fill roughly 667 standard A4 pages of text).

## The Abductive Logic of Integration

Stephen Paff (2022) is cited for characterizing both data science and ethnography as sharing an abductive approach: starting from prior thinking and refining it iteratively as research proceeds. For Breslin and Albris, this means ethnographic fieldwork may generate the focus or theoretical meaning for computational methods, and computational findings may redirect further ethnographic inquiry. The relationship is iterative and oscillatory, not linear.

The Krieg et al. (2017) study "Anthropology with Algorithms" illustrates this. Researchers used long-term ethnographic knowledge of drug use cultures to guide computational analysis of the Erowid platform and its Facebook group. Semantic networks of word co-occurrences in trip reports revealed that dosing GHB or GBL with a bottle cap was represented as far more common than the ethnographers had assumed, leading to a new research project on dosing practices. The construction of the word co-occurrence network required tweaking stopword lists and removing less-connected nodes, which the authors acknowledged as "a highly subjective process."

## Network Analysis

Networks can be built from many data types. For social media, Twitter data (follows, mentions, retweets) allows the mapping of actor relations at scales ethnographic methods cannot reach. Identifying which accounts retweet one another reveals which actors are central and which are peripheral.

Albris's COVID-19 case illustrates the integrated approach. The project examined how green NGOs in Denmark, Norway, and Sweden repositioned their climate agendas during the pandemic (Enggaard et al., 2023, Blok et al., 2022). The researchers began with interviews with NGO representatives, observations at socially distanced public demonstrations, and online ethnographic investigation of posts across Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram. These early stages defined which actor set to include: NGOs that participate in parliamentary hearings in each country. Large-scale scraping then collected posts and interaction data (likes, mentions, retweets, timestamps). A word embedding model analyzed relationships among terms related to climate, sustainability, and COVID-19. Principal component analyses (PCA) and retweet network analyses mapped actor positions and word positions in the public debate space. The network findings led back to re-interviews and additional online ethnography, and specific deep case studies followed, including a micro-history of the idea "Groen Genstart" (Green Restart) (Isfeldt et al., 2022).

## Content Analysis

Computational content analysis covers a spectrum from simple word clouds (produced in Python or through tools such as Wordle) and word frequency or word distribution graphs, to semi-automated text classifiers trained to categorize millions of tweets, to topic models that surface patterns across large text corpora. Natural language processing (NLP) packages in Python or R are the main technical vehicle.

The Breslin et al. (2020, 2022) case demonstrates computational anthropology as analysis of data without ethnographic fieldwork. The dataset consisted of Danish-language tweets from before, during, and after the announcement of the initial COVID-19 lockdown in Denmark on March 11, 2020. Dictionary-based sentiment analysis classifying tweets as "positive" or "negative" raised methodological and epistemological problems around context and meaning. This critique led the team to develop what they call "affect analysis," mapping tweet words onto eight "basic emotions" drawn from Ekman (1992) and Plutchik (1994). Situated experts (members of the project team) reviewed the emotion dictionaries for appropriateness in the Danish language.

A key finding was that words relating to trust (Danish: tillid) rose to being used twice as often around the lockdown announcement compared to before it. A further analysis classified a subset of trust-related tweets by whether they represented trust or mistrust and who or what the trust was directed at, following standard content analysis procedures. The authors interpreted the findings through anthropological theories of trust (Carey, 2017, Lewis and Weigert, 1985) and framed COVID-19 discussions on Danish Twitter as an "affective public" (Papacharissi, 2016) in which performances of trust operated as a form of biopolitical nationalism (de Kloet et al., 2020), shaped by the contagious networked affordances of Twitter as a platform.

## Epistemological Challenges

The authors identify three epistemological challenges.

The first is the "thinness" of data. Big data and digital trace data are frequently contrasted with the depth of ethnographic knowledge. The authors acknowledge this concern but argue that data cannot be set aside because it is entangled with changing norms, personhood, and social relations (Douglas-Jones et al., 2021).

The second is the question of who defines these methods and who gets to use them. Drawing on Bowker's (1993) analysis of cybernetics, the authors note that computational methods are sometimes presented as an inevitable future that everyone must adopt. They connect this to disciplinary power and to the structural ties between computational tools, major tech companies, and US military research and development, all of which are frequent targets of critique within critical humanities and social sciences.

The third challenge is reflexivity and the situatedness of data. Ethnographic validity traditionally rests on the ability to reconstruct the researcher's path through fieldwork. Computational methods complicate this because researchers often cannot examine the internal logic of an API or a neural network. The scope of large datasets can also mask what is missing or whose perspectives are absent. Large language models (GPT, Llama, CoPilot, Claude are named) raise these problems sharply: questions of data confidentiality, GDPR compliance, the uncited labor and data used in training, and the fact that these systems tend to function more as models than as transparent methods for modeling. The authors raise but do not answer the philosophical question of whether it matters whether a human or a machine produces opaque insights.

## Moral and Ethical Challenges

The authors distinguish ethical questions (what is permissible, governed by research protocols) from moral ones (what anthropologists feel they should or should not do). Drawing on Mogensen et al. (2021), they argue that moral considerations implicitly shape anthropological practice in ways that receive less explicit attention than ethics.

The moral objection to computational anthropology draws on concerns about surveillance capitalism (Zuboff, 2019), techno-solutionism, and reductionism. Related concerns include exploitative digital labor practices (Irani, 2015 on Amazon Mechanical Turk) and content moderation labor (Roberts, 2019), as well as the environmental costs of computation and data storage (Monserrate, 2022). Existing anthropological methods also carry a morally problematic colonial history.

The authors counter these concerns by invoking Donna Haraway's cyborg manifesto: "pleasure in the confusion of boundaries and responsibility in their construction" (Haraway, 1991, p. 150). They argue that access to computational skills has historically been unequal along gender and racial lines (boyd and Crawford, 2012, Hicks, 2017, McPherson, 2012), and that broadening the practitioners of these methods has moral value. Anthropologists, given their training in critiquing power relations, may be better positioned than many other disciplines to use computational methods cautiously and critically. The authors frame computational anthropology as potentially operating as a "counter-science" that continually interrogates and unmakes the terms of its own production (Foucault, 2002, p. 414).

## Key Terms and Concepts

- Thick description: Clifford Geertz's (1973) term for saturated, contextualized description of social or cultural phenomena, drawn originally from Gilbert Ryle.
- Broad description: a complementary approach that uses computational methods to provide coverage and context alongside thick ethnographic description (Astrupgaard et al., 2022).
- Netnography: Robert Kozinets's (2019) approach to qualitative research on social media environments.
- Word embedding model: a computational technique that captures relationships among words beyond simple frequency.
- Principal component analysis (PCA): a statistical technique used here to map positions of actors and concepts in a multidimensional debate space.
- Affect analysis: the authors' term for mapping text onto a set of eight basic emotions as an alternative to binary sentiment analysis.
- Affective public: Papacharissi's (2016) concept describing networked publics constituted through emotional performances.
- Abductive approach: a research logic of iterative refinement, treating findings as grounds for revising prior assumptions rather than confirming them.
