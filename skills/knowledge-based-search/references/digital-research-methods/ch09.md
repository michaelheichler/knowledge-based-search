## Overview

Chapter 9, written by Sharon Meraz, maps the computational turn in media and communication research. It describes how digital technologies reshaped the field's data landscape, surveys the methods scholars now use, identifies advantages and challenges those methods bring, and illustrates a hybrid approach with a case study of the #blacklivesmatter hashtag on Twitter across 2016.

## The Changed Media Landscape

Digital technologies have turned daily life into a stream of continuous data, some intentional and some not. Meraz locates the first inflection point around 1999 when blogging applications such as WordPress and Blogger appeared, and a second inflection after 2005 when Facebook and Twitter achieved mass adoption. These platforms shifted the media marketplace from a top-down, mass-mediated structure to a bottom-up, horizontally connected one. The media ecology is now described as a hybrid assemblage of old and new media (Chadwick, 2017), and the growth of social platforms has introduced what Willig (2021) calls platformization, in which platforms exert disproportionate influence over how content flows and is distributed.

The user activity generated in this environment is called digital trace data: data of user activity captured and mediated by digital services. The term covers intentionally collected data and what scholars call "found," "naturally occurring," or "massive, passive" data scraped from platforms and analyzed for existing patterns. Scholars also use the phrase "digital exhaust" (Watts, 2013) for the breadcrumbs recorded by always-on, ambient technologies.

Newsrooms changed in parallel. Newspaper circulation declined sharply in the 2000s, mass media embraced online distribution, and journalism adopted open-source content development including application programming interfaces (APIs). Terms coined to describe these changes include networked journalism, digital journalism, data journalism, automated journalism, algorithmic journalism, and robotic journalism. By 2023, generative AI tools such as ChatGPT and DALL-E were automating text and image creation, further disrupting both journalism and public information environments.

## The Computational Turn

Media and communication scholars had always used computational tools, including SPSS, SAS, and regression analysis, but the contemporary computational turn introduced a different range: automated text analysis, computerized content analysis, network analysis and visualization, data mining, and natural language processing (NLP). It also pushed scholars to use programming languages, primarily Python and R, to manipulate textual and network data.

Scholars work with what Heiberger and Reibling (2016) call "medium data," datasets smaller than the terabytes common in computer science but too large for purely manual analysis. The choice to use computational methods is research-design-driven. Their main attractions are locating patterns, generating inferences, and making predictions from datasets that are too large for manual coding.

Access to data through APIs was central to this work. Scripts in Python or R would tap a platform's API, retrieve data, and parse it using NLP. A tweet, for example, could be parsed for userID, username, tweet text, timestamp, geolocation, embedded hashtags, URLs, retweets, and @ markers. Before 2023 many platforms permitted such access. Access has since contracted sharply: Facebook closed its API to researchers in 2018, Twitter locked free API access in 2023 after Elon Musk acquired the platform and moved to a costly paid model, and Reddit restricted third-party API access in 2023.

### Methods Used in the Field

Content analysis is the dominant method in media and communication research. Riffe, Lacy, and Fico (2005, p. 25) define it as "the systematic and replicable examination of symbols of communication, which have been assigned numeric values according to valid measurement rules, and the analysis of relationships involving those values using statistical methods." Computational social science has reinvented this toolkit. Scholars now use unsupervised topic modeling (for example, Latent Dirichlet Allocation, or LDA) alongside semi-supervised or supervised text classification, the latter involving compiled dictionaries or training sets to automate coding. Sentiment analysis, which detects affect or emotion, is a related subfield, though it has not been widely adopted in media and communication because of the nuances in political text. The program Diction is cited as an early tool for coding emotion in media and political text.

Alongside text analysis, scholars have adopted social network analysis and semantic network analysis for visualizing actors and content on platforms. Out-of-the-box tools mentioned are UCINET, Netdraw, and Gephi. Network analysis packages in R (tidygraph, tidyverse) and Python (networkX) are available for those with coding skills. Network analysis has been applied to detect influential actors or gatekeepers, model influence, and study advocacy networks, partisan networks, and hate speech networks.

Survey research has also been enhanced: scholars have scraped Facebook groups and launched surveys within them, recruited participants via Mechanical Turk, and used Twitter for survey administration. Experimental design has been transformed too. Levy (2021) used combined Facebook, survey, and browser data to study exposure to cross-cutting information. A much-discussed earlier example is the emotional contagion study (Kramer, Guillory, and Hancock, 2014) in which Facebook and Cornell University researchers delivered positive and negative messages to over 61 million users in the 2010 US congressional elections without their informed consent, exposing ethical dangers in platform-based experiments.

Cloud-based storage and open science practices using platforms such as GitHub and Dataverse now allow scholars to share datasets, code, and documentation for replication.

## Advantages of Computational Methods

Computational methods increase scale and depth of analysis (Watts, 2013), produce novel insights into information transmission patterns and media effects, and enable serendipitous discoveries that would be invisible in smaller datasets (Lazer and Radford, 2017). They are faster and cheaper to revise than human-coded methods: adding computational power, storage, and code scales them exponentially at low cost. They also make research more replicable, transparent, iterative, and systematic (Zamith and Lewis, 2015). Algorithms are suited to repetitive tasks such as locating, obtaining, and organizing data.

The chapter stresses that few scholars view computational methods as replacements for traditional ones. They are supplements, best suited to parsing text, categorizing variables, and handling large datasets. They underperform where manual examination and situated knowledge matter, which are areas important to media and communication research. The value, as Lin (2015) puts it, lies in providing "better mousetraps" for data mining and bottom-up analysis.

## Challenges and Critiques

### Big Data Hubris

boyd and Crawford (2014) warn that computational methods are often packaged as superior windows into "truth, objectivity, and accuracy," though bigger data is not always better. Couldry and Mejias (2019) describe continuous data capture by private corporations as data colonialism, the commercial exploitation of user data for profit. Zuboff (2015) frames it as surveillance capitalism.

### Access and Data Quality

API closures have produced differential access among scholars, dividing them into "data rich" (those who can still scrape and code) and "data poor." Researchers who can no longer access APIs sometimes partner with platforms to obtain proprietary data, but it is unclear what such datasets include or exclude, how much diversity they represent, and how these alliances bias findings. Freelon (2014) had long cautioned scholars to prepare for a post-API age.

### Who Is Represented

Research on social media has overrepresented Twitter data, treating it as a "model organism" for social media participation (Tufekci, 2014). Hargittai (2020) notes that usage on social platforms is nonrandom, tilting toward users with higher digital skills and higher socioeconomic status, which excludes poorer and less-educated groups. High-skill users who dominate social network sites are often existing opinion leaders or gatekeepers (Meraz and Papacharissi, 2013) with extreme opinions. Nonhuman bots further taint the authenticity of digitally mediated communication. Platform algorithms and architecture shape what data exists in the first place.

### Validity and Measurement

Digital trace data emits diverse social signals whose meaning is contested. A meta-analysis by Freelon (2014) found that media and communication scholars interpreted Twitter signals such as retweets and hashtags differently, with broad agreement only that they indicated "influence, trust, and credibility." Jungherr (2015) distinguishes the "mirror hypothesis," the mistaken view that digital data reflects political reality, from the "mediation hypothesis," the more accurate view that digital data is a socio-technical product of platform affordances. Political digital data is a signal of political attention, not political support (Jungherr et al., 2017). Large volumes of data also produce high statistical power that can generate spurious correlations exaggerating the impact of small effects.

### Privacy

Users of digital platforms sign terms of service agreements in legal language they rarely read, and most platforms default to public with opt-in privacy. Personalized data is often collected without explicit informed consent. Scholars are expected to de-identify scraped data, though adherence to this norm is uncertain. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), framed in 2018, protects racial and ethnic origin, religious membership, sexuality, and health information, and gives users rights to access, erase, and restrict profiling of their data. As of the chapter's writing in 2024, no comparable protections exist in the United States.

## Case Study: #BlackLivesMatter in 2016

The chapter presents a mixed-methodology study of the #blacklivesmatter hashtag across 2016 as a practical illustration of the hybrid approach.

### Background

The #blacklivesmatter hashtag was created in 2013 by Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors, and Opal Tometi following the killing of Trayvon Martin by George Zimmerman in Florida. By 2016 it was the leading social issue hashtag on Twitter (Anderson and Hitlin, 2016), and the broader movement grew to 40 offline chapters. In 2016 the US presidential election, the rise of overt White nationalism, the proliferation of hate speech and misinformation, and Russian seeding of misinformation campaigns in the Black Lives Matter network to weaken Black solidarity all formed the study's political context.

### Theoretical Framework

The study draws on networked framing, a revision of Entman's (1993) framing theory developed by Meraz and Papacharissi (2013) to account for the hybrid media environment. Where classical framing theory gave mass media primary power to define issues for audiences, networked framing is "a process through which particular problem definitions, causal interpretations, moral evaluations, and/or treatment recommendations attain prominence through crowdsourcing practices" (Meraz and Papacharissi, 2013, p. 159). Hashtag activism (Rambukkana, 2014) is the specific practice studied: hashtags serve as design affordances that cue audiences to participate in issue elevation and framing in real time, and they are open to both supporting and dissenting publics, creating dynamic spaces for framing and counterframing.

### Data Collection and Processing

A paid third-party service, Tweet Archivist, was used to collect tweets. It accessed Twitter's real-time search function via the API, sampling up to 1,500 tweets per hour from as far back as seven days prior to the search. From February to November 2016, a running search query on #blacklivesmatter produced approximately 3 million tweets across 10 months. (Tweet Archivist closed its collection service in May 2023 after Twitter shut its API, so this method is no longer possible.)

Tweet batches of 50,000 (the Excel rate limit) were converted to CSV format. Python scripts using NLP parsed each tweet for: userID, username, tweet text, timestamp, geolocation (when present), embedded hashtags, URLs, user mentions (@), and follower counts. The study focused on hashtags embedded within tweets as indicators of issue and issue frame prominence.

### Analysis: Research Question 1

Python scripts aggregated unique hashtags by frequency across the 3 million tweet sample. The top 25 to 30 hashtags were extracted. Results showed strong allegiance from #blacklivesmatter to other Black digital spaces (#blm and #blacktwitter), names of specific police brutality victims (#altonsterling, #terrencecrutcher, #philandocastille, #charleskinsley, #korryngaines), geographic sites of incidents and protests (#batonrouge, #milwaukee), progressive political candidates (#feelthebern at 31,217, #imwithher at 17,034, #hillaryclinton at 9,289, #berniesanders, #bernie2016), and Trump-aligned counterpublics (#maga at 9,653, #trump at 12,218). Delegitimizing hashtags were also prominent: #alllivesmatter at 39,000 (the second-highest hashtag after #blm at 54,705) and #bluelivesmatter at 12,288.

### Analysis: Research Question 2

To examine co-occurring hashtag networks, low-frequency hashtags (occurring less than once across the period) were dropped, leaving 250 unique hashtags. Python scripts automated the construction of network matrices of hashtag co-occurrences within tweets. These matrices were loaded into NodeXL (a network analysis plugin for Excel), and the Clauset-Newman-Moore modularity algorithm clustered co-occurring hashtags into thematic communities. Clusters were then manually examined for issue similarity and verified through qualitative analysis of random subsets of tweets within each cluster.

Five broad issue-action frames emerged from the #blacklivesmatter networked public in 2016:

1. Call attention to justice sought for Black lives lost in specific locations (hashtags such as #justiceforaltonsterling, #sayhername, #nojusticenopeace, #itstoomuch).
2. Build networked bridges to other Black, brown, and non-Black ally communities (#muslimlivesmatter, #nodapl, #whitepeopleforblacklives, #jews4blacklives, #palestinians4blacklives).
3. Develop the movement as a global one (#blmtoronto, #blacklivesmatteruk, #blmfrance, #prayfornigeria).
4. Highlight current events significant to Black lives and Black history (#colinkaepernick, #veteransforkaepernick, #staywokebt).
5. Debate which Democratic presidential candidate (Bernie Sanders or Hillary Clinton) was better for Black lives in the 2016 primary and nomination season.

Counterpublic activity injected dissonance: #alllivesmatter countered with the narrative that White lives also matter, #bluelivesmatter elevated police officer safety over Black lives, and Trump-supporting hashtags brought conservative political content into a largely progressive space.

### Conclusions of the Case Study

The hybrid methodology combined computational pattern detection with manual qualitative verification. Computational methods provided high-level detection of prominent hashtags and clustering of thematic networks. Manual analysis then determined issue frames from those clusters and confirmed them through random tweet sampling. The chapter argues that this combination is stronger than either approach alone: computational methods handle scale and speed, and qualitative methods provide contextual meaning that algorithms cannot supply.

## Key Terms and Concepts

Digital trace data: data of user activity captured and mediated by digital platforms, shaped by the platform's affordances.

"Digital exhaust" (Watts, 2013): the data residue of online behavior.

Produsage (Bruns, 2008): the blending of content production and use by networked publics.

Platformization (Willig, 2021): the disproportionate influence of social media platforms on content flow.

Medium data (Heiberger and Reibling, 2016): datasets larger than manageable manually but smaller than terabyte-scale computer science datasets.

LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation): an unsupervised topic modeling method.

Sentiment analysis: automated detection of affect or emotion in text.

Networked framing (Meraz and Papacharissi, 2013): framing as a distributed crowdsourcing process rather than a mass-media-driven one.

Hashtag activism (Rambukkana, 2014): using hashtags as tools for real-time issue elevation and framing.

Mirror hypothesis vs. mediation hypothesis (Jungherr, 2015): the mistaken view that digital data reflects reality vs. the accurate view that it reflects platform affordances.

Data colonialism (Couldry and Mejias, 2019): private corporate exploitation of user data for profit.

Surveillance capitalism (Zuboff, 2015): invasion of user privacy by private companies capturing behavioral data.

NodeXL: a network analysis plugin for Excel, using the Clauset-Newman-Moore algorithm for community detection.

GDPR: EU General Data Protection Regulation (2018), requiring consent and setting retention rules for personal data.
