## Overview

Chapter 16, written by historian Johan Heinsen, examines how the digitisation of archival collections changes the conditions under which researchers encounter the past. The chapter's central claim is that archival data should never be assumed to be smooth or neutral, even when digitisation gives it the appearance of comprehensiveness. Heinsen argues that each layer of transformation (paper to image to computer-readable text to processed data) introduces new biases and new gatekeepers, replacing old constraints without eliminating gatekeeping.

## The Archive-as-Data Paradigm

Archival theorist Devon Mordell (2019) identified a shift she called the "archives-as-data paradigm," in which archival contents are treated as objects of computation. This shift is driven partly by born-digital records and partly by the retro-digitisation of paper holdings. Heinsen takes retro-digitised archives as his primary example and uses them to trace the tensions the digital introduces.

Tim Hitchcock's 2013 essay described the situation as halfway through a revolution. Since the late 1990s, trillions of words had been digitised and made keyword-searchable. That revolution was real, but it came with structural distortions that Heinsen catalogues in detail.

## The Technical Gatekeeping of Digitisation

Optical character recognition (OCR) performs well on modern print in Latin characters but struggles with fraktur and older print types, deteriorated paper, and handwriting. The bias toward certain collections was set by technological constraints rather than scholarly priorities. Early mass digitisation projects, including Early English Books Online, Eighteenth Century Collections Online, and Google Books, were predominantly Anglophone, making the lopsidedness concrete.

Digitisation is expensive and therefore tied to commercial interests, which can create paywalls or monetise user engagement indirectly. Metadata quality has often been poor, and the searchability promoted as a key benefit frequently rests on OCR output that does not match what users assume they are searching.

Heinsen illustrates this with Denmark's National Library newspaper archive. The interface advertises access to 35,464,209 pages. In practice, roughly four-fifths of the material requires an institutional licence. The remaining pages, available through an API, were digitised from microfilm rather than high-quality scans, producing character-level accuracy often below 50 percent. Accuracy is uneven across the collection. More recent newspapers are recognised more reliably than older ones, silently skewing search results toward the recent past. The user interface displays readable scans alongside search results, masking the poor state of the underlying text. Heinsen calls this a "sleight of hand" that is common across digital collections.

## Machine Learning and Transkribus

By the late 2010s, machine learning began to change text digitisation. Transkribus, developed at the University of Innsbruck under EU funding, uses deep learning to recognise both printed and handwritten text. Its performance on printed material exceeds traditional OCR, and for handwritten text the advance is large, since traditional OCR could not read handwriting at all.

In 2019, when grant funding ended, the project became a commercially run cooperative. In 2020 it was monetised through a credit-then-subscription system requiring users to upload documents to Transkribus servers. Training custom models requires large quantities of accurately transcribed text and typically depends on institutional funding. Models handle one language and script at a time, so there are more models for larger Western languages and for better-funded research contexts. Large language models (LLMs) can also read historical documents from images, but show the same skew toward recent and Western writing and offer a less direct path to correction through custom training.

Transkribus is entering European history curricula and users are building and sharing models. Heinsen notes that this activity confirms both the appeal of the technology and the constraints imposed on it. The platform cannot export trained models (only training data), and the subscription costs impose what Heinsen describes as a "vampire drain" on computational historical research.

## Text-to-Data: The Second Layer of Bias

Beyond image-to-text conversion, turning text into usable data introduces another layer of constraint. Computational text analysis typically requires lemmatisation, named entity recognition, and similar procedures, all of which depend on pre-existing language models trained on large text corpora. Such models exist for modern language versions but rarely for historical variants of the same languages. Historical documents also show high spelling variation. Even LLMs struggle as historical language diverges from contemporary usage. The practical consequence is that scholars working with quantitative text analysis face technological incentives to study the recent past. Creating historically specific language models is possible but requires substantial institutional backing.

## The Reading Room and What It Offered

To frame what is being lost or changed, Heinsen describes the traditional archive. The physical archive held singular paper trails, ordered by the principle of provenance. Each archival fond reflects the institution that created it, and the series within it mirror that institution's operations. Finding aids and registers offer only glancing descriptions, so researchers spend days or weeks opening boxes without knowing in advance what they contain. The experience involves double path-dependency. On one side, dependence on the historical process that created the records. On the other, dependence on finding aids that direct researchers toward what others found before them.

Historians have linked authenticity to this slow encounter. The traditions of historical source criticism share a desire to sift the archive and to understand what is found in context. Carolyn Steedman's portrait of archive travel, with cheap hotel beds and dust allergies, stands for a practice of interpretation built around close engagement with difficult material.

Historians working in this mode are not looking for universal causal laws but for the particular. The archive presents abundance and frustration together. Carlo Ginzburg's microhistory required reading large amounts of inquisitorial records to identify exceptional moments when those under interrogation revealed genuine beliefs rather than rehearsed answers. Robert Darnton argued that the archive's value lay in finding documents incompatible with present-day frames of understanding, which can only be reached by wandering through it. Arlette Farge located the allure of the archive in the emotional connection to messy remnants of open-ended past processes. All three positions depend on a slow, hands-on engagement that builds abductive reasoning from texture and pattern.

## The Archive as a Site of Power

Historians have also theorised the archive as a site of power rather than a neutral repository. Antoinette Burton argued that archives do not arrive innocent. They are products of specific political, cultural, and socioeconomic pressures, and those pressures leave traces. Ann Laura Stoler called for reading "along the archival grain" to understand how archives shaped power relations in the past.

Michel-Rolph Trouillot identified four moments at which silencing occurs: the making of sources, the making of archives, the making of narratives, and the making of history as retrospective significance. Each moment shapes what can be said of the past. Terry Cook argued that as much meaning is created when the archivist fills a box (and destroys the other 98 to 99 percent of records) as when the historian interprets what remains. Cook traced the principle of provenance itself to the emergence of Western statecraft, showing that even the archive's ordering reflects specific epistemological and political interests. He also noted that archivists have historically been agents of state-building, making gatekeeping a structural feature of the institution.

The nineteenth-century positivist ideal associated with Kristian Erslev (1852-1930), professor at Copenhagen University, editor of Denmark's leading historical journal, and head of Denmark's National Archives, held that truth is in the sources themselves and that the historian's task is to efface themselves and extract it. Mordell (2019) warned that the archives-as-data paradigm risks reinvigorating this positivism by associating data with objectivity and treating archival material as raw and clean.

## What Changes and What Is at Risk

Heinsen argues that digitisation does not remove the archive's materiality but transforms it. New technologies privilege some contents over others, just as old constraints did, but the biases are different and less visible. The user is no longer pushed into the slow encounter with difficult material that generated historical insight. Lara Putnam (2016) called the new mode of engagement "side-glancing." Godfrey et al. (2021) described how the earlier slower pace (ordering documents, waiting, retrieving, considering) gave time to reflect and form opinions. Now a single afternoon can produce more searchable text than an entire earlier research project, and the researcher is not pushed into the frustrated intimacy with the material that supported abductive reasoning.

New users approaching historical archives from social science or health science backgrounds may find the records attractive partly because they are free of copyright and GDPR constraints. Historical health data, for instance, are being used to model epidemic spread and the longitudinal effects of inequality. These users will focus on cleaning and modelling the data rather than understanding its provenance and entanglement. Rob Kitchin (2014) identified the core assumptions of big data, exhaustiveness and inherent meaning, as assumptions that echo the positivist historiography historians have spent decades arguing against. Those assumptions now appear in data science handbooks used to train humanists in programming.

Heinsen's concern is that contextual knowledge will be de-emphasised and that this will limit discovery. He quotes Godfrey et al.: "retrieval of information devoid of context fails to render data discoverable in the true sense of the term." The exceptional document that Darnton and Ginzburg drew attention to (the one whose form and content reveal past processes as lived and contested) may be invisible to a researcher optimising for aggregate patterns.

As historians become creators of digital archives and curators of models and metadata, they also become gatekeepers to others. The responsibility that once rested with professional archivists is shifting to researchers who may not reckon fully with what that role entails.

## Core Conclusion

The digital archive is not a neutral repository and should not be treated as one. Its materiality has changed, not disappeared. The move from reading room to computational interface brings real gains in scale and accessibility, and new technologies like Transkribus represent genuine advances. But each step of conversion (paper to image, image to text, text to data) introduces bias, commercial constraint, and technological skew toward recent, Western, well-funded material. The insights that came from slow, contextual engagement with archives risk being displaced by extractive, completionist assumptions borrowed from data science. Researchers from any discipline approaching digital archives should interrogate the data rather than assume it is smooth.
