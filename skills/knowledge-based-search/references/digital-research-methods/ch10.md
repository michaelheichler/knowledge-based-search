## Overview

Chapter 10, by Laura K. Nelson, argues that computational methods do not eliminate the subject from knowledge production, nor do they simply enhance intersubjective agreement in a Kantian sense. Instead, they make it possible to operationalize partial perspectives both as epistemology (the researcher's situated standpoint shapes inquiry) and as ontology (social reality itself is constituted by multiple, unequal, partial perspectives). The chapter draws on standpoint theory, particularly Donna Haraway's work, alongside social constructionism, Du Bois's concept of double consciousness, and the distributional hypothesis in linguistics, to build this argument. It closes with a call to center data provenance as the practical implication of this epistemological position.

## The Problem: Bias or Evidence?

Word2Vec, introduced by Google in 2013 (Mikolov et al., 2013), was among the first large-scale word embedding models. It demonstrated that training on large text corpora produces models that capture nuanced linguistic relationships. Researchers soon found that these models also encode social biases present in the training data (Bolukbasi et al., 2016). For AI engineers and ethicists, this was a problem to correct. For social scientists and humanists, it opened a different question: because these models faithfully capture social constructs, can computational methods be used to study society objectively?

Two opposing responses emerged. One camp treats computational models as the realization of pure objectivity, removing the human subject from knowledge production entirely, an aspiration associated with "social physics" and writers like Ball (2006) and Pentland (2015). The other camp, drawing on Kant and Peirce, sees computational methods as a vehicle for intersubjectivity: since we cannot access raw facts, universal knowledge is constructed through agreement among multiple subjective perspectives, and computational models give this process mathematical consistency. Nelson accepts part of this second view but argues it does not go far enough. The deeper opportunity, she claims, is to use computational methods to operationalize Haraway's theory of situated knowledges and the privilege of the partial perspective.

## Self, Society, and Double Consciousness

The chapter grounds its argument in social constructionist theory. Drawing on George Herbert Mead (Mind, Self, and Society), Nelson explains that selfhood is formed through language and communication. Individuals perceive themselves indirectly, through the perspectives of those around them. The result is not one unified self but a mosaic of selves shaped by different social contexts.

W. E. B. Du Bois adds a critical inequality dimension. Because white Americans refuse to grant the same cultural meaning to gestures performed by Black Americans, Black Americans cannot achieve self-consciousness on the same terms as white Americans. They are forced to see themselves through the eyes of others who view them, in Du Bois's words, with "amused contempt and pity." This double consciousness, having to inhabit a self-image formed by a dominant group that holds institutional power, shapes Black selves, communities, and language in profound ways.

To speak is to assume a particular sociocultural position (Fanon, 2008). Many groups, including colonized people (Fanon), the stigmatized (Goffman), those in borderlands (Anzaldua), and the formerly enslaved (Du Bois), must navigate multiple incompatible language communities with different access to power. These multiple selves, shaped by power dynamics and social location, are what computational methods can help map.

## Cultural Learning and the Distributional Hypothesis

Cultural associations, such as the association white Americans make between Black Americans and contempt, are learned rather than inherent. One theory of how this happens is connectionism (Rumelhart, 1980). In this theory, concepts acquire meaning through networks of associations with other concepts. Repeated co-occurrence of words or stimuli builds cognitive schemas, patterned ways of processing information that encode stereotypes and cultural frameworks (for example, femininity with weakness, or Canadians with niceness).

Neural word embeddings such as Word2Vec may provide a cognitively plausible model of how humans learn cultural representations from language (Arseniev-Koehler and Foster, 2022). Both humans navigating cultural environments and word embeddings training on text undergo a parallel process of schema learning. If cultural meanings are encoded in language, and embedding models encode meaning directly from text, then computational models achieve what Gabriel Tarde called the "internal viewpoint." Rather than modeling society from the outside as if from a godlike vantage, they model from the inside, through the subjects within the system.

Because each person occupies a different social position, and because inequality shapes those positions, the shared cultural meanings encoded in language are never universal. The view of the world from the powerful differs from the view of the powerless. This difference is real and objective, not merely a disagreement about facts between observers. It is the real, objective existence of different facts for different people.

## Objectivity Through Partial Perspectives

Kant held that knowledge is necessarily subjective but can achieve universality through intersubjective agreement. Du Bois and Haraway complicate this. That "universality" is itself partial, reflecting the perspectives of those with power to shape shared knowledge.

Haraway (1988), "Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective," proposed a route to objective knowledge that avoids totalizing ideologies. She identifies two axes.

The first axis is partial perspectives as epistemology. A scholar's perspective is shaped by their social and political experiences, which shape the questions they ask, what aspects of the world they notice, and what concepts are intelligible to them. Objective knowledge is not built by pretending the subject is absent, but by acknowledging that all knowledge is necessarily partial, and by quilting together objective but partial perspectives through scientific communities into broader accounts. This is compatible with but distinct from the Kantian view. Haraway deliberately avoids universal truth statements.

The second axis is partial perspectives as ontology. Each person in a social system views that system from their own location, which is shaped by intersecting axes of inequality. To understand social reality requires viewing social systems from the perspectives of each socially determined person.

Nelson argues that computational methods can operationalize both axes.

On the epistemological axis, computational methods codify the researcher's perspective. They force transparency about every decision in the analytical process. Compared to traditional content analysis, where a published codebook cannot fully explain why a specific text unit received a particular code (Biernacki, 2012), computational text analysis documents every decision in measurable and reproducible ways. Even the definition of a "word" (typically a string of characters bounded by white spaces) must be made explicit, with consequences that become visible in languages like Mandarin that do not separate words with white spaces. Every decision, from tokenization to feature construction to clustering, is encoded in rerunnable code, allowing the scholarly community to argue about and revise each step. This operationalizes the epistemology of partial perspectives.

On the ontological axis, a single model trained on large and diverse data encodes multiple, even competing, schemas within the same mathematical space. Vector arithmetic in embedding models makes it possible to isolate and compare these perspectives directly. Nelson gives a concrete example from her own research (Nelson, 2021). She trained a word embedding model on first-person narrations of experiences in the US South during the nineteenth century. By constructing artificial vectors for intersectional identities through vector addition (women added to Black individuals, for instance), she could visualize the same vector space from multiple perspectives. From the composite woman vector, words associated with the domestic sphere were closest. From the composite Black woman vector, economic words were closest. From the composite white woman vector, cultural words such as "refinement," "gentility," and "endowments" were closest. Black women viewed and were viewed by this social world through an economic lens, while white women viewed and were viewed by it through a high-culture lens. This operationalizes partial perspectives as ontology.

This approach also allows researchers to ask precise questions: are the perspectival communities in a dataset shaped by micro-level social groups (as in Du Bois), meso-level organizations and communities (as in Anzaldua), or macro forces such as temporal periods or nation-states (as in Fanon), or some combination?

## Data Provenance

Because the meaning encoded in computational models is entirely shaped by patterns in the training data, data provenance becomes one of the most important principles for using these models in social science and humanities research. Provenance means understanding the origin, lineage, and transformations undergone by data throughout its lifecycle.

For commercial products like ChatGPT, which exhibit predictable cultural biases, provenance helps engineers build guardrails and helps users interpret output appropriately. It is also essential when language models generate synthetic data for downstream tasks. The question is not only whether synthetic data faithfully reproduce the distribution of the underlying data, but whose perspectives that underlying data reflects. Commercial large language models are trained on well-resourced languages (primarily English) and on readily digitized sources such as Wikipedia and news articles, which are written by a small, non-representative group of people.

Closed-source models present a particular challenge because their training data is concealed, making it impossible to know which perspectives are well represented. Even open-source models can have provenance problems, especially as models grow larger and ostensibly more general. Training on massive data reinforces the idea of universal perspectives and aggregates away marginal and marginalized ones. An example of the errors this can cause is documented in Jackson and Foucault Welles (2016).

Nelson proposes two directions. Researchers could use provenance labels to trace linguistic patterns in large models back to their different origins, identifying and amplifying partial perspectives within those models. Alternatively, social scientists and humanists could train smaller, domain-specific models capturing precisely the perspectives relevant to their research questions, as Nelson did with the US South corpus. She suggests the field may find that, given these goals, smaller models are better.

## Conclusion

The chapter rejects the "social physics" aspiration of a subject-less computational science. Rather than removing the subject from knowledge production, computational methods, especially when applied to rich and complex data, necessitate and codify the role of the knowing subject. More than this, they make it possible to operationalize the universal/partial binary alongside the subjective/objective binary. Their power lies in enabling scholars to model partial perspectives as both epistemology and ontology in ways not previously possible. That power carries corresponding responsibility.

## Key Terms and References

- Word2Vec: word embedding model introduced by Google in 2013 (Mikolov et al., 2013).
- Distributional hypothesis: the basis for word embeddings. Words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings.
- Double consciousness: Du Bois's term for the experience of Black Americans who must see themselves through the eyes of a dominant group that refuses to see them as they see themselves.
- Situated knowledges and the privilege of the partial perspective: Haraway (1988), the theoretical foundation for the chapter's central argument.
- Connectionism: Rumelhart (1980), the theory that concepts are meaningful through networks of associations.
- Schema: a patterned way of processing stimuli that encodes cultural knowledge, including stereotypes.
- Data provenance: the origin, lineage, and transformations of data throughout its lifecycle.
- Nelson (2021): empirical study using word embeddings and vector arithmetic to compare intersectional perspectives in nineteenth-century US South narratives.
- Biernacki (2012): critique of traditional content analysis as failing to document why particular text units receive particular codes.
- Spirling (2023): argument that closed-source generative AI models are unsuited to scientific research.
