---
title: In Defense of the Researcher-Toolmaker Figure
source: Handbook of Digital and Computational Research Methods (Madsen, Munk)
---

This concluding chapter (labeled Chapter 25 in the source text) argues for the researcher-toolmaker as a distinct and necessary figure in Digital and Computational Social Sciences and Humanities (DCSSH), and warns that the growing prominence of the research software engineer (RSE) risks reinforcing an institutional divide that is incompatible with sustainable toolmaking.

## The Core Distinction

The researcher-toolmaker holds an academic position and treats the tool they build as an extension of their own scientific agenda. The RSE occupies a support role: the institution frames the RSE as a resource for others whose scientific agenda takes precedence. Baxter et al. (2012) use the alternative label "researcher-developer" for the same figure.

**Authorship and accountability.** As argued in Chapter 20 of the same handbook, tools bear theory. They are instruments of power and contribute to the politics of method (Adams et al., 2005). Owning a tool therefore entails methodological accountability. The RSE's supposed agnosticism encourages withdrawal from authorship and from accountability. The researcher-toolmaker is more prone to own authorship and is better placed to defend the tool's scientific impact, because the tool embodies their own research.

**Attachment and motivation.** Toolmakers are generally proud of their tools and care for them even when maintenance is burdensome. This attachment is a necessary counterpoint to the recurring labor of maintenance, fundraising, and project management.

**Maintenance and repair.** Maintenance is recurring but invisible. It belongs institutionally to the regime of operations (treated as fixed costs), not to projects (treated as investments). Universities traditionally assign operations to RSEs and project leadership to researchers. The RSE-toolmaker can more easily justify repair work but risks being reassigned. The researcher-toolmaker is free to maintain the tool but must do so during research time that no funding body will sponsor.

**Project management.** Writing grant applications and managing contributors and users is labor better assumed by a researcher-toolmaker than an RSE.

## The Spectrum and the Ideal Collaboration

The authors resist a strict binary. In practice there is a continuum of profiles. Some RSEs publish and assert a scientific agenda. Some researcher-toolmakers find strategies to get maintenance done. The authors identify the most effective arrangement as collaboration across the support/academic divide, between an RSE-toolmaker (a research-prone technician) and a researcher-toolmaker (a development-prone academic). This pattern is observed at the Digital Methods Initiative in Amsterdam, at Sciences Po's medialab in Paris, and at the King's Data Lab in London. The collaboration between Stéfan Sinclair and Geoffrey Rockwell on Voyant is named as the exemplary case of this form.

## Why the Researcher-Toolmaker Must Be Defended

The authors argue that the growing prominence of the RSE should not obscure that many tools in the DCSSH were invented by people with a scientific vision and a commitment to what Philip Agre (1997) called "critical technical practice." A tool is never a neutral embodiment of a pre-existing method. Implementation reshapes the method and enacts something new and often unexpected. The toolmaker does not invent the method before the tool. They develop the method as the tool. Toolmakers' methods are natively computational and only secondarily published material, regardless of the toolmaker's institutional position.

The chapter calls the view that academic brains imagine the scientific apparatus while support hands maintain it a "managerial delusion," arguing it contradicts everything known about toolmaking in practice.

## Institutional Critique and Closing Position

The authors acknowledge the emergence of RSE networks as a genuine advance for academic toolmaking. Caring for academic infrastructures counts as a contribution to science on its own terms. But reinforcing a strict support/academic institutional divide is described as antinomic to toolmaking as a practice. The chapter closes by calling for institutional changes that support more hybrid and flexible forms of scientific engagement with technology, on both sides of the divide.
