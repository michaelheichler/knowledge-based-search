## Overview

This is the introduction to Part III of the handbook, written collectively by the authors of the six chapters that follow. Those authors are tool builders working in the digital and computational social sciences and humanities (DCSSH). The introduction frames the concerns that run through the entire section: where tools come from, what assumptions scholars bring to them, and why those assumptions are often wrong.

## The tools named

The introduction opens by listing well-known DC tools for SSH that the section's authors have built or maintain. These span several areas. For network visualization: Gephi (Bastian et al., 2009). For bibliometrics: CitNetExplorer and VOSviewer (Van Eck and Waltman, 2017). For natural language processing: DaCy (Enevoldsen et al., 2021) and Talisman (Plique, 2020). For social media research: 4CAT (Peeters and Hagen, 2022), DMI-TCAT (Borra and Rieder, 2014), Contropedia (Borra et al., 2015), Hyphe (Jacomy et al., 2016), Minet (Plique et al., 2025), Netvizz (Rieder, 2013), and YouTube Data Tools (Rieder, 2015). For applying large language models to SSH: Prompt Compass (Borra, 2023).

## The assumptions scholars hold

Tools are, by nature, taken for granted once they exist. The introduction identifies a cluster of assumptions scholars commonly make when they encounter a tool. They assume it was made by professional toolmakers. They assume it was designed for people with needs like their own. They assume its promoted use matches its design intent. They assume it comes with a method that will work for them as it works for others. They assume its popularity signals validation by the research community. They assume it is funded and maintained by dedicated staff, and that it will persist. The introduction states plainly that none of these assumptions reliably holds.

## The actual origins and trajectories of tools

The authors describe two contrasting but both common paths. In the first, a tool comes into existence accidentally, built by someone to meet a narrow personal goal who never thought of it as a tool at all. The tool then circulates to strangers who repurpose and reappropriate it, enrolling it into their own methods and producing tutorials, papers, and plugins that form a surrounding culture. This creates a gap between users and the original maker. In the second path, a tool is created and maintained by professional developers for a non-academic public, people who are not researchers and who never meet the users (OpenRefine, maintained by Antonin Delpeuch, is cited as an example). Either way, even a popular tool can become unfunded, unmaintained, or cut off from its data source, eventually breaking and being abandoned.

## The design-versus-method gap

Even setting aside questions of origin and maintenance, the introduction identifies a structural problem shared by almost all DC tools for SSH: regardless of how much methodological commitment is baked into a tool's design, that design was not aimed at enacting the current state-of-the-art method. This is partly because there is rarely one consensual state-of-the-art method, but primarily because the toolmaker's motivation lies elsewhere.

## Why this matters for research practice

Understanding the origins, trajectories, and limitations of tools is not presented as an abstract academic exercise. The introduction argues it has direct consequences. Tools evolve, break, get discontinued, or resist adaptation in ways that shape the questions scholars can ask and answer. Engaging with toolmakers and their processes lets researchers navigate these changes, influence tool development, troubleshoot problems, or build custom solutions. It also invites recognition of toolmakers as collaborators in the research ecosystem. The introduction adds that tools are vehicles for scholarly impact: mastering or contributing to a tool can embed scholars in new research networks and collaborative projects.

## Context for the section

The six-chapter section grew from a panel on toolmakers convened by Aalborg University's MASSHINE center in Fall 2023. The authors come from an international range of institutions across humanities, social sciences, media studies, and scientometrics. They present their observations as broad and endemic to the field rather than specific to one institution or country.

A recurring thread through the chapters is a long interview with Geoffrey Rockwell, one of the makers (along with the late Stefan Sinclair) of Voyant, an online platform for hermeneutical text exploration in the digital humanities. His experiences with Voyant are used to illustrate the multiple facets addressed across the section.
