## Overview

Chapter 24 (titled "Valuing toolmaking in academia") is written by Mathieu Jacomy, Bernhard Rieder, Erik Borra, Guillaume Plique, Kenneth Christian Enevoldsen, and Nees Jan van Eck. It examines why academic institutions struggle to recognize and reward the work of building and maintaining research tools in digital and computational social science and the humanities (DCSSH). The authors aim both to advise aspiring toolmakers and to sensitize tool users to the systemic pressures that sustain research infrastructure.

## The Core Problem: Operations Do Not Count as Academic

The chapter opens with the project-management distinction between project work and operations work. Building a prototype is project work: it happens once, produces an event, and can be written up as a peer-reviewed publication. Maintaining a tool is operations work: it recurs indefinitely, produces no discrete event, and generates no recognized academic output.

Funding bodies want to invest in self-sustaining projects that minimize operating costs, so they resist funding maintenance. The costs of a popular tool include not only code upkeep and infrastructure but also documentation, community management, and web presence. Peer review similarly excludes operations on two grounds: maintenance appears to add nothing new, and its output is not treated as knowledge. Academic culture rewards novelty, and novelty must take the form of abstract, reusable knowledge. Describing a new algorithm is publication-worthy. Implementing it in a tool is not. Code is read as the material production of a worker, not the knowledge production of a thinker.

Geoffrey Rockwell (maker of Voyant Tools, quoted from a 2023 interview by Jacomy) illustrates the point: a new release of Voyant earns credit once, exactly as a new book does. The fact that maintenance continues the following year earns nothing further.

## Hidden Labor

The chapter frames the result as a hidden labor problem. A significant share of the time required to bring a tool into existence and keep it running remains invisible to academic accounting and therefore unfunded. This is not limited to toolmakers. Derrick et al. (2024), writing about the Festival of Hidden REF (Research Excellence Framework), describe a broader category of "hidden roles" including librarians, technicians, research software engineers, and data managers, all overlooked by a culture that equates value in research with publications.

The chapter distinguishes two sites of hidden labor.

Tool creation is unsolicited. A tool proves its value only during its lifecycle, when it is cited in seminal papers or used widely enough to be visible. At the moment of creation there is no institutional demand for it. Creation may be folded into a grant-funded research project as a camouflaged activity, which protects the toolmaker's freedom initially but creates problems once the tool acquires a public user base.

Tool sustenance involves a different kind of invisibility. Universities face three compounding difficulties in acknowledging it. First, they typically do not understand what sustenance consists of: source code is only part of the tool, and project management, technical watch, documentation, web design, and community management are equally necessary but entirely alien to most academic management. Second, toolmaking is culturally foreign to universities. Smithies et al. (2019), reporting on maintaining DH projects at King's Digital Lab (KDL) at King's College London, note that collaborators become uneasy with "industry" frameworks and "business" language, while funding agencies can handle data management plans but not the ongoing costs of the systems that generate and store data. Third, universities expect SSH outputs to have no recurring operating costs. Fixed costs are handled by support functions, not research budgets. KDL's experience, as reported by Smithies et al., was that funders did not expect to support long-term digital resources, and contractual responsibility for sustaining tools was routinely left undefined.

This produces a structural paradox. Universities want tool creation to lie on the research side (so it counts as academic output) and tool sustenance to lie on the support side (so it can be managed and overseen). A researcher-toolmaker falls between both categories. A research software engineer (RSE) is not permitted to pursue an independent research agenda, but a researcher who maintains a tool is doing something universities cannot classify or fund.

## Four Strategies for Repurposing Publication

Because software development does not naturally pass as academic work, toolmakers have developed a spectrum of strategies for extracting academic recognition from it.

**Strategy 1: The closet toolmaker.** The researcher builds tools but does not disclose them publicly, using the tool as a private competitive advantage in the publication market. A variant: tools so arcane that only the toolmaker's immediate circle can use them, such as Prospero and Marlowe developed by Francis Chateauraynaud and Jean-Pierre Charriau, which functioned more as embodiments of an ongoing research program than as widely usable tools.

**Strategy 2: Publish the science, not the tool.** The tool is released but only the research it enables is published. The tool may gain autonomy as others reuse it, but the toolmaker never publishes it as a citable object. The case study becomes the de facto citation. Contropedia, developed by Weltevrede and Borra (2016), is cited this way.

**Strategy 3: The proxy paper.** A publication stands in for the tool as a citable object. Gephi is the canonical example: it is almost universally cited as Bastian et al. (2009), a poster presented at ICWSM 2009, which had accumulated roughly 12,800 citations by August 2024 and received the ICWSM 2019 Test of Time Award, even though Gephi changed substantially in the intervening decade. Proxy paper titles typically begin with the tool name (e.g., "Scikit-learn: Machine Learning in Python," Pedregosa et al., 2011). The ForceAtlas2 algorithm for Gephi illustrates the friction involved: the paper was initially rejected from PLoS One for insufficient novelty, accepted only after the authors added a quantitative benchmark that, the chapter notes, is essentially irrelevant to the researchers who cite the paper. Proxy papers carry a structural flaw: the tool evolves while the paper stays fixed. The case of Jupyter Notebooks shows how a proxy paper can emerge by default. Kluyver et al. (2016), with about 3,200 citations by August 2024, became the de facto citation after the developers offered it as a temporary placeholder in a 2016 GitHub issue that remained open eight years later.

**Strategy 4: Cite the tool directly as an academic artifact.** A tool can be cited in APA format without peer review. Example given: "Ramos Ibanez, E., Bastian, M., and Jacomy, M. (2023). Gephi (0.10.1) [Software]." A Digital Object Identifier (DOI) helps but is not required for citability. The more developed form is the "software paper," as described by Jay et al. (2021): a publication whose peer review evaluates the tool itself, published in venues such as SoftwareX, the Journal of Open Research Software (JORS), the Journal of Open Source Software (JOSS), or F1000 Research. Unlike proxy papers, software papers are assessed on the tool's own merits, and they are more accessible to toolmakers who are less practiced at conventional academic writing.

The overall trajectory runs from covert strategies (the tool hidden behind its scientific results) to overt ones (the tool published as the primary object). The authors note that more overt strategies have become increasingly viable in recent years.

## Repurposing Grants for Toolmaking

Grants are a legitimate funding path, subject to three conditions. The tool must be sufficiently established and reputable for a funding body to trust its academic validity. The application must frame tool support as generating new academic value (new features, not maintenance). And someone must be available to write the application, which requires a combination of technical competence, research writing ability, and project management capacity that is difficult for a single person to sustain.

In practice, maintenance remains taboo as a budget line. Toolmakers work around this by overcharging feature implementation in grant applications to create space for maintenance time. All parties may be tacitly aware of this. The chapter treats this as a workaround that reinforces the taboo rather than dissolving it, leaving long-term sustainability unresolved.

## Changing Conditions

The chapter identifies several trends that are improving the situation.

The normalization of coding in research, supported by tools like Jupyter Notebooks, has created a more continuous gradient between developer and researcher, making coding more legible as a form of writing. Publishing code has become straightforward through the Open Science Framework and Zenodo (European Organization for Nuclear Research and OpenAIRE, 2013), with Zenodo supporting versioned software releases linked to GitHub commits via automatically updated DOIs.

Institutional recognition is also advancing. The Netherlands has incorporated software into its quality and relevance indicators for the humanities (Quality and Relevance in Humanities, 2024). KDL has formalized a Checklist for Digital Outputs Assessment (Ciula, 2019). The Software Sustainability Institute in the United Kingdom has published guidance for submitting software outputs. In France, Gomez-Diaz and Recio (2019) proposed the CDUR protocol (Citation, Dissemination, Use, and Research) for evaluating research software, addressing problems including unregistered software production, unclear authorship and versioning, chaotic dissemination, absence of licenses, and lack of laboratory policy on software.

Structurally, the Amsterdam Declaration on Funding Research Software Sustainability signals intent to change how funders approach the problem. In the Netherlands, the PDI-SSH infrastructure grant funded DMI-TCAT, YouTube Data Tools, and 4CAT. The Dutch NLnet Foundation has stabilized small- and medium-scale open source projects. The convergence of open source and open science movements may create institutional will at the European level to build sustainable digital commons.

The chapter closes by quoting Jay et al. (2021): "official recognition of software as a research output will ultimately be transformative, improving the quality, reproducibility and scalability of our knowledge production, as well as recognising the often hidden role of the increasing number of scholarly researchers who spend most of their time writing code."
