## Overview

Chapter 12, written by Richard Rogers, develops the concept of "online groundedness," which asks under what conditions online data can serve as a reliable basis for claims about broader societal and cultural phenomena rather than merely about the online world itself. The chapter traces the origins and evolution of this idea, examines recurring problems with online data as a research site, and surveys how researchers have adapted their practices after social media platforms began restricting data access.

## The Concept and Its Origins

The term "online groundedness" emerged in debates about internet research from the late 1990s and 2000s (Jones, 1998; Woolgar, 2002; Hine, 2005). At that time, the internet was commonly framed as "the virtual" or "cyberspace," a domain treated as separate from ordinary social reality. Rogers challenged this framing in a 2009 work titled "The End of the Virtual," arguing that the web should no longer be studied purely as its own realm but rather as a source of evidence about the world beyond it. This reorientation aligned with the roughly simultaneous emergence of the computational social sciences (Lazer et al., 2009) and what Duncan Watts called "21st-century science," both of which proposed repurposing digital traces for social inquiry.

Google Trends, launched in 2009, gave the concept practical traction. It presented itself as a tool to "predict the present" (Choi and Varian, 2009a), charting societal interest through aggregated search query volumes. This approach was also called "commercial sociology" (Burrows and Savage, 2014). The broader wave of big data studies followed from the same premise: that large volumes of web activity could stand as indicators of mass social behavior.

## Forms of Online Groundedness

Rogers distinguishes several arrangements for how online data are grounded:

**Guarded or mixed-methods groundedness.** Most research treats online data as a starting point that requires validation through qualitative work or comparison against known outcomes (such as election results or disease surveillance records). The online findings are considered preliminary until confirmed by some other baseline.

**Digital epidemiology.** Google Flu Trends (GFT), launched around 2009, used geolocated search queries about flu symptoms to anticipate influenza incidence faster than traditional public health surveillance. Its findings were validated against data from national health institutes. GFT was discontinued in 2015, partly because changes to Google Autocomplete and other algorithmic features corrupted the signal (Lazer et al., 2014). Even after GFT ended, a review of over 100 digital epidemiology studies found that more than 80% still drew on search engine or social media data (Park et al., 2018). The majority of such studies are exploratory rather than predictive.

**Online triangulation.** A researcher uses a second online data set to confirm what was found in the first. For example, geolocated recipe searches (as indicators of regional food preferences) might be reinforced by geotagged Instagram food photos with an event hashtag such as Thanksgiving.

**Comparative online analysis.** Comparing the "same" Wikipedia article across different language versions to identify cultural or linguistic particularism is a form of online groundedness that uses no offline referent at all (Rogers and Sendijarevic, 2012). Swings in Wikipedia article views and edits have similarly been used as proxies for public interest.

**Ungrounded indicators.** Some uses of Google Trends or social media data treat online signals as sufficient on their own, without a second qualitative step or external validation. Seth Stephens-Davidowitz's work on gender bias and racism using search query volumes is cited as an example (Stephens-Davidowitz, 2017). The argument for using search data alone is that because searches are treated as private by users, they may reveal attitudes that surveys and polling would not capture. The terms "database of intentions" (Battelle, 2003) and "honest signals" (Pentland, 2008) were used early on to describe this quality of search data.

## Problems with Online Data

Rogers catalogs four main categories of concern:

**Messiness and platform effects.** Online data are entangled with the algorithms that produce them. Google Autocomplete distorted GFT by shaping what users queried. On X/Twitter (formerly Twitter), hashtags are routinely hijacked for commercial purposes, and on TikTok, popular hashtags are "stuffed" with unrelated content. Facebook's "Meaningful Social Interaction" (MSI) algorithm, revealed by whistleblower Frances Haugen, elevated posts that generated "angry" reactions above those that received simple likes, on the grounds that anger produces more engagement. The result is data shaped by a platform logic rather than organic user interest. Some scholars conclude that heavily moderated platforms can only be used reliably to study platform culture itself, not broader social states.

**Representativeness.** Social media users are not representative of general populations. UK and US studies consistently found that Twitter and Facebook users skew younger, more politically engaged, and in the US more Democratic than the overall public. Among X/Twitter users in the US, the most prolific 10% produce 80% of all posts (Wojcik and Hughes, 2019). The myth of participatory culture, associated with titles like "The Wisdom of Crowds" (Surowiecki, 2005) and "Here Comes Everybody" (Shirky, 2008), was undercut by findings that only a small fraction of platform users create most content. Political content is disproportionately generated by elites compared to social and entertainment content (Blank, 2013).

**Data availability and archiving.** Social media companies hold their own archives, but it is unclear how complete these are. X/Twitter donated its first ten years of posts to the Library of Congress, but that arrangement ended in 2018. Content moderation and deplatforming have removed significant material, making research on subcultures such as the online "alternative right" difficult. What remains after deplatforming may amount to "traces of traces," such as screenshots found through image search.

**Algorithmic entanglement as the underlying problem.** Because platforms optimize for user watch time and engagement, all content served through them has been shaped to some degree by those incentive structures. Professional content creators further optimize their own posts. These effects cannot always be cleaned from the data.

## The APIcalypse and the Post-API Era

From the mid-2010s onward, social media platforms began restricting or closing the APIs that researchers had depended on. Axel Bruns named this the "APIcalypse" (Bruns, 2019). Freelon introduced the phrase "post-API age" to describe the research environment that followed (Freelon, 2018). Rogers identifies five directions researchers have taken in response:

**1. Maintaining the status quo.** Some work continues with social media and search engine data, acknowledging restrictions as a concern without abandoning the approach. Digital epidemiology research largely fits this pattern, though some scholars propose broadening the field to include digitized data not derived from platforms (Salathé, 2018).

**2. Pivoting to marketing data dashboards.** Dashboards such as Meta's own tools or BuzzSumo provide data comparable to what APIs once delivered but are organized around engagement as the primary sorting metric, which reflects marketing rather than research logic. Craig Silverman's BuzzFeed News work on fake news outperforming mainstream news on Facebook during the 2016 US election used BuzzSumo in this way. This approach is suited to studying platform phenomena and social issues together in a parallel or symmetrical fashion. Marketing firms do not have a stake in shaping user behavior the way platforms do, which is cited as a reason their measurement cultures are more robust (Wu et al., 2020).

**3. Cleaning data of platform effects.** Researchers attempt to remove bot activity, fake followers, algorithmic boosting, and other forms of platform manipulation before analysis. One technique is "platform perspectivism": using one platform to study another. For example, OILAB collected YouTube videos shared on 4chan to determine which had been removed by YouTube and by what method (channel-level versus individual video moderation). Content moderation critique similarly uses remaining traces to map removals, demotions, and demonetizations.

**4. Turning to "alt tech" and less moderated platforms.** These "alt tech" platforms have lower moderation and are less protective of their data. They attract users who have been deplatformed elsewhere and can serve as a window into a "splinternet" (Malcomson, 2016) reflecting domestic and broader culture wars. The research question here is whether low moderation coincides with more extreme speech and what that implies for the study of societal trends.

**5. Returning to scraping.** Web scraping bypasses APIs and dashboards but typically violates platform terms of service. The chapter notes two distinct proposals for handling this. Freelon (2018) recommends exhausting all platform-compliant collection methods before resorting to scraping. Mancosu and Vegetti (2020) propose developing a scraping technique that complies with GDPR without requiring a logged-in account. The legal landscape is contested: the US case Sandvig v. Sessions explored whether algorithmic auditing for discrimination might justify scraping, and the judge described scraping as "merely a technological advance" comparable to using a voice recorder instead of taking notes. In Europe, the Digital Services Act now grants vetted researchers limited data access contingent on providing credentials, a research proposal, and pre-publication review by the company.

Rogers also notes a distinction between scraping as interface-level data collection and API-based collection as developer-level data. The scraping mode captures what was actually served to a user. API data reflect what was boosted through algorithmic scoring. These represent competing online grounds, each offering a different window on the same phenomena.

Non-platform-centric collection methods have also been employed, including X/Twitter's 1% and 10% random samples and the Crimson Hexagon tool's 10,000-tweet samples (which had access to Twitter's "firehose"). A dataset of all tweets by parliamentarians from 26 European countries (van Vliet et al., 2020) is cited as an example of a collection designed to avoid engagement-driven distortion.

## State of the Field

Rogers describes the current situation as a retreat from the optimism of the "heady days" when web data were seen as a reliable societal barometer. The coincidence around 2009 of the computational turn, the rise of big data, abundant trace data, and the concept of online groundedness created high expectations. Since then, attention has shifted toward the problems: content moderation, algorithmic boosting, representativeness gaps, and restricted data access. The proposed remedies in the post-API era largely concern navigating these problems rather than restoring the original promise.

One response to restricted access is to build academic-industry partnerships rather than an adversarial relationship (Lazer et al., 2020). Meta's "data clean rooms" are an instance of this: researchers run analyses on company servers and retain aggregates rather than raw data. What ends up in academic data repositories under this arrangement would be aggregates, metadata, and paradata rather than primary records.
