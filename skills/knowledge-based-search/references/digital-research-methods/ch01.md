## Overview

This introduction by Anders Koed Madsen and Anders Kristian Munk frames the handbook's purpose and its central argument: Digital and Computational Social Sciences and Humanities (DCSSH) is a heterodox field whose breadth is obscured by stereotypes promoted by both enthusiasts and critics. The authors position the field as a practical response to a changed empirical situation, not a product of technological fetishism or a drive to harden soft disciplines.

## The Field and Its Problem

Three developments over the past 25 years altered SSH research: new types of data became available, new analytical methods became accessible, and new possibilities for making findings public became standard. The resulting cluster of practices is what the authors call DCSSH. A persistent problem is that researchers working in this space are typecast with epistemic commitments they do not hold. The opening question at SSH conferences, "isn't this Big Data?", carries the subtext that computational methods imply positivism, quantification, and political alignment with surveillance and control. Both advocates and critics reinforce this subtext, leaving little room for alternative accounts.

## Five Foundational Provocations

The editors asked all contributing authors to engage with five explicit rejections of default assumptions. Each provocation states that there is no self-evident reason to accept a particular equation.

1. The digital is not opposed to the actual. Digital empirical material does not entail a loss of grounding. It can be treated as part of the ground itself.
2. Computation does not automatically privilege quantification over qualitative inquiry. Using computers to explore large unstructured corpora does not replace qualitative ambitions with a quantitative gold standard.
3. Machine learning does not inherently serve prediction and control. Descriptive, generative, and exploratory uses are equally available.
4. Using computational techniques does not preclude critique of computation in society. Proximity to algorithms can enable more grounded criticism rather than less.
5. User-friendliness, IT support, and black-boxed tools do not automatically promote reflexive scholarship. Relying on ready-made tools risks outsourcing ontological and epistemological choices without awareness.

## The Digital and the Actual

The chapter traces a shift in thinking about the relationship between online and offline. In the 1990s, proponents of cyberspace imagined the internet as a separate reality free from geography and social hierarchy. Steve Woolgar's "Virtual Society" project (2002) and Daniel Miller's work on social media (2016) showed instead that digital technologies are always woven into everyday practices. Richard Rogers' 2009 inaugural lecture "The End of the Virtual" argued that because the digital is integrated into the everyday, online traces can ground a "web epistemology" for studying social phenomena. Noortje Marres (2015) extended this by warning against treating digital bias purely as noise, noting that actors like bots are constitutive parts of the online public sphere, not mere distortion.

The handbook applies this across disciplines. Schneider's chapter on digital foodscapes shows that food practices are now constructed across online and offline environments simultaneously. Breslin and Albris make the same point for contemporary anthropology. Borch and Skar-Gislinge address financial markets, where algorithms occupy central roles in high-frequency trading, making direct observation impossible and requiring methods such as agent-based modeling.

The opposite error is equally problematic: assuming that online data traces are more objective or theory-free than other empirical materials.

## Computation and the Qualitative-Quantitative Divide

The institutionalized divide between qualitative and quantitative research traditions in SSH is, historically speaking, recent. Venturini and Latour (2009) described digital mediation as a "giant carbon paper" of human action, arguing this rendered the divide obsolete. It had arisen as a practical consequence of the available empirical infrastructures, not from any deeper epistemological necessity. Statistics required aggregates. Fieldwork required single sites. Digital storage formats like JSON can hold qualitative and quantitative data in the same infrastructure. Computer vision and natural language processing can make qualitative materials countable. Franco Moretti's "distant reading" (2013) is one named practice that exploits this possibility: use network patterns to identify key brokers of information, then zoom in and read the text they produce.

## Machine Learning Beyond Prediction

Machine learning is commonly associated with prediction. The chapter challenges this by listing non-predictive applications across the handbook's chapters: Nelson's work on operationalizing partial perspectives, Mauri's explorative data visualizations, and Rogers' finding that only 15% of digital epidemiology projects have predictive ambitions. Even techniques like neural networks and decision trees, which in their supervised form are evaluated on ability to predict a target variable, can be repurposed.

Two named examples illustrate using machine learning to "slow down reasoning" rather than to predict. The "Thick Machine" project (Munk et al., 2022) staged a competition between a neural network and qualitative researchers to see how each guessed Facebook emoji reactions, with the analytical focus on the mistakes each made. The "Indistinct cities" project (Zhang et al., 2019) trained a neural network to classify city photographs by origin and attended closely to the confusion matrix, not as a measure of algorithmic precision but as a guide to finding locations that break a city's aesthetic pattern.

Decision trees are sometimes preferred over neural networks in SSH precisely because their lower predictive power comes with greater transparency: the tree structure makes visible how the algorithm divides data, supporting description rather than black-box output.

## Computation and Critical Practice

Because proximity to technical systems enables a kind of grounded critique unavailable from a distance, using computational methods is not in tension with critiquing computation. Latour's concept of "critical proximity" is invoked as an alternative to armchair criticism of technological society. Niederer and Colombo's "hacking ethos" is cited as an example: practical engagement with algorithmic systems carries critical potential. An illustrative case is Bernhard Rieder, author of the data curation tool Netvizz, who raised questions about Facebook's API permissions five years before the Cambridge Analytica scandal (Rieder, 2013). Kurpicz-Briki's work on bias in LLMs argues that meaningful critique of algorithmic bias requires concrete experimental setups involving system design, data collection, model training, and validation. Tool-making is presented as an inherently reflexive and critical practice, not a support function.

## Tool Design and Epistemic Outsourcing

Designing a tool involves making analytical choices. Using someone else's tool without understanding it means adopting those choices unreflexively. The authors use the analogy of a plumber: not knowing where your pipes are makes it impossible to have a meaningful negotiation about what needs fixing. The same applies to DCSSH. Rogers' contribution notes that popular social media dashboards embed the analytical logic of engagement metrics as the primary relevance signal, which may serve marketing research but is a poor fit for sociology or media studies. Mauri's chapter on visualization notes that many popular tools share the same framework derived from Bertin's work on visual variables, and the existence of graphical user interfaces can make this framework seem transparent rather than chosen. Schneider's chapter raises the related point that design specifications for a good inclusive research app and a commercially successful app may be different.

## A New Empirical Situation: 2004-2010

The authors identify the years 2004 to 2010 as a watershed for DCSSH, when methods and empirical situations began to merge. Three developments stand out.

First, social platforms launched and grew rapidly. Facebook (2004), Twitter (2006), and Instagram (2010) generated vast, granular, and continuous digital traces of social relations, political opinions, and more. By the end of this period, Facebook was gaining 100 million users per year. These traces cross the qualitative-quantitative divide and are of direct interest to SSH disciplines, though platforms have more recently begun restricting research access.

Second, the iPhone (2007) and the App Store (2008) put continuous internet access in users' pockets. This allowed digital traces to be left on the move, not only at fixed locations. It also enabled geo-tagged metadata to be added to shared content and changed the spatial and temporal character of digital trace production.

Third, large-scale digitization projects began. Google launched its Books project in 2004, scanning 12 million books by 2010 and launching the N-gram viewer for comparing words and phrases over time. Public initiatives included the Digital Public Library of America, initiated by Harvard's Berkman Center and later government-funded, which unified previously disparate sources such as the Library of Congress and the Internet Archive.

These developments prompted methodological responses. The Digital Methods Initiative was founded in Amsterdam in 2005 to research using traces born on the web (likes, shares, ratings) rather than digitized analogs. Michael Goodchild coined "Volunteered Geographic Information" (VGI) in 2007 to draw urban professionals' attention to the empirical value of phone-generated data. Lazer et al. (2009) coined "computational social science" in Science to make a parallel argument for sociological study of human dynamics. The US Office of Digital Humanities was initiated in 2008.

The key point the authors draw is that methods and empirical situations merged in this period. Network analysis was not just a method applied to pupils (as Moreno had done in the 1930s). It was now a factor in how the digital world itself was organized. Machine learning's potential to detect patterns in communication could now actually be trained on SSH-relevant traces because enough of them existed.

## Generative AI and the Present Moment

The introduction was written in 2025 and adds a reflection on generative AI. LLMs are now more capable of understanding context than those of a few years ago, synthetic datasets are possible, and synthetic interviewers can be imagined as a way to scale unstructured data collection. ChatGPT made computational methods a public concern nearly overnight. It opened experimentation with large unstructured datasets to a much broader range of organizations. Language models also enable what the authors call "vibe coding" (chat-based programming), which lowers barriers for SSH students and researchers with limited scripting proficiency in Python or R. This has given DCSSH a new public with opinions about its future direction and the distribution of research funding.

## Stereotypes That Constrain the Field

Three stereotypes act as a straitjacket on DCSSH, each with a distinct origin.

The positivist stereotype holds that DCSSH replaces theory with objective, data-driven analysis. This narrative originates partly in the tech sector's framing of digital traces as "honest signals" (Pentland, 2010) left without researcher prompting, and in editorial claims like Wired magazine's 2008 "end of theory" declaration (Anderson, 2008). Some DCSSH researchers reinforced it by explicitly claiming objectivity (Dalvean, 2015 is cited as an example in literary theory). Critics also reinforce it: Timothy Brennan (2017) in The Chronicle of Higher Education described digital humanities as applying "a scientistic gloss on method" in opposition to genuine humanistic thinking. The authors note this critique only holds if the researchers in question actually believe the end of theory, and most do not.

The technological solutionism stereotype, articulated by Allington, Brouillette, and Golumbia (2016), charges DCSSH with elevating technical and managerial competencies as a superior form of humanist knowledge, displacing critical scholarship in favor of tool manufacture. The handbook's final section by Jacomy and colleagues is described as a counterweight, showing that tool-making is itself a critical and reflexive practice, and also a precarious academic career path rather than a position of dominance.

The surveillance capitalism stereotype draws on Shoshana Zuboff's (2019) framework. Zuboff shows how big tech companies extract and commoditize the surplus digital labor of users. When applied to DCSSH, this frame implies that working with digital traces and machine learning means participating in or enabling exploitation. The authors note an irony: this critique shares a subtext with tech-sector narratives, namely that digital traces are valuable raw materials and that machine learning applied to them is powerful. Venturini (Chapter 15) is cited as arguing that many critics "buy too readily" into the rhetoric of algorithmic control and give too much credit to anecdotes such as supermarket pregnancy prediction or Cambridge Analytica's supposed steering of the 2016 US election.

## Longer Histories

The authors caution against treating the 2004-2010 period as a sharp break. The web's consequences began with the World Wide Web in 1991. John Perry Barlow's 1996 Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace and Nicholas Negroponte's 1995 vision of cyberspace as a liberating force preceded DCSSH by a decade. Early methodological innovations included netnography (Kozinets, 1998), web cartography (Rogers and Marres, 2000), and network analysis of online messages (Danowski, 1993). The anthology "Doing Internet Research" (Jones, 1998) was one of the first collections.

Computational techniques used in DCSSH also have long histories. Network analysis traces back to Moreno's work on "psychological geographies" in the 1930s, and core network metrics were developed decades ago. UCInet was initiated in the early 1980s and is still in wide use. Jacomy (2019) coined the term "digital glitter" for the tendency to use network imagery to signal novelty despite the method's century-old history. Neural networks were introduced in the 1950s. Decision trees and agent-based modeling became popular in the 1970s. Bibliometrics (Lawani, 1981) and citation analysis (Garfield, 1972) were established methods in information science. Co-word analysis combined natural language processing and network analysis to chart research themes in the same era. Wordij, one of the first GUIs for semantic network analysis, was introduced in 1991. John Tukey's 1977 book on exploratory data analysis argued that statistics and visualization should follow a more exploratory route.

## Structure of the Handbook

The handbook is organized in three parts.

Part I, "Disciplinary perspectives," contains nine chapters, each offering one discipline's account of how digital and computational developments have created particular challenges and possibilities within an existing ecology of methods, theories, and questions.

Part II, "Epistemic trouble," contains nine chapters, each revisiting a central concept from the philosophy of science in light of the computational turn. Authors trace the history of the concept and explain why DCSSH prompts reconsideration.

Part III, "Making the instruments of social sciences and humanities," gives voice to scholars and research engineers who build the tools DCSSH researchers use to gather, organize, analyze, and visualize data. It functions as an open roundtable addressing who makes tools, what motivates their design, how generic a tool can be, and how tools can be maintained.
