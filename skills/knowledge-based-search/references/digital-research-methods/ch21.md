## Overview

Chapter 20 (titled "Toolmaking is science by other means") argues that digital and computational (DC) tools for the social sciences and humanities (SSH) are not neutral technical aids but are academically loaded scientific instruments. The chapter is written by Mathieu Jacomy, Bernhard Rieder, Erik Borra, Guillaume Plique, Kenneth Christian Enevoldsen, and Nees Jan van Eck. Its core claims are that toolmaking is a form of scientific practice, that most DC tools are built for their makers rather than their users, that teaching shapes tool popularity in ways that compound over time, and that tools bear theory in a distinctive non-textual form the chapter calls "infrastructured."

## What a DC Tool Is

DC tools for SSH are scientific instruments in the same family as telescopes, microscopes, and MRI machines. The word "instrument" is more precise than "tool" because a scientific instrument exists to make a measurement or produce knowledge, not merely to intervene on matter. These instruments occupy a position at the intersection of infrastructure (durable technology maintained by support staff) and research (methodological objects with scientific purpose). This double nature is unusual: most university infrastructure is not specific to science, and most methods require active human participation to deploy, but an algorithm embedded in a tool can be executed by anyone without the user fully understanding it.

A tool, as a product, has a name, a stated function, and accountable developers. It may also have documentation, a website, and a user community. It stops being a tool and becomes infrastructure when it grows into a system to administer. It stops being a tool and reverts to pure practice when it is not documented or shared. The boundary between tool, infrastructure, and pure method is a spectrum, not a sharp line.

## Why DC Tools Are Not Built for Their Users

The chapter states plainly that meeting the needs of other researchers is rarely the main reason a DC tool is created. The market of potential users (students, scholars, journalists, activists) is too small and economically precarious to sustain development aimed at capturing a user base. Instead, the chapter identifies four primary reasons tools come into existence.

First, some tools are convenience tools made by a researcher-coder to solve a problem they personally face, then shared because sharing is cheap once the tool exists. Examples include Erik Borra's compare lists, URL harvester, TLD counter, Expand Tiny URLs, and GeoIP lookup for URLs.

Second, some tools are built to disseminate a method. The Lippmannian Device, built around a Google scraper and grounded in Walter Lippmann's 1922 theories of public opinion, allows researchers to identify which web actors align with specific issues. Pierre Ratinaud's text analysis tool IRaMuTeQ (2009) is explicitly dedicated to the ALCESTE method devised by Max Reinert (1990). Tiago Peixoto's Graph-tool library (2014) is openly opinionated, focused on the maker's own algorithms and skeptical of off-the-shelf alternatives.

Third, some tools are byproducts of academic practice. Bernard Rieder's Netvizz, a now-discontinued Facebook data extraction app active for over ten years, began in 2008 as a software experiment, became a way to understand Facebook's API, then became a means for Rieder to develop programming skills, and only later turned into a maintained product because of user demand.

Fourth, some tools aim to make a theoretical argument. The chapter lists a decade-long literature on this point: "building as theoretical modeling" (McCarty, 2004), "prototype as theory" (Manovich), "prototype as argument" (Galey and Ruecker, 2010), "API as argument" (Sherratt, 2015), "design as argument," "visualization tools as hermeneutical theory" (Rockwell, 2001), and "tinkering as scholarly reverie" (Prescott, 2014). Gephisto (Jacomy and Munk, 2024) was created to argue that tools supporting critical thinking do not have to be hostile to beginners.

Geoffrey Rockwell, maker of Voyant Tools, is quoted explaining that conducting a formal needs analysis with potential users produced less interesting results than simply building what the makers themselves found useful and needed.

A consequence of this pattern is uneven coverage: some researcher needs are addressed by multiple tools while others go unaddressed entirely, because popularity is not the primary incentive. The Schreibman and Hanlon (2010) survey of DH tool developers found that low adoption ranked fourth out of four concerns, not first.

## Tools as Teaching Devices

Teaching is often what makes a tool popular, and popularity is often what keeps a tool alive. Netvizz began as a demonstrator for a web programming course in 2008. Gephi was not made with teaching in mind but became the subject of at least two independently authored books (Cherven, 2015; Khokhar, 2015). Scott Weingart described Voyant Tools as "the gateway drug for digital humanities."

The relationship between teaching and popularity is a reinforcement loop: teaching a tool increases its user base, and a larger user base increases the probability that other teachers adopt it. This loop creates inertia. Tools accumulate audiences slowly but hold them for years, sometimes long after active development stops. A tool may still receive bug reports years after its developer has left, because it was being taught in a course the developer never knew about.

User communities that form around a tool through teaching are not unified or universal. Each community is situated, developing its own partial perspective on the tool's purpose, strengths, preferred settings, and good practices. The chapter gives Gephi as an example. Network scientists find it user-friendly and use it for visualization but consider its embedded algorithms too basic. Digital humanists find it difficult to learn but use it as their primary platform for computing network statistics. The former tends to understand networks as statistical models. The latter tends to treat them as descriptive phenomena. Some user communities form distinct epistemic cultures (Knorr Cetina, 1999).

Several DCSSH tools were built by scholars who were not themselves comfortable with existing technology. This discomfort motivated toolmaking as a way to gain understanding or skill from the process, not just from the result. Such makers tend to produce tools that are naturally attuned to the classroom because the maker acted as a "passeur" of methods for beginners.

## Tools Empower Users and Makers

Tools empower users by extending what they can observe and do, but this empowerment cannot be fully controlled or predicted. The tool-user assemblage (the equipped researcher) produces directions that emerge from a dialogue between the researcher's perspective and the theories built into the instrument. The opportunity to repurpose a tool is inherent to the empowerment it provides. Critics including Johanna Drucker (2014), Eef Masson (2017), and Dennis Tenen (2016) have noted that this empowerment does not guarantee good science.

The chapter argues that toolmaking also empowers the maker, and that this empowerment matters for academic knowledge production. Rieder, Peeters, and Borra (2024) describe this in three levels. At the first level, research software enters into conversation with existing knowledge and methodology. At the second level, the process of diving into specifications, encountering technical restrictions, and discovering loopholes is "epistemically productive," generating new pathways that become features or methods. At the third level, end users contribute to the tool's evolution and are in turn developed by the tool, which presents approaches to research they may not yet know.

The ForceAtlas2 algorithm for Gephi (Jacomy et al., 2014) illustrates this. While building Gephi's layout functionality, the makers independently rediscovered "edge-repulsion," a technique previously identified by Andreas Noack (2007), which produces better visual separation of network clusters. This led to a reexamination of whether the aesthetic qualities of ForceAtlas2 and LinLog stem from the algorithms' general energy model or from edge-repulsion itself. Making the tool opened an entry point into an academic debate about graph drawing and clustering.

## Tools Bear Theory

The central theoretical argument of the chapter is that tools bear theory. This claim does not assert that tools apply pre-existing theories. It asserts that theory is constituted within the process of toolmaking itself and then embedded in the tool in a form distinct from textual theory.

The chapter calls this form "infrastructured theory" (drawing on Gray, Gerlitz, and Bounegru, 2018). Infrastructured theories differ from theories expressed in text in two ways. They lose the discursive properties of text: they are less explicit, less discussable, harder to criticize, and may escape criticism entirely. In exchange, they gain affordances (Gibson, 1966), meaning material properties that guide user behavior without requiring prior explanation. Infrastructured theories are not read but enacted. Using a tool can convey patterns and know-how that a user may not be able to put into words, and this nonverbal knowledge is still theoretical insofar as it gives currency to some explanations over others. Ramsay and Rockwell (2012) are quoted: "to say that software is a theory is to say that digital works convey knowledge the way a theory does."

Because the toolmaker cannot fully control what theory ends up in a tool, the Trojan horse metaphor used by critics (Drucker, 2014; Masson, 2017) is only partially correct. The metaphor captures the tool's ability to obscure its theoretical load but wrongly implies that the maker deliberately hid and conveyed that load to influence or deceive users. In practice, when makers try to operationalize a theory it often fails, and they often end up implementing theories they are not aware of. The Lippmannian Device is given as an example: it originated from a simple Google scraper that was then reloaded with theory, not from a deliberate plan to embed Lippmann's ideas.

Rieder et al. (2024) argue for "tool criticism" as a practice that does not merely uncover hidden epistemological assumptions but actively shapes how tools are designed, documented, and communicated.

Even design decisions that appear minor, such as database structures, default parameters, or the order of options presented, can have significant effects on research outcomes (Borra and Rieder, 2014; Borra, 2023). Toolmakers are therefore inevitably in a normative position. Some respond with "prescriptive gestures" (Rieder et al., 2024), openly asserting a methodological direction. Others respond by maximizing options, as in scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011). Neither approach eliminates the theoretical load. It only changes how visible it is.

The chapter closes by invoking William James (1907): "theories thus become instruments, not answers to enigmas, in which we can rest." Infrastructured theory works as a means to make sense of the world, not as a definitive explanation, but as a vector of momentary insight, precisely because it is not a stable textual statement and cannot become a resting place for thought.

## Key Terms and Names

- DCSSH: digital and computational social sciences and humanities
- Infrastructured theory: theory embedded materially in a tool rather than expressed in text
- Tool criticism: critical examination of the epistemological commitments built into research tools
- Affordances: Gibson (1966), material properties of a tool that guide user behavior without explicit instruction
- Tool-user assemblage: the equipped researcher, whose research directions emerge from the interaction between researcher and tool
- Gephi / ForceAtlas2 / LinLog / edge-repulsion: network visualization tool and its layout algorithms
- Netvizz: Rieder's Facebook data extraction app (2008 to circa 2019)
- Voyant Tools: Rockwell and Sinclair's text analysis and visualization tool
- IRaMuTeQ: Ratinaud's text analysis tool dedicated to the ALCESTE method
- Lippmannian Device: Borra's search engine scraper grounded in Lippmann (1922)
- Graph-tool: Peixoto's opinionated network analysis library
- Gephisto: Jacomy and Munk's one-click Gephi tool (2024)
- DMI-TCAT: Borra and Rieder's Twitter capture and analysis toolkit (2014)
- DaCy, Minet: examples of tools without particular teaching affinity
- 4CAT: Peeters and Hagen's modular social media research toolkit (2022)
- Passeur: term used to describe a toolmaker who opens methods to beginners
