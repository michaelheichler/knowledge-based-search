## Overview

Chapter 19, by Nicholas Proferes, examines the privacy challenges that arise when social science and humanities (SSH) researchers use computational and digital methods. The chapter argues that computational SSH creates new vectors for privacy violations that existing institutional oversight structures are not fully equipped to handle. It gives researchers a theoretical framework and a set of practical strategies for meeting those challenges across the full research lifecycle.

## Defining Privacy

The chapter distinguishes three traditional conceptions of privacy: physical (freedom from intrusion into one's space), decisional (freedom to make choices without interference), and informational (control over personal data). Treating privacy as a fixed property of a space, decision, or data type is insufficient.

The central theoretical framework comes from Helen Nissenbaum's 2004 concept of contextual integrity. Privacy is not a binary attribute of information. It emerges from the situation as a whole. Contextual integrity is maintained when information flows respect the norms of the context in which information was originally shared. Those norms are defined relative to five parameters: the data subject (who the information is about), the sender (who may differ from the subject), the receiver, the type of information, and the transmission principles of the context (consent, coercion, sale, legal compulsion, surveillance, and so on). Norms are shaped by history, cultural influences, laws, and conventions, and they change over time, including in response to new technologies.

The chapter illustrates the framework with a doctor sharing a patient's diagnosis. Sharing it with a specialist treating that patient respects contextual norms. Sharing it with an advertising company does not, because the receiver and transmission principles fall outside what participants would expect.

For computational SSH researchers, the practical implication is that the question "is this information private or not?" is too narrow. They must ask whether their research's information flows respect the norms governing the original context.

## Ethical Principles and Their History

The chapter traces research ethics to a history of abuses. Nazi physicians conducted medical experiments on concentration camp prisoners during World War II. The US Public Health Service ran the Tuskegee Syphilis Study from 1932 to 1972, withholding treatment from Black men with syphilis to observe disease progression. Stanley Milgram's 1960s obedience experiments deceived subjects and filmed them without consent. Laud Humphreys (1975) covertly studied anonymous sex in public spaces, secretly recording participants' license plates and later visiting their homes under false pretenses. The Stanford Prison Experiment denied withdrawal to participants who showed severe emotional trauma.

These abuses produced regulatory responses. The Nuremberg Code (1949) and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) established foundational global standards. In the United States, the National Research Act of 1974 created the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research, which produced the Belmont Report (1979). The Belmont Report identifies three guiding values.

Respect for persons requires treating individuals as autonomous agents, obtaining informed consent, and allowing withdrawal. Beneficence requires weighing risks against benefits so that risks to subjects are minimized. Justice requires fair and equitable selection of subjects, freedom from exploitation, and equitable distribution of benefits.

Each value has a privacy dimension. Informed consent is an operationalization of both respect for persons and decisional privacy. Beneficence allows privacy risks to be weighed against research value. Justice requires considering whether privacy risks fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations.

## Institutional Review Boards and Their Limits

In the United States, institutional review boards (IRBs) enforce compliance with the Federal Common Rule. The Common Rule defines "research" as systematic investigation intended to contribute to generalizable knowledge. A "human subject" is a living individual about whom identifiable private information is obtained or studied. "Private information" is information produced in a context where the subject could reasonably expect no observation or recording, or information shared for specific purposes and not expected to be made public.

Computational SSH projects frequently fall into a gray area. Many IRBs treat publicly available social media data as exempt from ongoing review and waive informed consent requirements for public posts on platforms like X (formerly Twitter). Nebeker et al. (2017) and Vitak et al. (2016, 2017) document that inconsistent technical experience among IRB members produces inconsistent reviews, and that differing international regulatory regimes add further variation. IRB exemption does not eliminate privacy concerns.

Globally, bodies such as the Canadian Tri-Council, the Australian Research Council, the Research Council of Norway, the UK's NHS National Research Ethics Service, and the European Commission have developed their own frameworks. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679, adopted 27 April 2016) governs the acquisition, holding, and use of personal data, with direct implications for research.

## Privacy Challenges Across the Research Lifecycle

### Data Collection

Users' mental models about information flow often differ sharply from researchers' assumptions. Fiesler and Proferes (2018) found that roughly 60 percent of Twitter users were unaware researchers might use their tweets for scientific study, and almost 40 percent believed researchers were forbidden from doing so without permission. Volume and temporal scope amplify the concern: computational tools allow a researcher to collect every tweet a person has ever posted, including embedded media and metadata. Fiesler and Proferes (2018) found users were significantly more uncomfortable with the use of their entire digital history than with a single tweet. Webb et al. (2017) note that whether a subject is a public figure (celebrity, politician) is a relevant contextual variable.

Privacy concerns can scale from individuals to whole communities. A researcher collecting all tweets around a particular hashtag studies the group as a whole, yet IRBs typically assess risk only at the individual level.

### Practices of Consent

Obtaining prior informed consent is practically impossible at the scale of most social media datasets. Nevertheless, Fiesler and Proferes (2018) found that 66 percent of Twitter users would be uncomfortable if not told a researcher was using their data.

Several alternatives have been developed. Zong and Matias created "Bartleby", a digital system designed to debrief participants after data collection and elicit their views on studies that involved them (published 2018, 2022). Rather than front-loaded consent, Bartleby emphasizes post-collection notice, though engagement rates were low relative to the scale of users contacted.

The Documenting the Now project developed tools that allow researchers to download sets of Tweet IDs rather than full tweet text and metadata. When the researcher later "rehydrates" the dataset, deleted tweets are not populated. Deleting a tweet can be understood as withdrawing consent for that content to circulate, so this mechanism lets researchers honor users' autonomy retrospectively.

Connecting with community gatekeepers, such as group moderators, is another approach. Eschler (2015) documents that members of online cancer groups studied without explicit consent felt taken advantage of. Small online communities may function with the psychological character of private spaces even when technically public.

For cases where reidentification is possible and the individual is not a public figure, seeking informed consent specifically for verbatim quotation is recommended. Fiesler and Proferes (2018) found more than half of Twitter users would want to be asked before a tweet appeared verbatim in a publication.

### Data Analysis

Analysis can surface information that individuals never explicitly disclosed. Looking across a user's full posting history may reveal sleeping patterns, work habits, group memberships, or health conditions the individual never stated. Researchers may also categorize individuals into groups (for example, inferring emotional states from sentiment analysis) in ways subjects did not anticipate.

The chapter cites the controversy over Wang and Kosinski's (2017) machine-learning system, which used publicly available dating profile photos to predict sexual orientation. Privacy organizations criticized it for lack of representational diversity, racial bias, and reducing sexuality to a binary. Deriving sensitive attributes through public means can violate contextual integrity regardless of technical accuracy.

A further concern involves indirect data subjects, such as children discussed by parents on social media (Ammari et al., 2015, 2018). These individuals appear in data without any ability to be notified or to opt out.

Synthetic data, defined as data values mirroring the statistical characteristics of original data, is one mitigation. It is gaining traction in medical research and reduces (without eliminating) reidentification risk. Large language models and deep learning techniques are being explored for similar purposes in computational SSH contexts (Liu et al., 2022).

### Data Reporting and Sharing

Anonymization is the primary tool for protecting subjects during publication. Techniques include omitting usernames, obscuring direct quotes so they are not findable through search engines (fabrication tools are discussed by Reagle 2022 and Reagle and Gaur 2022), and reporting in aggregate. Ayers et al. (2018) recommend that authors convey aggregate findings without revealing identities, that editors refuse to publish reports that do so, and that IRBs attend to these issues when reviewing social media studies.

Anonymization is imperfect. A 2014 release of anonymized New York City taxi data covering approximately 173 million trips was inadequately anonymized: a graduate student cross-referenced it with Google Images to identify celebrities' pickup and drop-off locations and tip amounts. Machine learning techniques can further undermine anonymization (Sun et al., 2020).

Publicly naming small or stigmatized online communities, even without identifying specific individuals, carries its own risks. Communities organized around health, mental health, trauma, drug use, sexuality, or minority political or religious views depend on norms of trust. Identifying their location opens them to outside scrutiny and can damage the environment that makes honest participation possible (Dym and Fiesler, 2020).

The Documenting the Now project illustrates the stakes of data-sharing decisions. The project assembled a collection of tweets from Ferguson, Missouri, around the police shooting of Michael Brown Jr. When a data mining company requested the collection and the project team discovered its clients included the Department of Defense and the intelligence community, they declined to share it.

Data use agreements are one mechanism for managing downstream use. The CLEF eRisk workshops share datasets of social media content on topics such as depression, pathological gambling, and eating disorders, requiring signatories to agree not to reshare the data, not to identify or cross-reference individuals in the dataset, not to contact those individuals, and not to make commercial use of the data.

## Conclusion and Recommendations

The chapter closes with recommendations for researchers who want to go beyond minimum institutional compliance.

Reflexive practice means proactively identifying privacy risks at every stage, not just during IRB review. Discussing ethics in research group meetings, departmental settings, and at conferences helps surface blind spots. Documenting ethical decisions as part of published methodology normalizes protective practices and exposes subsequent researchers to those approaches.

Professional societies increasingly incorporate ethics criteria into publication and conference review processes. Organizations including the Society for American Archivists, the British Sociological Association, the Social Research Association, the Social Policy Association, and the American Sociological Association have each issued ethical guidance documents.

The Association of Internet Researchers (AoIR) offers an ethics guide (Franzke et al., 2020) written for researchers, students, IRB members, and technical developers facing ethical concerns in digital research. This guide draws on interdisciplinary experience and is oriented toward internet research rather than biomedical norms. While no guide can guarantee contextual integrity is never violated, such resources help sensitize researchers to the forms of privacy risk at stake in their particular contexts.
