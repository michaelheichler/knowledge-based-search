## Overview

Chapter 11, by Mascha Kurpicz-Briki, argues that bias in machine learning (ML) is not a single, containable problem but a multidimensional one shaped by three intersecting axes: the type of bias (human, statistical, systemic), the group or property it targets (gender, origin, age, and their intersections), and the stage of the ML pipeline where it appears (data collection, annotation, model training, deployment). The chapter connects the older psychological and statistical meanings of the word "bias" to contemporary discussions of algorithmic bias, traces how bias enters ML systems in practice, surveys methods for detecting it in word embeddings, and concludes by advocating augmented intelligence as a partial remedy.

## Definitions and Forms of Bias

The chapter opens with the American Psychological Association dictionary definition of bias, which covers four senses: (1) partiality or predisposition, (2) any systematic tendency, (3) systematic error in sampling or data analysis, and (4) deviation of a measured value from its true value. The chapter argues that bias in AI draws on all four senses simultaneously, which is part of what makes the problem hard to frame.

Human bias can be explicit (conscious and declining in social acceptability) or implicit (unconscious and persistent). The standard instrument for measuring implicit human bias is the Implicit Association Test (IAT), developed by Greenwald et al. (1998), which records reaction-time differences when subjects pair concepts. Shorter reaction times indicate perceived similarity. IAT studies have shown that names associated with European Americans are more readily linked to pleasant terms than names associated with African Americans (Caliskan et al., 2017).

Statistical or learning bias has an older, more technical meaning. Mitchell (1980) defined it as "any basis for choosing one generalization over another, other than strict consistency with the observed training instances." This inductive bias, as Hellstrom et al. (2020) call it, is actually necessary for ML to generalize beyond the training data at all. A related early concern was the minority class problem: if 90 of 100 training examples belong to class A and only 10 to class B, a classifier can achieve 90 percent accuracy while failing entirely on class B (Mac Namee et al., 2002). Fraud detection and medical diagnosis are cited as domains where this matters most.

Systemic or pre-existing bias refers to historical, social, and institutional biases baked into training data and design choices by default (Hall et al., 2023; Schwartz et al., 2022). Hellstrom et al. (2020) call this "bias of the world," meaning data that was sampled correctly but contains unwanted social properties.

Algorithmic bias is the umbrella term now common in public discourse, defined as "the deviation of the algorithmic results from specific social expectations, based on epistemic or normative reasons" (Mittelstadt et al., 2016). Hellstrom et al. (2020) add the term model bias for the bias that accumulates in a final trained model from learning bias, world bias, and data generation bias combined.

The chapter notes that the line between human bias and statistical bias has blurred. Statistical algorithms are tools built by humans "who may bring their own agenda and context to the task" (Cowgill et al., 2020), so the two cannot be cleanly separated.

## Machine Learning Types and How Bias Enters Each

The chapter distinguishes narrow (weak) AI, which handles specific tasks, from general (strong) AI, which would equal human capabilities broadly. What actually exists is narrow AI (Broussard, 2019), and the division of labor between humans and machines is itself a design choice that affects how bias manifests.

Traditional algorithms follow fixed rules. Expert systems from the 1970s onward use explicit rule bases, which makes bias easier to detect and trace. Even so, traditional software can produce unfair outcomes through implementation errors or by encoding biased value judgments into numeric scores. An example cited is a US bank whose software incorrectly rejected home loan modifications, causing some borrowers to lose their homes (Zweig, 2022).

ML algorithms replace fixed rules with data-driven learning from training examples.

Supervised learning uses labeled data. A classifier trained on labeled photos will perform poorly on types of examples absent from the training set. When humans label data, they can introduce their own prejudices, for instance by rating utterances from unfamiliar ethnic groups differently (Hovy and Prabhumoye, 2021).

Unsupervised learning discovers structure without labels. Hidden clusters in data can still reflect biased distributions present in the source.

Reinforcement learning trains a model through reward signals. If the reward mechanism inadvertently reinforces biased behavior, that behavior is amplified over time.

Pre-trained models (such as those underlying ChatGPT or Midjourney) are trained on massive internet-sourced corpora and then fine-tuned on task-specific data through a process called transfer learning (Hagiwara, 2022). Because the pre-training corpus is too large for manual review, it routinely encodes offensive content, historical viewpoints, and societal stereotypes. Fine-tuning introduces a second dataset, so there are two potential sources of bias. Because pre-trained models are distributed off-the-shelf for reuse across applications, any bias they contain propagates widely.

Word embeddings are vector representations of words that allow a computer to perform mathematical operations on language. Procedures for training them include word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013a, 2013b), GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014), and fasttext (Grave et al., 2018), trained on corpora such as Wikipedia or the Google News Corpus. The vector arithmetic "Man is to King as Woman is to X" yields "Queen," which is benign. However, the same arithmetic yields "man is to computer programmer as woman is to homemaker" and "man is to doctor as woman is to nurse" (Bolukbasi et al., 2016), showing how societal stereotypes are encoded in the vector geometry.

## Bias as a Multidimensional Problem

### Sources in Data Collection

Hellstrom et al. (2020) identify five bias sources at the data generation stage:

1. Specification bias: choices about what counts as input and output.
2. Measurement bias (observational or information bias): errors in observing the world.
3. Sampling bias (population bias): under- or over-representation of groups.
4. Annotator bias: prejudices introduced by human labelers.
5. Inherited bias: bias passed along when one ML tool generates inputs for another.

A widely cited example of sampling bias is the Amazon recruiting tool (Dastin, 2022). The system was trained on past job applications, which skewed male because the tech field is male-dominated. The resulting model penalized female applicants. Sensitive features (or protected attributes) such as gender can be implicit rather than explicit: fields like military service or membership in certain associations can serve as proxies.

Wikipedia is discussed as a case study in training-data bias. A gender contribution gap (few women editing) has produced a gender content gap (less content about women). Wagner et al. (2015) found that Wikipedia articles about women link to men more often than the reverse, and discuss family and romantic relationships far more frequently than articles about men do. These asymmetries can affect search and recommendation algorithms built from Wikipedia data.

### Bias in Word Embeddings and the WEAT

The Word Embedding Association Test (WEAT), developed by Caliskan et al. (2017), adapts the IAT for word vectors. Where IAT uses reaction time, WEAT uses vector-space distance. Words whose vectors are close are treated as strongly associated. The test uses two target word groups and two attribute word groups, following the same structure as the IAT.

Key replicated findings: flowers associate more strongly with pleasant words and insects with unpleasant ones (a low-stakes finding). European-American names associate more strongly with pleasant expressions than African-American names do. Women's names associate more with family words and less with career words, while men's names show the reverse pattern (Nosek et al., 2002; Caliskan et al., 2017).

Cross-linguistic studies show that bias is not confined to English. Kurpicz-Briki (2020) applied WEAT to German and French word embeddings, substituting names typical of those language regions and comparing them to names of other origins. Measurable bias in association with positive and negative expressions appeared analogously to the English results. Kurpicz-Briki and Leoni (2021) extended this to names from major migration groups and found bias in word groups related to labor market participation and social integration. A notable culture-specific finding: academic subjects with the highest and lowest proportions of women in Switzerland (based on Dubach et al., 2017) mapped predictably onto female and male word associations in the embeddings. The chapter concludes that bias in word embeddings is culturally variable and must be studied separately for each language.

Because pre-trained word embeddings are publicly available and reused across applications, embedded stereotypes propagate into many downstream systems. At the same time, the chapter notes a dual use: bias in word embeddings can serve as a research tool to study historical and contemporary cultural stereotypes (Kozlowski et al., 2019), provided the training data is chosen deliberately.

### Bias Mitigation

Bias mitigation requires action at multiple levels. On the process side, key levers include selecting inclusive and representative training data, continuously updating datasets to counteract stereotypes, ensuring transparency about training datasets (Gebru et al., 2021 propose "datasheets for datasets"), and involving diverse stakeholders throughout design and maintenance (Hall et al., 2023). An example of stakeholder participation is participatory data design (Jensen et al., 2021), in which STS researchers and domain stakeholders co-create data in intensive workshops.

For word embeddings, mathematical debiasing methods have been proposed (Bolukbasi et al., 2016), but Gonen and Goldberg (2019) argue these methods conceal rather than remove systematic bias. Different languages also require different methods because of differing linguistic properties such as grammatical gender.

## Discussion and Conclusions

The chapter identifies four reasons bias in ML is structurally hard to eliminate.

First, multiple bias types (systemic, statistical, human) interact and are not cleanly separable.

Second, bias can target many group properties, and effects are intersectional. Recent language models show more bias against people belonging to multiple marginalized groups than additive combination of single-group biases would predict (Bender et al., 2021). An intersectional audit must watch for "token-clusters," for example a dataset that includes many women and many people of color but few Black women (Soraa, 2023).

Third, bias can enter at any stage of the ML pipeline, from training data and annotation through model training to deployment choices.

Fourth, bias detection depends on the technology type. Structured data may expose sensitive attributes directly, while text and video data hide them, and the features a neural model finds relevant may not be interpretable by engineers.

The chapter presents these dimensions in Figure 11.2: the axes are the type and form of bias, the technology used, and the ML pipeline component affected, with cultural context and language as additional modulating factors. Hellstrom et al. (2020) are cited in support of this multi-dimensional framing.

The closing argument is about augmented intelligence. Rather than aiming for general AI that replaces human judgment, the chapter advocates AI that supports humans in work, research, and daily life through applications with a specific and limited scope. Using machines where they excel (fast analysis of large document sets) and humans where they excel (critical reflection on decisions), augmented intelligence is presented as better suited to handling high-stakes, bias-prone decisions than fully automated systems.

For researchers in computational social science and humanities, the chapter recommends critically examining potential bias at every stage: research question formulation, experimental setup, system design, use-case selection, data collection, model training, and validation. It cites Hellstrom et al. (2020) on the need to include bias measures alongside traditional performance metrics when designing ML systems, aiming for an optimal system that is not necessarily statistically unbiased.
