## Overview

Chapter 17, written by Michele Mauri, reframes visualization not as a static artifact but as a process unfolding across three stages: ideation, development, and usage. The central move, borrowed from Science and Technology Studies (STS), is to treat representation as a verb rather than a noun (Hoeppe, 2015). The chapter asks how digital technology has changed each stage of that process, and what researchers in Digital and Computational Social Sciences and Humanities (DCSSH) need to understand to avoid uncritical enthusiasm about computational tools.

## Key Terms

The chapter establishes precise vocabulary before proceeding.

- **Visual representation**: any visual artifact created to convey knowledge.
- **Visualization**: the subset of visual representations built from data and information, including plots, maps, and charts.
- **Visualization design**: the process of creating such artifacts. It always involves authorial choices, regardless of whether the creator has formal design training.
- **Phenomena**: the subject of the visualization process.

## Direct vs. Indirect Documents

Drawing on Italian designer Giovanni Anceschi (1992), the chapter divides visual outputs into two categories.

**Direct documents** are produced through physical or chemical processes that make otherwise invisible phenomena visible. Examples include radiography (X-rays passing through film or a digital detector), photography, infrared thermography, and the analog oscilloscope (where an electron gun, deflection plate, and phosphor-coated screen work together to trace electrical signals). These outputs are artifacts shaped by the technology that generated them, not unmediated copies of reality (Lynch, 1988; Drucker, 2014).

**Indirect documents** first translate observed phenomena into organized measurements (data) and then generate visual artifacts from those measurements. Bar charts, scatter plots, and network diagrams are typical results. Before the computational turn, this translation required hand drawing or custom-built mechanical machinery.

The computational turn has greatly increased the convenience and reach of indirect documents. Digital sensors of all kinds (cameras, microphones, web APIs, IoT devices) output the same form of data, which can then be represented in many ways. Two additional forces have pushed this shift: the digitization of existing materials such as books and historical photographs (enabling approaches like Franco Moretti's distant reading, 2000), and the growth of real-time data sources including social media and the web.

Once phenomena are mediated as data, data mediation becomes unavoidable in any further representation, including tables, which already encode information through the visual variables of horizontal and vertical position.

## Three Consequences of the Computational Turn

### 1. Digital Mediation

Because indirect documents always pass through a data layer, visualization designers must translate categories, quantities, connections, and orders into visual elements. The chapter presents two frameworks that guide this translation.

**Gestalt grouping principles** (originally from Max Wertheimer, popularized in graphic design by Arnheim, 1974) describe how human perception organizes visual fields. The five principles most cited in visualization literature are:

- Proximity: nearby items appear to belong to the same group.
- Similarity: items sharing color, shape, size, or texture are read as grouped.
- Continuity: overlapping items are perceived as one continuous path.
- Common region: items within a shared boundary are grouped.
- Connectedness: touching or linked items are grouped.

The underlying insight is that "the whole is something else than the sum of its parts" (Koffka, 1935, p. 176). Gestalt principles allow a viewer to perceive both individual data points and global patterns at the same time.

**Visual variables**, formalized by Jacques Bertin in "Semiologie Graphique" (1983), provide a systematic way to encode data. Every mark in a visualization necessarily occupies a position in space. Beyond position, a mark's size, shape, color tint, color hue, texture, and rotation can each carry information. The framework links each variable to three perceptual functions: distinguishing groups, indicating order, and expressing quantity. Color hue works well for groups but poorly for order. Size works well for quantity. Roth (2017) later extended the framework to 12 variables.

These theoretical frameworks became influential through software libraries. Protovis (Bostock and Heer, 2009) translated Bertin's approach directly into code, exposing functions to create marks and assign visual attributes. Its successor, D3.js ("Data-Driven Documents," Bostock, Ogievetsky, and Heer, 2011), created a direct binding between data dimensions and standard HTML5 elements, and became the dominant library for web-based visualization. Tools built on the same binding concept include Lyra (Zong et al., 2021), Voyager (Wongsuphasawat et al., 2016), and RAWGraphs (Mauri et al., 2017).

The chapter warns that tight coupling between data and visual elements can make the translation feel transparent, as if no interpretive work is involved. In fact, the visual encoding always adds or alters information. Stretching an axis changes perception of a trend. Screen or print resolution merges nearby elements. Data almost always undergoes aggregation, transformation, and categorization before it reaches the visualization stage. Lynch's parking-lot analogy captures this: the work of flattening and framing raw material produces something that is not the original terrain.

### 2. Digital Process

Visualization does not produce a single artifact. An analysis generates many interim outputs (datasets, intermediate charts, exploratory plots) that involve multiple authors across the production cycle. The chapter distinguishes two types of visualization by purpose.

**Exploratory visualizations** are made for the researcher, used to test or reject hypotheses, and typically discarded once they have served their moment. They are inspired by the concept of exploratory data analysis (Jacomy, 2019).

**Explanatory visualizations** are made for an audience beyond the research team. Their goal is to persuade, challenge, or confirm prior knowledge.

Both types exist within a network of connected artifacts, and the credibility of explanatory outputs depends on making that network visible. The chapter uses EMAPS (Electronic Maps to Assist Public Science), a European project that mapped debates around aging and climate change adaptation using digital traces, as its main example (Venturini et al., 2014). EMAPS divided its process into an exploratory phase (notes and tests for researchers) and a communication phase (structured outputs for wider audiences). Its resulting web platform, Climaps (www.climaps.eu), made the network of datasets, analytical choices, and outputs navigable via hyperlinks.

The web's hyperlink structure is the main technical means for tracing process networks. The chapter cautions that openness must be designed deliberately and handled carefully, particularly when minority or disadvantaged groups are involved (Burns et al., 2021). Transparency that invites further exploration is a design goal, not an automatic byproduct of using digital tools. Researchers risk locking readers into a single visualization rather than leaving results open.

### 3. Digital Support

"Support" refers to the physical object through which a reader encounters a visualization: a poster, book, monitor, or smartphone. Bertin (1983) identified support constraints explicitly before presenting his visual variables framework. The artifact must be printable on white paper, of a standard size visible at a glance, at normal reading distance, and under constant lighting.

Most contemporary visualizations are not read under those conditions. Monitors and smartphones are the common support, and they introduce three sources of complexity not present with print.

**Variable display environments.** The designer has no control over the reader's screen size, resolution, or color rendering. The spread of tablets and mobile phones has multiplied this variability. Many visualizations are "vernacular" (Snyder, 2017), made by people without visualization training who follow the defaults of whatever software they use.

**Interaction design pitfalls.** Adding interactivity to a digital visualization appears natural but requires deliberate planning. Rollovers that work on desktop computers do not function on touchscreens. Hiding important content in tooltips risks that content never being seen. As Archie Tse, Graphics Director of the New York Times, stated in 2016: "If you make a tooltip or a rollover, assume no one will ever see it." Interaction design should be developed alongside the visual model rather than added as a final step (Brüggemann et al., 2020).

**Short lifespan of interactive artifacts.** Interactive web displays age faster than articles or books. Building digital tools that outlast their immediate context requires deliberate design choices.

Three project cases illustrate how working within support constraints improves results.

"A Field Guide to Fake News" (Bounegru et al., 2018) handled a large data-sprint with many researchers by defining the output format in advance: a printed book where each spread matched the size of a standard 15-inch monitor. This constraint structured the production process and directed attention toward visualizations that could stand alone without dynamic elements.

The book "Controversy Mapping: A Field Guide" (Venturini and Munk, 2022) required converting over 90 visualizations from various original media into black-and-white printed format. Communication designer Federica Bardelli led this redesign across three axes: determining minimum legible sizes for shapes and text, replacing all color encodings with non-color alternatives, and creating a shared visual language across diagrams that had originally been made independently.

The Climaps platform resolved the complexity of digital support by imposing explicit constraints: all visualizations fit within a 1200x800-pixel space and used only one interaction type (click or tap). This forced researchers to ask not "how can I fit this analysis into the platform?" but "is this analysis suitable for this platform?"

## Conclusions

Examining visualization as a process reveals three concrete consequences of the computational turn. First, digital data is an unavoidable mediator between observation and visual output, separating sensing from display and requiring deliberate design of the translation. Second, visualization outputs are part of networked processes involving multiple authors. Digital tools can expose these networks and support accountability, but openness must be deliberately built in. Third, the digital display medium has its own materiality and constraints. Treating the monitor as a limitless, immaterial surface leads to avoidable design errors. All three aspects are directly relevant to DCSSH research practice.
